# Summary

Steve follows up on the Apple silicon backdoor from the previous episode and concludes that the most consistent explanation is that Apple deliberately built a last-resort, hash-protected way into its own devices that was never meant to be shared, and that a secret leaked from inside; he notes Apple has lost plausible deniability toward law enforcement and may still hold access. News covers Russian use of hijacked Ukrainian security cameras to adjust missile strikes and the destructive Kyivstar telecom attack attributed to Sandworm, with the warning that other countries are similarly exposed. 23andMe faces about 30 lawsuits after the credential-stuffing breach that exposed 6.9 million users, 2023 crypto theft totalled USD 1.8 billion (down from 3.7 billion) with North Korea linked to USD 600 million, and Mandiant's X account was hijacked for a crypto scam. The Merck NotPetya insurance case settled before the New Jersey Supreme Court could rule on the "warlike action" exclusion. LastPass is forcing 12-character master passwords and MFA re-enrolment, Windows 10 still holds two-thirds of desktops, and Google settled the Incognito-mode tracking class action. Listener items cover Edge's Startup Boost, why SpinRite 6.1 cannot run in a VM, how to untrust root certificates, and credit freezes. The finale shares SpinRite findings that spinning drives' ends run at half the speed of their starts.

# Key points

- Steve's theory: Apple designed and kept a closed emergency access path in A12 to A16 silicon (the GPU-adjacent registers protected by a secret hash); someone with knowledge leaked it. Apple blocked the ranges in software but could re-enable them, and the A17 may still contain it. The claim that the lookup table was an error-correction table is wrong; it is a simple hash.
- Reply to ROP-mitigation question: address randomization raises difficulty, but the malware spent much of its bulk scanning memory to locate the code pieces it needed.
- Ukraine: over 10,000 security cameras taken down since the invasion; Russian hackers used two hijacked cameras to improve strikes on Kyiv. Kyivstar (24 million users) suffered a wiper attack from 12 December 2023; the SBU says attackers were inside since at least May 2023, probably Sandworm; ATMs and air-raid sirens were affected; the SBU says it thwarted over 4,500 major attacks in 2023.
- 23andMe: about 14,000 accounts compromised via credential stuffing led to 6.9 million exposed profiles, an average of roughly 493 per account, which Steve finds poor architecture; the company also tried to push mandatory arbitration into its terms.
- Crypto thefts in 2023: USD 1.8 billion across 751 incidents (CertiK); top 10 incidents were USD 1.1 billion; private-key compromises cost over USD 880 million. Incidents: CoinsPaid (USD 7.5 million, second hack), Gamma (6.1 million), Radiant Capital flash-loan attack (4.5 million).
- Mandiant's X account was hijacked to promote a crypto scam, among other gold-badge account takeovers (the SEC's X account was hit the same day).
- Merck NotPetya case: Merck claimed USD 1.4 billion in damage; insurers invoked the "hostile/warlike action" exclusion; an appeals court had awarded about half; the parties settled hours before New Jersey Supreme Court arguments, leaving no precedent. Steve advises customers to demand explicit cyber-war wording.
- LastPass account changes (12-character minimum, MFA re-enrolment) read as late damage control, possibly implying TOTP seeds were also at risk. Steve argues sites should enforce stronger passwords or limit sensitive actions for weak ones.
- Windows 11 climbed from about 16 to 26 percent of desktops in 2023 while Windows 10 kept two-thirds.
- Google's USD 5 billion Incognito-mode class action reached a preliminary settlement before trial.
- Edge keeps running after closing when Startup Boost or background apps are enabled; disable them or close via the menu.
- SpinRite 6.1 uses direct hardware access and a flat real mode trick, so it will not run properly in virtual machines. Untrusting a certificate is reversible by dragging it to the Untrusted Certificates store in the Windows snap-in.
- Credit freezes (not paid "locks") are free by federal law; Investopedia link recommended; some bureaus allow automatic re-freeze after a set period.
- SpinRite time estimation: on spinning drives the end runs at about half the speed of the start; add about 30 percent to the one-minute estimate; the first 60 percent of the drive takes half the total time. SSDs and SMR drives behave differently.
