# Summary

So much happened that the planned Q&A became a pure news episode. Microsoft warned of targeted attacks using a malformed TIFF image against Office 2003 and 2007 (and Office 2010 on XP and Server 2003), bypassing DEP and ASLR; the interim fix disables the TIFF codec. The main story is the Adobe breach, now counted at about 130 million records: passwords were not salted hashes but encrypted with what looks like 3DES in ECB mode, so identical passwords gave identical ciphertext, plaintext hints were stored beside them, and "123456" topped the list with nearly two million uses. Steve then covers Dragos Ruiu's "badBIOS" claims of malware jumping across air-gapped machines by ultrasound, which Tavis Ormandy and a BIOS veteran judged unsupported. CryptoLocker gained a Tor-based decryption service with a higher price after the 72-hour deadline, while Sandboxie or a virtual machine contain it; OpenDNS Umbrella can block its generated domain lookups. Other items: LastPass 3.0, BitTorrent Sync's closed protocol, the TrueCrypt audit funding goal being exceeded, Google engineers' angry reaction to the NSA's cable tap, Chrome Canary re-prompting for the OS password, the Knock phone-unlock app, and doubts about Lavabit's Dark Mail fundraiser.

# Key points

- TIFF zero-day in Office 2003/2007 (and 2010 on XP/2003): merely previewing an email with the image can lead to takeover; exploit defeats DEP and ASLR; Microsoft's stopgap is a registry change disabling the TIFF codec, with a patch expected.
- Adobe breach: initially described as 3 million, then 38 million, finally about 130 million records with email addresses, plaintext password hints and encrypted passwords; payment-card data was also described as "encrypted", raising the worry of the same weak scheme.
- Why it is bad: block-cipher ECB mode with no salt or chaining means same password gives same ciphertext, so frequency analysis and hint cross-referencing reveal passwords; Steve contrasts this with salted hashing, and notes Adobe could return a forgotten password, proving it was reversible.
- Password list lessons: "123456" first, "1234" accepted (no minimum length), "monkey" 26th, "trustnoone" 70th; the xkcd strip showed the leak as a crossword puzzle. Strong random passwords from a manager or generator are unaffected.
- LastPass built an email-lookup page for the leak; Leo notes it emailed third parties' addresses, producing spam.
- badBIOS: Ruiu said for three years malware infected firmware across operating systems and communicated via speakers and microphones. Ormandy found nothing suspicious in his logs and fonts, and an experienced BIOS developer argued BIOS code is not portable between boards and would be easy to spot; Steve is sceptical although Ruiu is respected.
- CryptoLocker: some versions delete volume shadow copies; a Tor hidden service lets victims pay 10 bitcoin (about 2,600 dollars at the time) after 72 hours, or 2 bitcoin within the window, after submitting an encrypted file.
- OpenDNS Umbrella (a paid business service) detects the malware's flood of long generated domain names and blocks key retrieval; Leo points out the author could generate the key locally to evade this.
- Sandboxie around mail client and browser, or a VirtualBox VM without access to drive letters, neutralises CryptoLocker; CryptoPrevent (now version 4) is only a soft group-policy fix with false-positive risk.
- LastPass 3.0 brings a redesigned interface and Family Folders; Steve praises the openness of its design versus BitTorrent Sync (a million users, API opened, protocol still closed) and Heml.is (pretty but secret crypto).
- TrueCrypt audit crowd-funding passed its 25,000 dollar goal; plan is reproducible builds and a line-by-line review, led by cryptographer Matthew Green.
- Google engineers reacted bitterly to the NSA/GCHQ tap of its leased fibre between data centres; traffic is now encrypted; encrypting at Google's scale needs large key-management infrastructure.
- Chrome Canary for OS X re-prompts for the OS password before showing saved passwords, as Safari does.
- Knock iOS app unlocks a Mac by double-tapping the phone over Bluetooth LE using an RSA-encrypted password; Steve judges the design sound.
- Lavabit's Dark Mail Kickstarter (about 196,000 dollar goal): Moxie Marlinspike criticised Lavabit's design, which decrypted stored mail with the user's password at login; Steve says email's store-and-forward nature resists encryption.

# Other segments

- Leo apologises for mass Twitter invitations caused by an Android sign-up default.
- Steve and Leo review the film "Ender's Game"; Steve's middle name Maury and a Wikipedia edit joke; SQRL now has a Wikipedia page.
- Chrome's visible-password feature was by design, a point Steve defended in August.
