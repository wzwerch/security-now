# Summary

Recorded with Mikah Sargent substituting for Leo Laporte. Steve first covers Microsoft's statement that the ability to uninstall the Recall feature, visible in a Windows 11 24H2 preview build, is a bug and will be removed; Recall is to be previewed with Insiders in October as opt-in with an encrypted database and Windows Hello authentication, and Steve hints at a GRC freeware tool to neuter it. Next is NinjaLab's side-channel attack on the Infineon cryptographic library inside YubiKey 5 series keys (firmware before 5.7.11), allowing ECDSA key extraction after hours of access with about $11,000 of equipment; Steve judges it practically irrelevant outside nation-state scenarios. A long listener feedback section follows: WhatsApp versus Telegram encryption, a fourth credit bureau (Innovis) to freeze, noise-based dog deterrents, a Unix 2038 clock, new Bobiverse and Peter F. Hamilton books, and SpinRite's live data display. The main topic is RAMBO, Mordechai Guri's research at Ben-Gurion University showing that malware can modulate RAM bus activity to emit radio signals carrying data out of an air-gapped computer, and Steve explains Manchester self-clocking encoding.

# Key points

- Recall: KB5041865 preview exposed Recall in "Windows features"; Microsoft says that listing is a bug; the EU Digital Markets Act might force an uninstall option in Europe as happened for Edge; Steve compares it with the Internet Explorer "cannot be removed" argument of the past.
- YubiKey 5 cloning (NinjaLab): vulnerability in the Extended Euclidean Algorithm used for ECDSA nonce inversion in Infineon's library, present for about 14 years across roughly 80 certification evaluations; fixed in new hardware because the code is in mask ROM and not field-updatable.
- Attack needs the target's login and password, physical possession of the key for hours, opening the casing, about $11,000 of lab gear, and resealing it unnoticed; OTP and non-FIDO functions are unaffected; Steve and Mikah conclude ordinary users need not replace their keys.
- Same flaw class as the 2021 Google Titan key cloning; FIDO keys remain among the strongest authenticators.
- Telegram vs WhatsApp: WhatsApp uses the Signal protocol and is always end-to-end encrypted, including groups; a listener argues Telegram suffices for non-sensitive chats; Steve counters that average users do not understand the difference.
- Credit freezes: Innovis is the fourth bureau worth freezing (online form, PIN by mail, no account); grc.sc/npd and grc.sc/credit were GRC's most-clicked shortcuts ever; new shortcut grc.sc/freeze; some of the small "secondary" bureaus on a list had broken links.
- Steve proposes the proper design: credit files frozen by default, with per-request access PINs.
- Dog deterrent: Steve's "Quiet Canine" experiments showed ultrasonic-style blasters only work at point-blank range; Mikah reports small-dog success with a bark-triggered sound device.
- Unix time 2038 hex clock (grc.sc/2038) illustrates the signed 32-bit rollover.
- SpinRite's Real Time Activities screen shows data flashing past; Steve plans a secure wiping tool "Beyond Recall" because used eBay drives retain previous owners' data.
- RAMBO: malware modulates memory-bus activity (using non-temporal store instructions) to generate radio emissions at memory clock harmonics, received with software-defined radio and a simple antenna; reported leakage up to 1,000 bits per second over about 7 metres; a 4096-bit RSA key in roughly 4 seconds.
- Encoding: simple on-off keying plus a preamble, and Manchester encoding for self-clocking (each bit has a mid-cell transition, so receivers stay synchronized); Manchester dates to 1948 and is still used in IR remotes, RFID and early Ethernet.
- Context: air-gap malware families such as Stuxnet, Agent.BTZ, Flame, ProjectSauron and APT31's USB tooling; countermeasures are TEMPEST-style shielding and zoning; infection must first occur via USB, insiders or supply-chain updates.
- Steve notes that strong cryptography concentrates value into a few key bits, so even a slow covert channel suffices to leak the secret that guards cloud data.
