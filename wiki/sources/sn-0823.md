# Summary

Steve reconstructs, from Kaspersky reports, how attackers in April 2021 chained a Chrome renderer exploit with two Windows kernel bugs (CVE-2021-31955 information disclosure and CVE-2021-31956 elevation of privilege, both patched in June). The episode's timeline shows attackers watching the Pwn2Own results and public Chromium commits and regression tests, creating a window of roughly eight days between a public fix and shipped patch. The June 2021 patch day was industry-wide: Intel fixed 73 flaws, Adobe 41 CVEs (21 critical) and Microsoft about 50 including six exploited zero-days. TikTok's updated privacy policy openly allows collecting faceprints and voiceprints after a $92M Illinois BIPA settlement; Apple's iOS 14.5 tracking prompt gets about 25% opt-in worldwide (14% in the US). Steve then details the ANOM/Trojan Shield sting, an errata about "Windows 10 is the last Windows", and a personal story of recovering a BitLocker-encrypted NVMe drive via a Thunderbolt enclosure. The main topic is ALPACA, research showing that TLS is not bound to the application protocol, so a man-in-the-middle can redirect an HTTPS connection to a mail or FTP server that shares a certificate; ALPN is the proper fix but only works if all servers require it, which Steve doubts will happen.

# Key points

- Chrome attack timeline: Kaspersky saw targeted attacks on April 14-15, 2021 using a Chrome RCE plus Windows kernel bugs (tuned for Windows 10 builds from 1809 through 20H2). A Pwn2Own type-confusion bug was fixed in Chrome 89.0.4389.128 (CVE-2021-21220), a related public exploit appeared on GitHub; a second V8 type bug committed April 12 still worked on the new Chrome 90 until a fix on April 20. Kaspersky suspects attackers derived their exploit from the public regression test.
- Lessons: attackers watch everything in public view; open-source security fixes can create an exposure window; browser vendors need rapid short-cycle patching; Steve praises Microsoft's move to Chromium.
- Chained exploits are the norm for layered defenses (renderer exploit, sandbox escape, privilege escalation), unlike credential-based breaches such as Colonial Pipeline.
- Intel patch day: 29 advisories and 73 vulnerabilities (UEFI/BIOS, Bluetooth, AMT, NUC, a weak random number generator in a security library); Intel says 95% were found by its own research or bug bounty. Steve urges NUC owners to check firmware.
- Adobe fixed 41 CVEs (Acrobat/Reader, Photoshop, Creative Cloud, RoboHelp, After Effects, Animate). Microsoft fixed 50-51 flaws including six exploited ones: an Enhanced Cryptographic Provider issue paired with an Acrobat bug, Defender RCE, Office MSGraph RCE, a DWM Core Library privilege escalation (CVSS 8.4), and the kernel information leak CVE-2021-31955.
- TikTok: $92M BIPA class-action settlement (compared to Facebook's $650M); new policy discloses collection of approximate and, with permission, precise location, image and audio analysis, and faceprints/voiceprints; only five US states (California, Illinois, New York, Texas, Washington) restrict biometric collection.
- iOS 14.5 App Tracking Transparency: Flurry measured about 25% global opt-in and about 14% in the US. Leo argues third-party tracking is fading but first-party tracking by large platforms stays.
- ANOM (FBI Operation Trojan Shield / AFP Operation Ironside): an informant-built encrypted phone, distributed from October 2018 after Phantom Secure was shut down, relayed all messages to the FBI (via a third country's court order to avoid US legal limits); 27 million messages, 12,000 devices, 300+ syndicates, 800+ arrests in 16 countries. Leo notes subsequent pushes for encryption backdoors in Australia.
- Errata: Microsoft never officially said Windows 10 is the "last version"; a developer evangelist (Jerry Nixon) said it in 2015 and it became lore. Windows 11 rumored to be a cosmetic refresh.
- Laptop recovery story: a Lenovo P51s with a BitLocker-encrypted 1TB Samsung NVMe failed with WHEA_UNCORRECTABLE_ERROR; the drive read cleanly elsewhere but would not work with that M.2 slot. The recovery key was not found in OneDrive or Microsoft accounts. A Thunderbolt 3 NVMe enclosure made the drive appear to the TPM as acceptable and it booted. Steve imaged it, removed BitLocker, and pointed the owner to Sync.com. Takeaway: make sure you have your BitLocker recovery key.
- Syncthing added encrypted folders for syncing to untrusted devices; Sync.com is end-to-end encrypted, versioned and HIPAA-capable but has no Linux client.
- ALPACA (Application Layer Protocol Confusion Attacks; USENIX Security 2021, Black Hat): TLS authenticates the connection but not the application protocol or port, so a man-in-the-middle can redirect HTTPS to an SMTP, IMAP, POP3 or FTP server that presents a compatible certificate (such as a wildcard) and can steal session cookies or inject script. Findings: 13 of 25 mail/FTP implementations exploitable in the lab, IE and legacy Edge sniff content and are vulnerable to all variants, about 1.4 million web servers share trusted certificates with other services, of which about 114,000-119,000 pair with an exploitable server.
- Mitigation: Application Layer Protocol Negotiation (ALPN) and SNI, but the fix needs both clients and all TLS-accepting services to require ALPN. Steve is pessimistic about that happening and notes his own shared certificate use.
