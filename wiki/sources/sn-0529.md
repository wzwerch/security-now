# Summary

The episode opens with an interview of Joe Siegrist, founder and CEO of LastPass, after the announcement that LogMeIn is acquiring the password manager. Siegrist says the team, office and security model stay unchanged, he keeps decision authority, and LogMeIn is giving more resources; Steve and Leo express concern because LogMeIn previously ended its free remote-access product and let Hamachi decay, and they conclude that nothing is wrong today but vigilance is warranted. Steve then covers Patch Tuesday (a critical scripting-engine flaw affecting only old Windows versions), a new academic result showing a "freestart" collision against the SHA-1 compression function, and a CA/Browser Forum ballot to allow limited SHA-1 certificate issuance through 2016. He also notes the Obama administration's decision not to seek mandatory encryption backdoors, quotes Snowden on the "nothing to hide" argument, and tells how he used SpinRite on a failing RAID drive. The Q&A covers WiFi Sense opt-out, the filter bubble, fingerprint unlock and compelled disclosure, free Windows 10 upgrade conditions, security questions, network-wide ad blocking with Pi-hole, AES key sizes, dynamic DNS, why VPNs use UDP, and searching episode transcripts.

# Key points

- LastPass acquisition: deal signed but not yet closed; Steve later cites roughly 110 million dollars plus a bonus tied to goals. Siegrist says the service stays under the LastPass brand, with servers still run by LastPass and more datacenter diversity planned with notice. Meldium, a team password sharing product, has a different design and may contribute features.
- Steve's stance: stay with LastPass for now, since the vault is encrypted client-side and can be exported to CSV, but apply "zero tolerance" if trust is breached; Leo says Siegrist's continued presence is his canary and mentions KeePass as the no-trust alternative with less convenience.
- Patch Tuesday: six bundles, three critical; the worrying one is a remote-code-execution flaw in the JScript and VBScript engines affecting Vista and Server 2008 (and probably unsupported XP), exploitable via web pages and ads; not yet exploited, but patch diffing makes exploits likely.
- Tools: the Firefox add-on Check4Change for watching page regions; 1Blocker 1.1 for iOS added easy per-site whitelisting; Tweetbot 4 revived for iPad.
- SHA-1: researchers produced a collision in the full-round SHA-1 compression function ("freestart") using a 64-GPU cluster over about ten days; not a full hash collision but it pulls forward Schneier's earlier timeline. Steve says GRC's SHA-1 certificates expire at the end of 2015 and he will switch to SHA-256 certificates.
- A CA/Browser Forum ballot proposed by several CA and vendor representatives would allow SHA-1 certificate issuance through 2016 for a few large enterprises that could not migrate in time; mitigation proposed was restricting issuance to subordinate CAs with path length zero and relying on serial-number entropy.
- Encryption policy: the New York Times reported the administration would not push legislation forcing decryptable devices; Steve adds that strong encryption is just math and cannot be taken back.
- Snowden quote: dismissing privacy because you have nothing to hide is like dismissing free speech because you have nothing to say.
- Listener Q&A: sharing your Wi-Fi password with a Windows 10 user could let WiFi Sense share it onward unless the SSID carries the _optout suffix.
- Filter bubble: personalised search results differ per user; Leo is not convinced it is dramatic; logged-out or incognito browsing reduces personalisation.
- Fingerprint unlock: courts can compel a fingerprint but not a passphrase; iPhone settings separate unlocking from other Touch ID uses and a reboot or power-off forces passcode entry; Steve uses a full alphanumeric passcode.
- Free Windows 10 upgrade carries no privacy difference from a paid one, but Leo says free upgrades require accepting Windows updates.
- Security questions: use random strings as answers and store them in the password manager; Leo noticed Keeper offers question-based recovery, which Steve says could be acceptable only if the answer derives the key.
- Network-wide ad blocking via DNS: Pi-hole on a Raspberry Pi blackholes ad domains for the whole LAN, but Steve expects anti-adblock detection to make lack of per-site whitelisting painful.
- Encryption strengths: AES-128 on the TLS channel is fine; password managers pre-encrypt vaults with AES-256 anyway, so channel strength matters little.
- Dynamic DNS (for example No-IP in routers) solves a changing home IP for remote access and is unrelated to ISP DNS security.
- VPNs use UDP because tunnelling TCP inside TCP causes duplicate retransmission and can collapse the tunnel.
- Searching: GRC.com has a search box covering transcripts, and Google's site: operator works too.

# Other segments

- SpinRite story: Steve recounts that the product began as a recovery of an unbacked-up accounting disk and describes rescuing his operations manager's failed RAID mirror overnight; he adds that consumer RAID should stop booting on first drive failure.
- Leo and Steve joke about LogMeIn "sucking up" products the show praised, including Hamachi.
