# Summary

Steve explained the technical substance of the FBI's court order to Apple over a shooter's work iPhone: Apple was asked to supply a modified build that removes the ten-guess wipe and software delays and permits electronic passcode guessing. He noted that iOS updates are personalised per device with a signed nonce, so a one-off build could be locked to a single phone, while stressing the policy question belongs to Congress and not a single judge. News items followed: Linux Mint's download site was breached and an ISO with a backdoor was served along with a doctored checksum; Tavis Ormandy found Comodo's suite installs GeekBuddy with a remote-desktop service on by default; Hollywood Presbyterian Medical Center paid about 17,000 dollars in bitcoin after ten days of ransomware; Dan Kaminsky analysed the glibc flaw and why DNS caches may block most attacks; and Apple released an iOS update to fix Error 53, saying it had been a factory test. The bulk of the show was Steve's account of the GRC attacks: how Level 3 null-routed addresses, why the attacker followed each change, why reverse-proxy DDoS services would force him to hand over certificates and kill services such as ShieldsUP!, and how listeners and industry rallied.

# Key points

- Apple versus FBI: the order sought three things (disable the ten-attempt wipe, remove added delays, allow automated guessing through a port), with only the hardware 80 ms per guess left. Weak short passcodes make the ask meaningful. Steve says the design means a signed, device-and-nonce-specific build could not be reused, but the real question is legislative; the case drew competing public views from a senator and a district attorney who holds many locked phones.
- Argument made on air: software makers lack the liability seen with physical products, and law has not decided whether phones deserve stronger protection than a locked safe.
- Linux Mint: attackers replaced a download with an ISO containing a backdoor that connected to an IRC server for commands, and altered the posted checksum; lesson is to host hashes on a separate site. Leo moved away from Mint, citing weak security attention.
- Comodo again: GeekBuddy installed as a service together with a VNC server by default, a paid support upsell. Steve advises avoiding the vendor.
- Hollywood Presbyterian Medical Center paid 40 bitcoin (about 17,000 dollars) to regain systems after reverting to paper and fax for ten days.
- Kaminsky on glibc: unusually bad because it is a universal library at a universal protocol; local-network attackers can exploit it, but traversal through intermediate DNS caches is uncertain, and network length limits would break things.
- Apple Error 53: Apple said it was a factory test never meant to hit customers, issued an iOS 9.2.1 fix and reimbursement route.
- DDoS history: SYN floods (and SYN cookies) gave way to raw bandwidth floods; bandwidth is billed on a 95th percentile basis; null routing drops traffic at the provider's edge but hides whether the attack continues; Level 3 gave Steve a dedicated port to protect neighbours.
- The attacker adapted: after the main IP was null routed, the web address was hit, then the news server, so Steve null routed the whole address block. Later short probes suggested testing of defences.
- Reverse-proxy mitigation providers would terminate TLS with a certificate for GRC and control its DNS, and cannot proxy raw TCP services; ShieldsUP!, the DNS Spoofability Test, newsgroups and email would be lost, so Steve chose to do nothing for now.
- Listener and industry support was strong, including duplicate SpinRite purchases and free mitigation offers.
