# Summary

Steve begins with PrintNightmare: Microsoft's June fix for CVE-2021-1675 only closed the researcher's example case, leaving a related flaw in the Windows Print Spooler (local privilege escalation plus remote code execution through the printer-driver install path) that was publicly exploited after a security firm briefly published a working proof of concept. Microsoft then issued an out-of-band update during the recording; Steve advises disabling the spooler wherever printing is not needed, especially on domain controllers. A joint NSA, CISA, FBI and UK NCSC advisory describes a GRU unit (Fancy Bear/APT28) running a Kubernetes-based brute-force and credential-stuffing campaign against hundreds of targets, with multifactor authentication as the main mitigation; Steve uses it to argue that passwords alone must be replaced. Western Digital took responsibility for the My Book Live wipe, explained the 2011 authentication refactor bug (CVE-2021-35941), and offered data recovery and trade-ins, but the larger group of My Cloud OS 3 devices remains vulnerable and unsupported. The main topic is the Kaseya VSA incident of July 2, 2021: an REvil affiliate used an authentication-bypass zero-day in Kaseya's on-premises remote management product to push ransomware through managed service providers to their clients, affecting around 1,000 to 1,500 organizations; the Dutch DIVD had already reported the bugs. Steve highlights the unusual ransom structure (per file extension, per MSP customer, and a $70M universal decryptor offer) and promises a cryptography deep dive next week.

# Key points

- PrintNightmare: Microsoft's June 8 patch for CVE-2021-1675 addressed the reported example but not the underlying bypass of the authorization check in the printer-driver installation call; Sangfor's published exploit code was pulled but forked; an emergency Windows update (KB5004945 and siblings) followed. Steve's advice: install it and disable the Print Spooler service on machines that do not print, notably domain controllers; the researchers promised more spooler bugs at Black Hat. 0patch offered a micropatch.
- GRU Unit 26165 (85th GTsSS) advisory from NSA, CISA, FBI and NCSC: since mid-2019, distributed brute-force and credential-stuffing from a Kubernetes cluster against Office 365 and on-premises email, combined with Exchange vulnerabilities for follow-on access. Mitigations: MFA, lockouts, strong passwords, zero-trust access signals, anomaly detection, and blocking anonymization services such as commercial VPNs and Tor where atypical. Steve also recommends allow-listing known source IP ranges and moving away from "something you know" authentication; he says FIDO and WebAuthn are useful but incomplete.
- Western Digital My Book Live follow-up: June 30 update admits two flaws (a root command injection, and an unauthenticated factory-reset endpoint introduced in April 2011 when authentication was centralized into a component configuration file and one endpoint was left without the right authentication type); attackers often exploited both. WD promised free data recovery and a trade-in to My Cloud; Steve is impressed.
- My Cloud OS 3 devices carry a remotely exploitable flaw chain and are no longer supported; WD released OS 5 days before Pwn2Own Tokyo, invalidating researchers' entries; WD told Brian Krebs it had not responded because it had "no questions". A researcher-made patch does not survive reboots. Steve says NAS devices should never be exposed to the internet and should be isolated; Nextcloud on maintained Ubuntu is his preference if exposure is unavoidable.
- SpinRite 6.1 milestone: the SATA/AHCI and PCI bus-mastering drivers were finished; the new core is next. Picture of the Week: a BMW owner running SpinRite on the car's hard-disk drive to fix the infotainment unit.
- REvil interview (from October 2020) trivia: the name stands for "Ransom Evil" (from Resident Evil); targets named include IT providers, insurance, legal and manufacturing; the group says decryption failures are rare; RDP and brute force remain the best vectors.
- Kaseya VSA incident: Kaseya (Dublin, with US headquarters in Miami) announced a limited attack on July 2; later estimates showed about 40 MSPs and 1,000 to 1,500 downstream businesses; Huntress traced it to an authentication bypass in the VSA web interface; Sophos counted at least 70 MSPs and 350 organizations; Coop in Sweden closed about 500 stores. VSA agents are required to be excluded from antimalware, which let the ransomware dropper run unscrutinized. DIVD (Victor Gevers) had reported zero-days to Kaseya, which was still testing fixes. Exposed VSA servers fell from about 2,200 to about 140 after notifications.
- Ransom structure: initial demands near $44,999 per file extension; $5M per MSP customer for all files; $70M in bitcoin for a universal decryptor published for everyone. Steve says victims' data was probably not exfiltrated and infers sophisticated hierarchical cryptography; next week's episode will cover REvil's design.
- Steve compares the attack to SolarWinds as a supply-chain compromise whose scale is a deliberate choice of the attacker; Leo recalls the Supermicro story.
