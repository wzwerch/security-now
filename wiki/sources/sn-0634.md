# Summary

Steve opens by saying security keeps getting both better and worse, then offers an RFC-themed picture about breaking protocols by checking every "must" and "should". ROCA fallout spreads to Gemalto's IDPrime .NET smartcards (potentially hundreds of millions, discontinued at end of September) and to roughly 750,000 Estonian electronic IDs. Researchers including Matthew Green present DUHK, where a deprecated ANSI X9.31 random number generator with a hard-coded key lets a passive observer recover VPN and HTTPS secrets from public nonces in a dozen products, notably Fortinet. The Kaspersky controversy gets a debate about the company's promise of source review and "transparency centers". Android appears to be getting DNS over TLS, another trojanized Mac download (Elmedia Player and Folx delivering the Proton RAT) continues the supply-chain trend, and Chrome is considering throttling mining pages. Leo reports on the Advanced Protection Program trial. The closing topic is the IoT Reaper/IoTroop botnet, already about two million devices, growing 10,000 per day and exploiting known flaws in routers, cameras and NAS boxes.

# Key points

- ROCA specifics: Gemalto IDPrime .NET cards (sold from 2004) use the faulty Infineon library; an Enigma Bridge test of 11 cards found all weak; Estonian national ID cards (about 750,000) and possibly Slovak and Spanish ones are affected. Each card has a unique public key, so cracking (about $10,000 of compute) enables targeted impersonation of one cardholder, not mass compromise. TPM 1.2 has the same exposure.
- DUHK (Don't Use Hard-coded Keys): the ANSI X9.31 PRNG was fine only if its output stays unseen, but protocols publish nonces; with a hard-coded key, observers can derive the generator state and decrypt past and future sessions. The authors found 12 FIPS 140-2 certified products with static keys; against Fortinet FortiGate they recovered RNG state for 21% of HTTPS hosts serving the default certificate, and demonstrated private key recovery on IPsec hosts. Lesson: avoid "application drift" of old primitives.
- Kaspersky (software on around 400 million computers) pledged external source and process audits, transparency centers in Asia, Europe and the US, and a bug bounty of up to $100,000. Leo is unconvinced because reviewed source does not prove the shipped binary matches; Steve notes reproducible builds are hard and Kaspersky faces severe commercial damage.
- Android appears to be adding DNS over TLS (RFC 7858, port 853) with opportunistic and key-pinned modes; it needs resolver support that barely exists yet. DNSSEC alone gives no privacy.
- Supply-chain trend: Handbrake mirror (earlier in 2017), CCleaner, and now Eltima's Elmedia Player and Folx, which delivered the Proton remote-access trojan for Macs (sold for $50,000 on cybercrime forums); download of 19 October affected; the vendor reacted quickly and recommends reinstalling the OS if infected.
- Browser mining: donateyourtab.to uses Coin Hive (30% fee) to mine for charities and lets users see hash rates; uBlock Origin, AdBlock Plus, NoCoin and minerBlock block miners; Chromium is discussing throttling pages that use excessive CPU.
- Chrome Cleanup now combines ESET's detection engine with Chrome sandboxing to remove unwanted software.
- Advanced Protection Program experience: needs one USB and one Bluetooth LE FIDO key, no extra cost, blocks third-party apps, did not work for Leo on Linux; no fallback, so losing keys triggers slow recovery.
- Marcus Hutchins: a judge lifted GPS-tracking and curfew limits; the Justice Department moved to revoke.
- Microsoft TruePlay in the Fall Creators Update: a manifest flag asks Windows for isolation to fight game cheating, an example of the OS providing protected-process sandboxing.
- IoT Reaper: about 2 million infected devices, adding roughly 10,000 per day, versus about 100,000 for Mirai a year earlier. Unlike Mirai's password guessing it exploits known vulnerabilities (some from 2013) in D-Link, Netgear, Linksys, TP-Link and MikroTik routers, GoAhead/JAWS/AVTECH cameras, a Vacron recorder and Synology NAS; integrated Lua environment and over 100 open DNS resolver addresses point to reflection DDoS; a new exploit was adopted within two days of publication. Steve says the owners will not patch and a major attack is likely.

# Other segments

- SpinRite testimonial: running Level 2 on a flaky Galaxy S7 SD card stopped repeated remount alerts; Steve explains the SD controller repairs weak regions.
- Discussion of whether security is improving, and of IETF versus IEEE/Wi-Fi Alliance paywalled specs.
