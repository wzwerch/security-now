# Summary

Steve opens with a blog-driven story about an iLife robot vacuum that phones home with logs, a house map and the owner's Wi-Fi credentials, and that was remotely bricked after its owner blocked telemetry. He then covers a proposed Russian law that would force researchers to register and report every vulnerability to the FSB-led state system, modelled on China's 2021 rules, which are linked to a rise in Chinese zero-day use. Short news items follow: two UK teenagers charged over the 2024 Transport for London attack attributed to Scattered Spider, the European Commission's preliminary Digital Services Act findings against Meta and TikTok, and a Microsoft Teams feature that sets work location from Wi-Fi. Coveware's Q3 2025 report shows ransom payment rates falling to 23% and describes how attackers get in (remote access compromise, social engineering, known unpatched flaws). A large block of listener mail debates NIST's no-forced-rotation password guidance and lists the main SP 800-63B rules. The main topic is the return of DNS cache poisoning: BIND CVE-2025-40780 (weak pseudo-random number generator that makes source port and query ID predictable) and CVE-2025-40778 (resolver too lenient with unsolicited records), plus related Unbound issues, patched on 22 October 2025. Steve recaps the 2008 Kaminsky attack and argues that a DNS resolver, which sits in a flood of unpredictable network traffic, has no excuse for a weak entropy source.

# Key points

- Robot vacuum (iLife A11): owner found it sending telemetry, a Cartographer-built home map and unencrypted Wi-Fi credentials to the vendor; blocking telemetry was followed by a remote kill command; Android Debug Bridge was reachable on the board and an rtty remote-root tool was preinstalled. Steve's view: such IoT devices run Linux, hold your Wi-Fi credentials and can reach the internal LAN, so use genuinely isolated guest networks.
- Steve would like a consumer tool that attributes outbound traffic to individual devices and hosts.
- Russia: draft law requires all vulnerabilities to be reported to the state (FSB, its NCCCI/CERT, and FSTEC), with registries of researchers (real names) and bug-bounty operators; failure to report would be criminal. Researchers object over kidnapping and foreign-sanction risks. Draft expected in the Duma by end of 2025.
- China's July 2021 vulnerability regulations (48-hour reporting to MIIT, no public disclosure before a patch) are cited, via a think-tank report, as feeding offensive operations; Microsoft's Digital Defense Report 2022 linked a jump in PRC zero-day use to the rules.
- Two teens (18 and 19) charged under the UK Computer Misuse Act over the August 2024 Transport for London attack (about 39 million pounds cost, roughly 5,000 customers notified); trial set for June 2026; the NCA attributes it to Scattered Spider.
- EU Commission preliminary DSA findings: Meta (Facebook, Instagram) and TikTok gave researchers burdensome data access; Meta's notice-and-action and appeal tools use dark patterns. Fines of up to 6% of global revenue are possible. Steve sees a lasting shift toward regulated platforms; Leo warns about protectionist motives.
- Microsoft Teams will (from December 2025, off by default, admin-enabled) set a user's work location from the organization's Wi-Fi, enabling return-to-office monitoring.
- Backup reality check: a survey found 95% of organizations confident in ransomware recovery but only 15% of attacked ones actually recovered their data.
- Coveware Q3 2025: ransom payment rate fell below 25% (23%) for the first time, from about 85% in 2019. Akira ran record volume via a vulnerability; groups split into volume RaaS against mid-market and targeted high-cost enterprise intrusions (CL0P file-transfer campaigns, Scattered Spider SaaS data theft).
- Initial access (Coveware): remote access compromise is over half of incidents; social engineering and remote access are merging (help-desk abuse, OAuth consent); most exploited flaws are old known bugs in network appliances; stale local accounts, unrotated credentials and unmonitored OAuth tokens cause breaches even on patched systems.
- NIST SP 800-63B feedback: 15 characters minimum for single-factor passwords (8 with MFA), accept at least 64 characters, accept Unicode, no composition rules, no forced periodic change except on evidence of compromise, no hints or knowledge-based questions, verify the full password. Listeners defend rotation (LastPass iteration-count example, delayed breach notices); Steve and Leo counter that long random manager-generated passwords, one-time codes and passkeys make rotation unnecessary, and that forced changes harm user goodwill.
- F-Droid is an app plus repository, never in Google Play (listener correction); Steve notes Texas SB2420 age-verification rules could make F-Droid non-compliant.
- Listener David worries age-verification standards (IAB/W3C workshop) could enable broader content restrictions; Steve thinks lawmakers act regardless and privacy-preserving technology is the priority.
- Java's updater now offers to uninstall itself after six months of non-use; Steve holds it up as model security behaviour.
- DNS: Kaminsky's 2008 attack (predictable source port and 16-bit query ID, spoofed UDP replies timed to cache expiry) recapped. BIND CVE-2025-40780 (weak PRNG, severity 8.6) and CVE-2025-40778 (accepts unsolicited resource records, 8.6), plus Unbound counterparts (5.6), patched; Red Hat rates them Important not Critical. Leo and Steve discuss entropy pools and reseeding of PRNGs.
- GRC's DNS Spoofability Tester (grc.com/dns/dns.htm) from 2008 is mentioned as the way to check resolver randomness.
