# Summary

Billed as a Q&A, the episode runs out of time before any listener questions because of a heavy news week. Steve covers the Gooligan Android campaign that roots older phones through third-party app stores, steals Google tokens and fraudulently installs apps for ad revenue; a Windows 10 feature-upgrade quirk that exposes a BitLocker-protected disk through a recovery command prompt; a Mirai variant that disabled routers for hundreds of thousands of UK customers; AirDroid's trivially weak encryption; a distributed guessing technique that recovers all card fields by exploiting merchants that check different subsets; a large new botnet seen by Cloudflare; hidden accounts in 80 Sony IP camera models; and how a researcher gained access to the Muni ransomware operator's email account. Smaller items include a USB port-frying device, keyless-entry jamming, conductive-ink fingerprint spoofing, UK police seizing an unlocked phone in the street, and the open-source BeamGun tool that blocks rogue USB keyboards and network adapters. Steve also answers a listener's question on judging new hard drives with SpinRite.

# Key points

- Gooligan: infected third-party-store apps (dozens found) download a root exploit for old Android 4 and 5 flaws, steal Google authentication tokens, and then install and rate apps to earn ad-network payments; over a million accounts were reported compromised and thousands more per day.
- BitLocker and Windows 10 upgrades: a researcher showed that pressing Shift-F10 during a major feature upgrade in the setup environment yields a command prompt with access to the otherwise encrypted drive. Steve infers Windows retains a way to unlock the volume during upgrades, unlike third-party full-disk encryption; mitigations are limiting Insider builds and unattended major upgrades.
- Mirai variant: UK customers of TalkTalk and the Post Office, and about 900,000 Deutsche Telekom customers, lost connectivity; affected D-Link and ZyXEL DSL routers were also being harvested for SSID and password, which can be mapped to a location through a public WiFi database.
- AirDroid (about 50 million installs): Zimperium found one round of DES in ECB mode with a hard-coded key and no authentication, letting a man in the middle inject updates and run code; the vendor was slow to respond and two new versions were still vulnerable before disclosure.
- Card-data guessing: because merchants validate different subsets of number, expiry date, security code and address, an attacker can recover the missing fields piece by piece across many sites; MasterCard locks cards after about ten failures while Visa lacked a comparable network-wide limit. Steve describes GRC's own strict attempt limits.
- Cloudflare tracked a new botnet driving layer 3/4 floods of up to several hundred Gbit/s for hours daily, distinct from Mirai's application-layer traffic.
- Sony: static firmware analysis found undocumented hard-coded accounts across 80 professional IP camera models that could enable telnet or SSH and give root; Sony issued patches.
- Muni ransomware aftermath: a researcher guessed the recovery question for the attacker's Yandex mailbox and passed the content to a journalist; it showed over a dozen bitcoin wallets and at least about 140,000 dollars extorted, mostly from US manufacturers and construction firms. SFMTA said it never considered paying and restored from backups.
- Atlas Recall: a cloud service capturing everything seen on screen to make it searchable, interesting but a clear privacy tradeoff; early Mac/iOS only.
- Echo and Google Home can be set into an endless loop of commands via calendar entries.
- USB Killer: a thumb-drive-shaped device that discharges high voltage into USB data lines, damaging most tested devices because ports lack surge protection.
- Keyless-entry jamming: thieves block the lock signal as owners walk away; always confirm the car actually locked.
- Samsung and Huawei fingerprint readers were fooled in about 15 minutes with conductive-ink prints.
- UK police staged a street seizure of a suspect's phone while it was unlocked to avoid needing his cooperation.
- BeamGun (open source) uses Windows device-notification hooks to block newly attached USB keyboards until approved and to disable new USB network adapters.
- Drive-health tips: compare SMART error-rate figures across several identical new drives, or watch the spread between minimum and maximum error rates on one drive.
