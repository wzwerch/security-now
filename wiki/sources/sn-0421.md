# Summary

Steve devotes the episode to the previous week's reports that the NSA had been weakening encryption, arguing that headlines claiming it can crack Internet crypto go well beyond the documents. Those documents, from a SIGINT Enabling Project with a 250 million dollar annual budget, state goals such as inserting vulnerabilities into commercial products and influencing standards, but give no specifics. The one known concrete case is the Dual_EC_DRBG random number generator in NIST's SP 800-90, whose unexplained elliptic curve constants could hide a trapdoor, as Schneier wrote in 2007 and as Shumow and Ferguson showed; nobody uses it because it is about a thousand times slower. Steve calls this a "perfect accusation": a lesson never to accept unexplained constants in standards again, and notes that NSA also improved DES's S-boxes in the 1970s. He quotes Matthew Green on how systems break (attack the math, the implementation, or the people) and which libraries deserve scrutiny, and reminds listeners that ECC and mainstream TLS are unaffected. Earlier items: September Patch Tuesday (14 bulletins, 47 flaws) plus Adobe updates, Yahoo joining the suit against the FISA court to publish request counts, myOpenID closing as OAuth social login takes over, LastPass's statement that it has no backdoor and would shut down rather than add one, the iPhone 5S Touch ID sensor and 64-bit processor, and SpinRite benchmark progress.

# Key points

- Patch Tuesday: four of 14 bundles critical, 47 flaws; Microsoft advised prioritizing Outlook, IE and SharePoint fixes. Adobe patched Flash, Acrobat, Reader, Shockwave and AIR. Browsers like Chrome and IE10 now auto-update.
- Yahoo petitioned the FISA court to publish the number of government requests, saying reports that PRISM taps its servers directly were false; Google, Facebook and Microsoft had made similar requests.
- myOpenID (Janrain) announced shutdown for February 2014; OAuth social login by then was led by Facebook (46 percent), Google (34), Yahoo (7) and Twitter (6).
- LastPass stated it has not weakened its product or added backdoors and would fight demands or shut down. Steve explains LastPass only stores a client-side encrypted blob keyed by a locally computed hash, so it is TNO and not Lavabit-like, though a compelled code change remains a theoretical risk.
- Documents quoted describe efforts to covertly influence commercial product designs, insert vulnerabilities, influence public key standards, get access to a major peer-to-peer voice and text service (suspected Skype) and enable encryption chips in VPN and web devices.
- Dual_EC_DRBG: one of four generators in NIST SP 800-90, biased and slow; Shumow and Ferguson showed the fixed curve points could be related by secret numbers letting someone predict outputs from about 32 bytes. They did not claim intent. Schneier advised using CTR_DRBG or Hash_DRBG instead.
- History: IBM's DES S-boxes were altered by the NSA, apparently to fix a weakness that academics only understood later.
- Matthew Green's analysis: three routes to break crypto (math, implementation, human side), Schneier says the math holds but code has been subverted; the Microsoft CryptoAPI/SChannel and OpenSSL libraries and Intel's hardware random generator deserve scrutiny.
- Steve's conclusions: nothing mainstream such as TLS has been shown broken, the hype serves budget requests, and combining several entropy sources (such as mixing OS randomness with local noise) defeats a biased generator.
- Apple's iPhone 5S adds a capacitive fingerprint sensor that compares against a stored hash of features rather than identifying a person; Steve is optimistic, and sees it as a good local factor for his authentication idea. The 64-bit processor is mainly useful for large-number math such as crypto.
- Earlier-reported claim that Miranda carried a written password was disputed by Greenwald.
- Hoax and fan items: Riddick film review, the net neutrality satire TheInternetMustGo.

# Other segments

- SpinRite 6.1 benchmarks match manufacturers' maximum sustained throughput; unusual cases found (an OCZ SSD not matching the ATA spec, add-on controllers hijacking the interrupt controller); development takes place interactively in GRC's NNTP newsgroups.
- Leo leaves for three weeks, with Tom Merritt hosting TWiT shows.
- Steve is tentatively preparing a web page for his authentication proposal.
