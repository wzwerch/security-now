# Summary

Ahead of Black Hat and DefCon, Steve and Leo start with the Chrysler fallout: the automaker issued a recall of roughly 1.5 million vehicles and the carrier blocked the exposed port, with a filing admitting a port was left open and firewall rules were too permissive. The main news is Stagefright, a set of memory-safety flaws in Android's native media library found by Zimperium, reachable by a crafted multimedia message and putting about 950 million devices at risk, with old phones unlikely to ever be patched. Steve then goes through a Google study on how security experts and non-experts stay safe (experts update, use two-factor authentication and password managers; non-experts lean on antivirus and password changes), a Fifth Circuit ruling limiting the DMCA anti-circumvention clause, and Anti-Phishing Working Group statistics. He recommends the InfoSecIndustry news aggregator, gives a registry setting for suppressing the Windows 10 upgrade offer, and corrects an earlier claim that System Restore helps against CryptoLocker. The topic segment explains how Tor works and why press claims that HORNET "fixes" Tor are wrong: it is an academic design for onion routing at the network layer that would need new router support, was only benchmarked with synthetic traffic, and does not solve traffic-correlation problems.

# Key points

- **Chrysler follow-up:** recall of about 1.5 million vehicles; owners can check by VIN, receive USB drives, or visit dealers; the cellular carrier closed the exposed port; a filing admitted an unintentionally open communications port and permissive radio firewall rules.
- **Stagefright (Zimperium):** about a dozen bugs in Android's C++ media code, around half remote code execution, triggered by MMS reception without user interaction; Android 2.2 onward affected, with pre-Jelly Bean most exposed because of weaker mitigations; Google has patches but handset and carrier updates lag; Steve hopes a benign test tool appears; the ACLU sued over abandoned phones. One bug was an off-by-one in an array size.
- **Google survey (231 experts, 294 non-experts):** experts stress system updates, two-factor authentication, password managers and checking the address bar and HTTPS; non-experts rely on antivirus, password changes, visiting only known sites and deleting cookies, and distrust password managers.
- **DMCA ruling (Fifth Circuit, MGE UPS/GE dongle case):** bypassing a technical protection is not itself a DMCA violation unless it enables traditional copyright infringement; seen as helpful for researchers.
- **Phishing (APWG, second half of 2014):** about 124,000 campaigns, about 95,000 unique domains; mean uptime about 30 hours and median 10 hours; three quarters of attacks target the top ten brands (Apple, PayPal and Taobao lead) and three quarters of registrations sit in five TLDs; e-commerce, banking and money transfer dominate.
- **InfoSecIndustry.com:** aggregator of advisories, blogs, podcasts and conference calendar, a companion to privacytools.io.
- **Windows 10 upgrade nag (GWX):** hiding the icon is not robust; a policy registry DWORD named DisableGWX set to 1 under the Windows policies key turns it off; hidden preload folders already appear on Windows 7.
- **Errata:** System Restore does not restore personal files, so it is not a CryptoLocker remedy.
- **HORNET:** high-speed onion routing at the IP layer using symmetric crypto per hop and Curve25519; prototype software router reached about 93 Gbps in synthetic tests; does not exist as deployable software, needs router support and does not address traffic analysis. Steve also recaps layered onion encryption and Tor's performance limits, with about two million daily users.
