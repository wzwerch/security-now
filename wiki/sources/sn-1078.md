# Summary

Steve opens with the FCC reversing part of its router policy: waivers allowing foreign-made consumer routers and drones already on the Covered List to receive firmware updates now run until January 2029, and Netgear (and then Eero) obtained a full conditional pass. AISLE reports CVE-2026-42511, a 21-year-old wormable remote command-execution flaw in FreeBSD's dhclient, imported from OpenBSD in 2005. Let's Encrypt briefly paused issuance after finding that its Gen Y cross-signed intermediates lacked a required serverAuth EKU. A TheNextWeb summary describes hundreds of malicious models on Hugging Face (pickle-based "nullifAI" evasion) and 341 or more malicious skills in OpenClaw's ClawHub, alongside brief package hijacks (LiteLLM, Bitwarden CLI, PyTorch Lightning). The CISA 2015 information-sharing law is moving toward long-term reauthorization. A SANS posting shows Microsoft Edge keeps saved passwords in cleartext in process memory, which Microsoft calls intended behavior. After listener feedback about Mythos pattern recognition, AI and computer-science education, and the risk of sending proprietary code to cloud AI, the main segment dissects DigiCert's April 2026 breach (social-engineered ZIP in support chat, EDR gap, exposed code-signing initialization codes, 60 certificates revoked) and its model incident report, and criticizes Microsoft Defender for mistakenly deleting DigiCert root certificates in response.

# Key points

- FCC: waiver for software and firmware updates to already-authorized covered routers and drones extended from March 2027 to at least 1 January 2029; no new models allowed. Steve calls the policy incoherent since router hardware is only as trustworthy as its firmware, and links the lapse to CISA's weakened state. Netgear and Eero got full approvals exempting them.
- AISLE (Joshua Rogers): CVE-2026-42511 in FreeBSD dhclient, where attacker-controlled DHCP reply data is persisted into a trusted script/config format and later executed with privilege; reachable by a malicious access point or on the same network, so laptops, PlayStation-class devices and servers are affected. OpenBSD removed dhclient-script in 2012. AISLE frames it as engineering, not "model mythology".
- Let's Encrypt outage: issuance paused after discovering the cross-signed Gen Y intermediates (YE/YR, created September 2025) lacked the serverAuth EKU required by CCADB policy; fixed by config change and issuance resumed. Steve reiterates his unease about Let's Encrypt as a single point of failure (70 percent of web certificates, six-day certs available).
- AI supply chain: Hugging Face (about 2.9 million models) has hundreds of malicious models; Protect AI scanned 4M+ models, flagging ~51,700; the nullifAI technique abuses pickle plus 7z compression to evade Picklescan. ClawHub audit: 341 malicious of 2,857 skills (335 from the "ClawHavoc" operation); Snyk reported broader flaws. March 2026 LiteLLM PyPI compromise, April Bitwarden CLI, PyTorch Lightning 42-minute compromise (Mini Shai-Hulud), Trivy poisoning hitting the European Commission.
- Advice: treat shared AI models and agent skills as untrusted; Leo has an assistant review and rewrite skills rather than installing public ones.
- CISA 2015 (Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act), extended temporarily to September, is on its way to long-term reauthorization; it gives private firms legal cover to share threat data.
- Edge stores saved passwords unencrypted in browser process memory, so a memory dump plus strings exposes site, user and password; Microsoft labels it intended behavior; Steve notes newsgroup members reproduced it.
- Listener points: Rival Security argued the FreeBSD NFS/RPCSEC_GSS bug Mythos found resembled the old MIT Kerberos CVE-2007-3999, meaning pattern recognition across training data; Steve and Leo see it as still valuable. A computer science educator argues AI makes coding cheaper but judgment more valuable. Another listener warns that sending closed-source code to cloud AI creates exposure risk; Steve agrees but notes many firms already use GitHub and expects local models eventually.
- DigiCert incident: on 2 April 2026 an attacker sent a malicious ZIP (a .scr screensaver disguised as a screenshot) through support chat; four attempts were blocked, the fifth compromised analyst machine ENDPOINT1 (contained 3 April). ENDPOINT2 lacked a CrowdStrike sensor and stayed compromised until 14 April. The attacker used the support portal's customer-view function to read initialization codes for approved but undelivered EV code-signing orders and minted certificates.
- DigiCert revoked 60 certificates (27 tied to the attacker, including some for Lenovo, Kingston and others linked to the GoldenEyeDog / APT-Q-27 Zhong Stealer campaign; 33 precautionary) within 24 hours of discovery with revocation dated to issuance, canceled pending orders, and published staged incident reports with a root-cause analysis.
- DigiCert's contributing factors: incomplete EDR coverage; insufficient privilege minimization (portal function not under PAM, codes not masked); initialization codes not treated as bearer credentials; overly permissive file attachments in support chat and Salesforce cases. Also noted: device-bound Okta FastPass acted as an MFA bypass on a compromised device, and the first investigation closed too early. 21 action items followed.
- Microsoft Defender detection update (around April 30, flagged as Trojan:Win32/Cerdigent.A!dha) wrongly removed DigiCert code-signing root certificates from Windows trust stores, invalidating everything chained to them; fixed in a later security intelligence update that restored certificates. Steve calls Microsoft's explanation unsatisfying and says checking thumbprints was all that was needed.
- Steve notes DigiCert's acquisitions mean many apparently competing CAs share a back end; GRC is moving its code signing to IdenTrust purely because of price.
