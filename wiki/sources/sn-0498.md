# Summary

This episode was delayed and recorded early because Steve had a cold. News: March Patch Tuesday (14 bulletins, five critical) included a fix for the FREAK flaw in Internet Explorer; The Intercept published Snowden documents showing the CIA, with defense contractors, has worked for years at the annual "Jamboree" on attacks against Apple devices, including extracting encryption keys and a modified Xcode that could insert backdoors, though Leo doubts the Xcode claim and notes the documents are dated. Steve advises stacking a trusted router behind an ISP-supplied one, explains how his own blogs broke after adding HSTS "include subdomains" because they use a CNAME to WordPress, and discusses the Apple Watch, the new MacBook and USB Type-C. The main topics are FREAK and RowHammer. FREAK exploits leftover 1990s US export-grade cipher suites (40-bit RC4 with 512-bit RSA): a man in the middle can rewrite a client's cipher list so the server picks an export cipher, and about 37 percent of servers with browser-trusted certificates, including NSA, White House and IRS sites, accepted it, as did clients such as Apple's Secure Transport, OpenSSL and Internet Explorer. RowHammer, from Carnegie Mellon, flips DRAM bits by repeatedly reading adjacent rows between refreshes; Google's team turned it into a Native Client sandbox escape and a Linux privilege escalation using the page map, and found about half of 30 laptops vulnerable.

# Key points

- March 2015 Patch Tuesday: 14 bundles, five critical remote-code-execution fixes, including an Internet Explorer cumulative update, a VBScript fix, and mitigation of FREAK in Windows; Stuxnet-related patch bypass noted.
- Snowden documents via The Intercept: a CIA research program funded for years through Lockheed Martin staff targeting Apple's encryption and firmware, with talks on non-invasive key extraction and physically extracting keys from chips, plus a claim of a modified Xcode; Steve notes a device in your hand can always be reverse engineered, and Leo notes Gemalto disputed the earlier SIM-key story.
- Steve says the NSA and other agencies are as aggressive as criminals and that export-grade crypto existed because strong crypto was classified a munition.
- If you cannot flash your ISP's router, put a router you trust behind it; the two stack cleanly.
- GRC lesson: adding "include subdomains" to HSTS broke the CNAME-hosted WordPress blogs because browsers demanded HTTPS certificates for GRC subdomains; removing it and revisiting GRC refreshed the cached header.
- Leo asks for a future explainer on DNS record types (CNAME, MX, A) and notes CloudFlare is lobbying to drop the DNS "ANY" query as an amplification vector.
- Apple Watch pricing and band costs, the new MacBook with a single USB-C port (up to 100 W power, two 10 Gb/s channels) and Force Touch trackpad discussed; Steve ordering one.
- FREAK: clients advertise strong suites, but an attacker replaces the list with an export-grade suite; vulnerable servers accept it, and vulnerable clients, though they never offered it, proceed. Export-grade keys (512-bit RSA) can be factored cheaply. About 36.7 percent of 14 million sites with trusted certificates were vulnerable, partly via Akamai; Chrome before 41, IE and Safari before March 9, Opera before 28, the Android browser and BlackBerry were affected; both ends must be vulnerable.
- RowHammer: repeated access to adjacent DRAM rows within the 64 ms refresh interval flips bits in neighboring rows; 111 of 129 DRAM modules tested from three makers were vulnerable, mostly newer, denser parts. Google's Project Zero (referred to in the audio as a "Code Zero" team) made two exploits: a break out of Chrome's Native Client sandbox by flipping bits in verified instruction blocks, and a Linux exploit that flips a bit in the process's own page table, using the readable pagemap, to gain access to all RAM; a rowhammer-test tool on GitHub lets Linux users check their machines.
