# Summary

Steve reports that KU Leuven researchers broke SIKE, one of the four NIST post-quantum candidates in the later round, with a purely mathematical key-recovery attack on its SIDH foundation; the already-selected lattice and hash-based algorithms (Kyber, Dilithium, Falcon, SPHINCS+) are unaffected, but experts note this is the second late-stage scheme broken in six months and urge caution. VirusTotal's "Deception at Scale" report shows 87% of signed malicious samples carried a valid signature, so code signing no longer signals trust and file-hash reputation matters more. Microsoft admitted that Windows 11 and Server 2022 corrupted data when vectorized AES (VAES) code paths were used for XTS and GCM modes, fixed in May to July 2022 updates, while a new Defender External Attack Surface Management service looks promising. Listener topics include 0patch for legacy Windows, the "three dumb routers" network isolation design, a FIDO Alliance demo of cross-vendor passkeys, in-flight Wi-Fi image blocking and emergency medical information. Steve teases next week's topic, Daniel Bernstein's second lawsuit over NSA/NIST post-quantum work, and closes by sharing Paul Graham's essay on the maker's schedule versus the manager's schedule.

# Key points

- SIKE (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation) was broken by KU Leuven's CSIS group in a classical attack based on a 1997 glue-and-split theorem; they received a $50,000 Microsoft bounty for "An Efficient Key Recovery Attack on SIDH". The NIST process began in 2017 with 69 candidates. Jonathan Katz notes three of four remaining schemes rely on assumptions whose difficulty is not fully understood.
- Leo clarifies SIKE was one of the alternates, not among the four chosen algorithms; Steve still stresses that data stored encrypted today may need re-encryption under post-quantum schemes.
- VirusTotal "Deception at Scale": 87% of more than one million signed malicious samples since January 2021 had valid signatures; over 58% of the most abused code-signing certificates were issued by Sectigo (formerly Comodo); the most spoofed apps are Skype, Adobe Reader and VLC, with 7-Zip, TeamViewer, CCleaner, Edge, Steam, Zoom and WhatsApp also in the top ten; malware is commonly hosted on abused legitimate services such as Discord, Squarespace, Amazon AWS and MediaFire.
- Steve's practice of scanning downloaded drivers with VirusTotal; many red flags from a single obscure engine are noise, dozens are not. Leo cautions against third-party driver downloads.
- Windows 11 and Server 2022 VAES issue (KB5017259): new vectorized AES paths in SymCrypt could damage data for AES-XTS (BitLocker) and AES-GCM (TLS) on the newest CPUs; the fix temporarily disabled VAES (slower) and later versions restored speed. Steve sees this as evidence of inadequate testing.
- Microsoft Defender External Attack Surface Management: daily discovery of internet-exposed unmanaged assets and shadow IT, comparable to a vendor-run Shodan; Steve finds the wording imprecise but the idea valuable.
- 0patch micropatches recommended by a listener for unsupported Windows 7 and 2008 systems, installed without reboot.
- Three dumb routers: two NAT routers behind a third give two mutually isolated networks; with two routers, put the network needing privacy behind the other. A listener's village-hall guest network problem illustrates upstream LAN access.
- FIDO Alliance demo (grc.sc/883): passkeys sync within an ecosystem (Apple iCloud) and cross-device login uses a Bluetooth plus QR flow, then creates a new local passkey, so no cross-vendor sharing; websites still need WebAuthn server support.
- In-flight Wi-Fi likely blocks images by capping bandwidth rather than inspecting encrypted content.
- Emergency medical info: Steve suggests a wristband with readable essentials on the underside and an optional QR code; Leo recommends iPhone Medical ID.
- Teaser: Dan Bernstein (originator of the Curve25519 family, SYN cookies and the 1990s crypto-export lawsuit) filed a second lawsuit over NSA, NIST and post-quantum cryptography.
- Paul Graham's "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule": makers need half-day blocks, meetings break them, and office hours confine interruptions; Steve links it to his dislike of running a 23-person company.
