# Summary

On a Patch Tuesday with medium-severity fixes, Steve covers a House vote of 383 to 43 to bar funding for NIST to work with intelligence agencies on weakening encryption, and the breach at the US Office of Personnel Management, where millions of unencrypted records were stolen and officials called encryption a new capability. He then describes a young researcher's firmware rootkit research for a major hard drive brand, which hides on the drive and replaces the boot sector only on the first read, and argues drive makers should sign firmware and move it onto the processor chip. Other news: Apple's iOS 9 moves to six-digit passcodes, Amazon applying to become a certificate authority, Let's Encrypt generating its root and an intermediate cross-signed by IdenTrust, the UK government selling surplus IPv4 space and the resulting routing-table fragmentation, Mozilla's server-side TLS guidance, and a graphene supercapacitor record. A second SQRL demonstration shows logging in by tapping a QR code on an iPad. Listener questions cover preparing a phone for repair, Apple routers, putting a vulnerable router behind another, a built-in fob sleep mode, the Windows BitLocker Elephant diffuser, Project Soli versus Leap Motion, fraud detection for card testing, expired-certificate errors caused by a reset PC clock, and a motion-activated fob idea.

# Key points

- Patch Tuesday: two critical updates (Internet Explorer and Windows Media Player), including a fix that stops a malicious website from reading browser history; a reboot is needed.
- House amendment to block NIST funding for collaborating with the NSA or CIA on undermining encryption passed 383 to 43; Steve recalls the compromised Dual_EC random generator that RSA shipped.
- OPM breach: about 4.1 million records, attributed to China-linked actors though China denied it; data was unencrypted, and the CIO said encryption was a new capability; contractors describe government networks as a sprawling mess.
- Drive-firmware rootkit research (MalwareTech): a boot kit stored in hard disk firmware survives reinstalls and formats, replaces the master boot record only on the first read, and is invisible to host software; installation needs admin rights, and removal requires reflashing the chip. The author did not release a proof of concept. Steve says firmware is unsigned and stored in an external chip that can be read directly; the fix is signed firmware and on-chip storage, but existing drives stay vulnerable.
- iOS 9 requires six-digit passcodes; Steve notes the earlier power-cut trick for guessing and prefers alphanumeric passcodes. OS X 10.11 reportedly still ships with the firewall off.
- Amazon applied to Mozilla and Android root programs to become a CA offering standard and EV certificates.
- Let's Encrypt created its root and intermediate; IdenTrust cross-signed the intermediate so that it is trusted from day one while the root spreads to trust stores.
- UK Department for Work and Pensions held a Class A IPv4 block, using about 70 percent; the government began selling surplus addresses (150,000 to a Norwegian company), with prices around eleven dollars each. Steve explains that selling pieces of big blocks fragments routing tables.
- Mozilla's Server-Side TLS wiki gives recommended configurations and cipher ordering; Steve's own Windows server list is limited by a 1K string length.
- A graphene supercapacitor prototype reached 131 Wh/kg compared with about 200 for lithium-ion.
- SQRL demos: tapping or clicking the QR code on a device with the SQRL client logs you in; the single secure factor simplifies login and gives the site no secret to keep; a Windows client is in progress.
- Preparing a phone for repair: turn on full-disk encryption then perform a reset, so wiped keys leave only noise; Leo notes that only the Nexus 6 enforces encryption by default and hardware support affects speed; iPhones encrypt by default.
- Apple AirPort routers have a clean vulnerability history and omit WPS and UPnP, making them a decent turnkey choice.
- A router with a USB NetUSB flaw can be placed behind another NAT router; check exposure of port 20005 with GRC's probe.
- Some key fobs have a documented sleep mode that stops radio responses; Steve urges reading manuals and checking in-car settings.
- Elephant diffuser: a BitLocker feature to make ciphertext tampering in an unauthenticated sector-encrypted disk harder; Microsoft removed it for performance, which Steve says weakens the design slightly.
- Soli differs from Leap Motion by being radar-based and potentially nearly free to integrate.
- Card-testing fraud can be reduced by real-time fraud engines that flag repeated small transactions from one device or location.
- An expired certificate error was caused by a PC clock reset to 1980 because certificates also have a not-valid-before date.
- A motion sensor in fobs would stop them answering when idle, though it would not stop an attack against a moving owner.
