# Summary

The first half of this episode continues the WannaCry story: reporting on how the NSA weighed disclosing EternalBlue, the bipartisan PATCH Act proposing a Vulnerability Equities Review Board, the likely purpose of the worm's kill-switch domain as a sandbox check, the doxing of the researcher who registered it, and the discovery that Windows XP through 7 can leave the RSA primes in memory so that tools such as WanaKiwi may recover files. Steve then covers Stack Exchange's explanation of why Let's Encrypt did not suit its HTTPS migration, ProPublica's findings of weak WiFi at Trump properties, British Conservative plans to heavily regulate the internet, and the long, unfinished story of certificate revocation, including Firefox dropping OCSP checks for domain-validated certificates and Apache and Nginx failing to handle stapling correctly. Further items include research on Rowhammer-like disturbances in multi-level-cell SSDs, Twitter abandoning Do Not Track, and LastPass Authenticator adding online backup, which Steve dislikes. The episode closes on Bob Metcalfe's Ethernet invention anniversary and listener feedback.

# Key points

- The Washington Post reported NSA staff knew EternalBlue was powerful enough to discuss disclosing it to Microsoft but kept using it for over five years ("like fishing with dynamite"). Steve says the public lacks the full picture of its intelligence value.
- The PATCH Act (Protecting our Ability To Counter Hacking) would codify a Vulnerability Equities Review Board of senior agency heads; Steve calls it bureaucratic theater with no effect, while Leo argues a structured process is still better than none. Steve notes Microsoft only patched XP and Vista after the outbreak.
- Kill switch logic: malware often queries a nonexistent domain to detect sandboxes that answer all DNS names; WannaCry's hard-coded gibberish domain probably served that purpose, so registering it stopped the original strain. Botnets then tried to DDoS the sinkhole, but only fresh or rebooted infections check it and new variants removed the check; a variant with six NSA exploits appeared.
- The researcher MalwareTech (Marcus Hutchins) was identified by UK tabloids despite wanting anonymity; he planned to donate a $10,000 HackerOne award.
- Decryption: WannaCry used the Windows CryptoAPI, and CryptReleaseContext does not wipe key material on Windows XP through 7, so on a machine that stayed on the key primes can be found in memory; WanaKiwi automates recovery. The sinkhole counted about 417,000 infected IPs, and Kaspersky data showed nearly all victims were on Windows 7, since the exploit fails on XP.
- A Wall Street Journal headline: all IT jobs are now cybersecurity jobs; a listener's company runs internal phishing tests.
- Stack Exchange completed its HTTPS conversion; its engineer explained why Let's Encrypt did not fit hundreds of domains, no wildcard support, non-SNI clients at 2 percent, shared certificates across load balancers and its CDN, and its use of DigiCert for a large EV SAN certificate.
- ProPublica and Gizmodo found weakly encrypted or open WiFi, unprotected printers and outdated servers at Trump family properties, including Mar-a-Lago, without breaking in.
- UK Conservative manifesto promised heavy regulation of the internet, including liability for search engines, a levy on internet companies, regulation of news, and a sanctions regime; headlines called it a new internet.
- Certificate revocation history: CRLs became too large, Chrome's CRLSets are minimal (revoked.grc.com still loads in Chrome but Firefox rejects it), OCSP adds latency and soft-fail weaknesses, stapling avoids the lookup, and the OCSP Must-Staple extension is meant to force it, but Apache (about 46 percent of servers) and Nginx (about 20 percent) discard a valid staple when a refresh fails, a bug reported to Apache in 2014. Firefox plans to stop OCSP fetching for DV certificates for speed.
- ETH Zurich researchers described program-interference and read-disturb attacks on MLC SSDs that raise error rates, similar to Rowhammer; Steve is unsure how practical they are but had chosen SLC drives.
- Twitter dropped support for Do Not Track.
- LastPass Authenticator now backs up codes to the LastPass vault; Steve argues this undermines the independence of the second factor and prefers a non-exportable app like Google Authenticator with printed QR codes.
- Listener items: Bitcoin passed 2,000 dollars; Cellebrite-style attacks on retry counters work by restoring nonvolatile snapshots; IDA Pro/IDA disassembler is the standard tool for reverse engineering; Windows NT supported MIPS and Alpha as well as other architectures; NTP can be tampered with and used for amplification; a listener argues against general-purpose Windows on signage and kiosks.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: Bob Metcalfe's 1973 hand-drawn Ethernet sketch, followed by Steve's explanation of why CSMA/CD with random backoff beat Token Ring, and a 1984 conference photo of Steve with industry figures.
- WannaCry riddle joke, SpinRite testimonials (a NAS drive refreshed by Level 2, a hot laptop cooled after a run).
- Maker Faire banter between Leo and Steve.
