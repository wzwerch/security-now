# Summary

Two "ultra-clever" attacks headline the episode. eFail tricks HTML-rendering email clients into decrypting a previously captured PGP or S/MIME message and leaking the plaintext to an attacker's server through a remote-image URL; Steve and Leo agree the real fault lies in missing authentication of ciphertext and sloppy mail clients. Throwhammer shows Rowhammer bit flips can be triggered remotely by sending traffic over fast RDMA-enabled networks, turning a local-only attack into a network one. Earlier in the show: UPnProxy abuse has evolved to rewrite source ports to dodge DDoS filters, an Electron framework setting allows cross-site scripting to become code execution, EternalBlue has become commodity malware a year after WannaCry, several botnets fight over vulnerable GPON routers, thousands of Brazilian ISP routers have Telnet open without a password, and Hide and Seek is the first persistent IoT botnet with a peer-to-peer control mesh.

# Key points

- eFail: the attacker builds a multipart email whose first part opens an image tag with a URL on their domain, whose middle part is the intercepted encrypted message, and whose last part closes the tag. The victim's client decrypts the middle part and fetches the "image", sending the plaintext in the URL. Defenses: do not render HTML in the mail client that decrypts, update clients (several had quiet fixes), decrypt outside the mail client; EFF advised temporarily disabling email encryption plugins and using end-to-end messengers such as Signal. Matthew Green: PGP's lack of default authentication for 17 years and client sloppiness are to blame; S/MIME is more exposed. One vulnerable recipient is enough.
- Throwhammer (universities in Amsterdam and Cyprus): over 10-gigabit RDMA, about 9 million memory accesses per second sufficed to flip bits remotely and compromise a server without a software bug; mitigation requires hardening DRAM or moving network buffers out of main memory.
- UPnProxy follow-up (Imperva): about 1.3 million routers expose their UPnP description, and attackers use port translation to move DNS/NTP amplification traffic off ports 53/123 so static DDoS filters miss it.
- Electron apps (Skype, VS Code, Atom, Signal, Slack, Discord and many others): when the webview option is not explicitly disabled, cross-site scripting can enable Node integration and run code on the desktop; fixed in newer Electron versions, but each app must ship an update.
- EternalBlue has spread beyond XP/7 to Windows 8 and 10 versions of SMBv1 targets and is in many malware toolkits; WannaCry still scans.
- Mirai, Hajime, Satori, Mettle and Muhstik vie for roughly 240,000 vulnerable GPON routers; vpnMentor offered an unofficial antidote patch while the ISP stalled.
- Brazilian ISP Oi supplied Datacom routers with open, password-less Telnet exposed on the WAN (about 5,000 found).
- RSA Conference survey: 47% patch promptly, 8% only once or twice a year, 26% ignored a critical flaw for lack of time, only 17% ever hired a pen tester; 71% believe an outside attack on their own company would succeed.
- Hide and Seek (Bitdefender): about 90,000 devices; custom peer-to-peer UDP control mesh; persists by writing into init scripts after Telnet brute forcing; many CPU architectures; closes Telnet behind itself.
- Steve corrects the belief that a SpinRite repair means the drive must be replaced: ECC runs constantly and spare sectors exist; a sector that slipped past correction is not the same as drive failure.
- Spectre-NG: no new details yet beyond last week's Heise report.

- Listener feedback: an old router with a self-signed or SHA-1 certificate forced a user to switch to HTTP-only on the LAN; Steve notes browsers' strictness collides with legacy devices.
