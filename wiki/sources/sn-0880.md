# Summary

With Jason Howell filling in for Leo, Steve first revisits Honda's weak reply to last week's Rolling-Pwn disclosure, noting that rolling codes exist precisely because attackers have sophisticated tools. He then notes that IPv4 addresses now cost roughly $50 to $60 each while free IPv6 sits unwanted, and walks through a Council on Foreign Relations task-force report concluding that the open, global internet vision has failed. Facebook responded to Firefox and Brave URL-parameter stripping by turning its links into opaque encrypted blobs, which Steve takes as proof that anti-tracking must come from law, not browser tricks. Apple's Lockdown Mode comes with a record $2M bounty. Clearview AI was fined 20M euros by Greece, three ransomware gangs added searchable leak databases, and Russia fined Google heavily. Patch Tuesday fixed 84 flaws with one exploited zero-day. A SpinRite floppy-recovery testimonial and listener tips on travel routers and Tailscale exit nodes precede the main topic: RetBleed, an ETH Zurich attack showing that return instructions can be hijacked like indirect branches, defeating the retpoline mitigation for Spectre variant 2 on Intel and AMD CPUs.

# Key points

- Honda follow-up: spokesperson claims "sophisticated tools" are needed and that newer 2022-23 models (Civic, MDX, HR-V) send immediately expiring codes, which Steve says is contradictory since all rolling codes expire by design and the researchers tested 2022 models. Safe resync may only move counters forward.
- IPv4 prices (SIDN): about $5 per address in 2015, $25-35 a year ago, $50-60 now; IPv6 is free and plentiful but unadopted. Steve says ShieldsUP! and the DNS Benchmark need IPv6 support after SpinRite work.
- Council on Foreign Relations report "Confronting Reality in Cyberspace" (116 pages): the global open internet era is over, data is geopolitical power, cybercrime is a national-security risk, sanctions and indictments have not stopped state hackers; 16 recommendations include a privacy policy interoperable with GDPR, election and financial-system norms, accountability for attacks emanating from a state's territory, and ISP incentives (for example blocking spoofed source addresses).
- Facebook turned shared URLs into opaque encrypted tokens within days of Firefox 102 stripping tracking parameters; Steve cites RFC 1738 on URLs as opaque to everyone but the server, and concludes privacy enforcement needs policy.
- Apple offers a $2 million bounty for a reproducible Lockdown Mode compromise of iOS 16, aimed at commercial spyware such as Pegasus.
- Clearview AI: Greek regulator fined it 20M euros under GDPR and ordered deletion of Greek residents' data and an EU representative, even though it has no EU customers; earlier UK, French and Italian rulings, Austria expected. Faces are treated as biometric data. Steve calls the extraterritorial reach a bit of overreach but expects Clearview to lose the fight.
- Ransomware gangs AlphV, Karakurt and LockBit added searchable databases of stolen data to their leak sites to raise reputational pressure on victims (Emsisoft's Brett Callow).
- Russia fined Google about $358M (21 billion rubles) for not removing content demanded by Roskomnadzor, on top of earlier fines; Google's Russian subsidiary filed for bankruptcy.
- July 2022 Patch Tuesday: 84 flaws, 52 elevation of privilege, 12 remote code execution, one actively exploited zero-day, no major breakage reported.
- SpinRite testimonial: an engineer recovered a power-station PLC configuration from corrupt 3.5-inch floppies using SpinRite 5, which Steve says oddly beats 6 on diskettes (v5 download is documented in the GRC FAQ).
- Listener tips: a travel router with a NAT firewall using the home SSID, and a Tailscale exit node on a home device to route traffic through home from untrusted Wi-Fi. A Ford key fob with bidirectional confirmation is noted.
- RetBleed (ETH Zurich researchers; CVE-2022-29901 and an AMD counterpart): return instructions behave like indirect branches under some microarchitectural conditions, so an unprivileged attacker can steer speculative execution and leak kernel memory on fully patched Intel Core generations 6-8 and AMD Zen 1, 1+ and 2, defeating Google's retpoline mitigation. Leak rates of 219 bytes/s (Intel Coffee Lake) and 3,900 bytes/s (AMD Zen 2) were shown. Disclosed to vendors in February, embargo lifted July 12 (Patch Tuesday). Mitigations cost between 14% and 39% performance, and AMD's "Phantom JMP" issue up to 209%. SUSE had removed IBRS three years earlier on Intel's advice; Linus delayed Linux 5.19 by a week (rc8). Intel's advisory called the leak "non-transparent sharing". Steve's refrain: attacks only ever get better.
