# Summary

The episode opens with a "404" parking-meter picture and a quick tour of Firefox 106 (in-browser PDF editing, redesigned private windows, text recognition in images on macOS, a WebRTC library jump). Steve then highlights Google's open source KataOS, a secure-by-design embedded operating system built in Rust on the formally verified seL4 microkernel, with a Sparrow reference platform; he acknowledges it is a toy for now but argues this is what the future shape of secure software must look like, while Leo prefers hardening mature systems such as Qubes. The week's crypto craziness is the Mango Markets exploit, where a trader used oracle price manipulation to extract about $114 million and then, after being identified, negotiated to keep $47 million. A Windows zero-day lets files with a deliberately malformed Authenticode signature skip the Mark-of-the-Web warning, and is being used to deliver Magniber ransomware via JavaScript; Microsoft claimed it could not reproduce it. Apple fixed its ninth zero-day of the year (CVE-2022-42827), Mandiant explained why Ursnif abandoned banking fraud, and VMware patched a year-old critical XStream flaw (CVE-2021-39144). After listener feedback, the main topic is Australia's proposed jump in privacy-breach penalties (to the greater of $50 million, three times the benefit, or 30% of turnover), on which Steve argues that culpability must matter and that the software industry's license terms shield vendors while victims are punished.

# Key points

- Firefox 106: PDF editing, default PDF handler on Windows, pinned private windows, swipe navigation on Linux Wayland, image text recognition on macOS 10.15+, libwebrtc updated from version 86 to 103, plus unspecified security fixes.
- KataOS: three Google Research engineers open-sourced a secure OS for embedded ML devices built on seL4 (formally proven for confidentiality, integrity, availability) using the CAmkES component framework, written almost entirely in Rust, with a Sparrow reference design adding an OpenTitan RISC-V root of trust; initial target is 64-bit ARM under QEMU/Renode simulation.
- Steve's argument: software can be provably flawless if the hardware is correct (Rowhammer and Spectre remain hardware problems), and Windows' move of GDI into the kernel for speed is the cautionary tale of trading security for performance; Leo notes provable systems avoid messy I/O and remain impractical.
- Mango Markets (Solana-based DeFi exchange): Avraham Eisenberg manipulated a price oracle to borrow against inflated collateral, draining about $114 million; token holders (voting by wallet size) approved letting him keep $47 million and return $67 million; he called the trades legal market actions. Steve says the DeFi world is immature; Leo says only a regulated bank has rules.
- Windows MotW bypass: HP found Magniber ransomware delivered as JavaScript files signed with a malformed signature; Will Dormann confirmed that Windows 10/11 then shows no SmartScreen or Mark-of-the-Web prompt (Windows 8.1 is unaffected) and that disabling the "Check apps and files" reputation setting restores the older behaviour. Microsoft said it could not reproduce, and no CVE existed yet.
- Steve explains NTFS Alternate Data Streams: the Zone.Identifier stream marks internet-origin files, triggers SmartScreen and Office Protected View, and is visible with `dir /R`.
- Apple CVE-2022-42827: kernel out-of-bounds write in iOS/iPadOS 16.1, possibly exploited; ninth Apple zero-day of 2022.
- Banking malware: Mandiant reports Ursnif/Gozi dropped banking fraud for a generic backdoor (access-as-a-service) because source leaks, diverging branches, and Internet Explorer's removal killed its old variant; banks' MFA and transaction verification, plus ransomware economics, pushed Emotet, TrickBot, Dridex and Qbot the same way. Ransomware can be deployed within 30 to 60 minutes of intrusion, so detection matters.
- VMware Cloud Foundation (NSX-V) fixed an unauthenticated remote code execution (CVE-2021-39144, CVSS 9.8) via the XStream deserialization library about a year after disclosure; a vCenter 8.0 privilege-escalation flaw from Nov 2021 also remained unpatched.
- Listener feedback covered NetWorx (free 5.5.5 version) with SNMP monitoring and logarithmic bandwidth graph, a power/reset puck for motherboards, SpinRite 6.1 testing (still at drive-detection stage; promised free upgrade for 6.0 owners), why chained cipher modes being brittle is a feature for tamper detection (encrypt then authenticate), and Elaine Farris as the show's transcriber.
- Chrome will drop Windows 7 and 8.1 support in early 2023, which Steve calls arbitrary given about 12.7% of desktops; Steve also tried Edge and noted Alt-Tab switching between browser tabs on Windows 10.
- Australia's Privacy Legislation Amendment (Enforcement and Other Measures) Bill 2022, after Optus, Telstra, Medibank, Woolworths and EnergyAustralia incidents, raises maximum penalties from about AUD 2.2 million to the greater of AUD 50 million, three times the benefit, or 30% of adjusted turnover. Steve: fines should consider negligence versus an unavoidable zero-day, and the industry's "no warranty" licensing contributes; Leo adds that many breaches stem from avoidable errors such as open S3 buckets.
