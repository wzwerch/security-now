# Summary

This is a pure deep-dive episode with no news. Steve first explains his recent silence on SpinRite: he scrapped and rewrote the core data-recovery code, which had become brittle after six months of special-case patches for badly failing drives. The new design centralizes error and abort handling in the IO abstraction layer, which can reset the stack to a checkpoint, and it also handles an odd ATA-8 allowance that a drive may abort a transfer without delivering earlier data. The rest of the show walks through the IETF draft "Detecting Unwanted Location Trackers" from Apple and Google. Steve finds the 22-page draft poorly organized and less complete than hoped (no full crypto detail), but it defines tag modes, address rotation, sound and motion behavior, serial-number lookup and the pairing registry. He argues that the registry, available to law enforcement on valid request, creates accountability, and discovers that Apple already provides paired-account details to law enforcement today. Leo is uneasy at first but, once it is clear that only ownership (not location) is disclosed, accepts it; Steve plans to buy AirTags for luggage.

# Key points

- SpinRite rewrite: special-case handling moved into the common IO layer, which resets the stack to a saved checkpoint at the top of each block or sector worker so any abort unwinds cleanly; SpinRite 6.1 requests large transfers and now re-requests data before an error sector; the work will carry into SpinRite 7. Steve had 683 registered development testers at the time.
- Coding lesson, quoted from a colleague: "do it, then do it better, then do it right."
- Market context: about 55 million AirTags sold in two years (over $1 billion) and around 40 million Tile trackers; one Apple account can hold up to 16 AirTags. Location comes from crowd-sourced reports by nearby phones, not GPS or cellular in the tag.
- The draft applies as REQUIRED to small, hard-to-notice accessories; large items (over 30 cm in one dimension, and similar thresholds) get only recommendations. E-bikes with built-in Find My were cited as an example.
- Tags have Near-Owner and Separated modes and must switch within 30 minutes; location-enabled advertisements (sent every 0.5 to 2 seconds) are advertised only when separated, to avoid false alerts for people traveling with the owner.
- Bluetooth LE addresses must be private and rotate: on every mode change, every 15 minutes near the owner, and every 24 hours when separated, derived from a pairing secret key (comparable to TOTP).
- Non-owner finding: after a random 8 to 24 hours separated, an accelerometer arms; movement over about 10 degrees on two axes triggers a loud sound of at least 5 seconds, faster sampling, a cap of 10 sounds or 20 seconds, then a six-hour back-off. Any nearby phone can also command a tag to play a sound. Steve notes an acoustic enclosure could defeat the sound.
- Tags expose product ID, manufacturer and model strings, a category byte (luggage, bike, inhaler, and so on) and capability flags; instructions for disabling a tag must be reachable via a registry URL and require a physical action.
- Serial numbers must be printed on the tag and retrievable by NFC tap or by Bluetooth LE after a physical button action; the payload is encrypted so only the manufacturer server can decrypt it, enabling rate limiting and anti-fraud, with replay protection suggested.
- Pairing registry: verified owner identity is stored with the serial number (kept at least 25 days after unpairing); nearby users see only obfuscated owner info (last four phone digits or masked email); full data goes to law enforcement on valid request.
- Apple's 2022 AirTag statement says it already provides paired-account details to law enforcement on subpoena; Steve stresses Apple cannot see tag locations, only ownership.
- Leo smashes an AirTag with a hammer on air; it stops reporting.
