# Summary

Steve and Leo record a Q&A after a quieter week. The news covers an iMessage string that crashed iPhones whenever it was rendered in notifications or previews, a researcher's finding that certain older MacBooks leave their firmware writable after resuming from sleep (so an attacker with administrator rights could alter the firmware, mitigated by shutting down instead of sleeping and by avoiding an admin account), and Microsoft's tray icon that offers a free Windows 10 reservation, which many users mistake for malware. Steve describes Google's Project Vault, a microSD-form-factor hardware security module that appears as virtual read and write files, and Project Soli, a 60 GHz radar for fine hand gestures. He then demonstrates SQRL live by logging in to a demonstration page on Leo's computer from his phone by scanning the displayed QR code. Other items include the EFF being sued by a patent attorney, the Senate passing the USA Freedom Act, and failed TSA covert tests. Listener questions cover why Steve is moving to Windows 10, whether static-prevention bags block key fobs (they do not), a free ad-blocking app that rerouted traffic through a VPN, balancing NoScript use, credit-card testing against small merchants, and the apparent contradiction between Steve's comments on key escrow and his praise for Blaze's testimony.

# Key points

- iMessage crash: a crafted combination of English words, Unicode and Japanese characters crashed the text-rendering component (CoreText) when shown in notification or preview views; workarounds only delete the message; iPhone 4 on its last iOS version was not affected. Steve and Leo suspect fuzzing found it and note crashes can be precursors to exploits.
- Mac firmware bug: on MacBook models with firmware from about mid-2014 and earlier, resuming from sleep fails to re-apply the firmware write protections; a program with administrator privileges could then modify the firmware below the OS level. Apple appeared to have silently fixed it in newer machines but not for older hardware. Mitigations: do cold shutdown, run as a standard user, update firmware if available.
- Windows 10: Microsoft added a Get Windows 10 reservation icon via two update packages that can be uninstalled; the actual release was planned for late July. Steve explains his interest in pairing desktop and server versions.
- Google Vault: a hardware module in microSD form that does bulk encryption and keeps keys inside; Steve calls it a TPM with processing power that is portable; it exposes itself as read and write pseudo-files so no drivers are needed.
- Soli: radar-based gesture sensing at 60 GHz with Doppler measurement allowing very fine finger motion detection at 5 cm to 5 m.
- Supercapacitors: a utility partnership claimed a 10,000-farad device but gave no voltage rating; Steve reviews storage options for electric vehicles.
- SQRL demo: Steve logged Leo into a GRC demo page without Leo touching his computer or sending credentials; the QR code is unique per display, includes a counter and encrypted data, and ties the phone's signed reply to the browser session; potential use for letting family log in once without sharing passwords.
- EFF was sued for defamation by the attorney behind a questionable patent it had criticised; Steve and Leo explain how costly it is for small parties to challenge patents.
- USA Freedom Act passed the Senate 67 to 32; call records would stay with phone companies and be provided on request.
- Covert tests found most weapons passed through TSA screening.
- Windows 10 upgrade rationale: Steve still uses Windows XP desktop but sees Windows 10 as the final big Windows version and wants desktop and server in sync.
- Anti-static bags do not block radio signals; a conductive sealed container or a shielding pouch does; Leo confirmed his pouch prevents the car from recognising the fob.
- A free iOS ad blocker worked by installing a VPN profile and routing all traffic through a third party; Steve notes it cannot see inside HTTPS without a certificate installed and that such apps carry privacy risk.
- Mozilla's tracking-protection statistics: some pages load around 150 tracking elements and about 80 percent of third-party content is script.
- NoScript advice: default-block while browsing broadly and temporarily allow scripts on sites you chose to use.
- Credit card testers submit small orders to check stolen cards, often targeting charities and small shops; no good block was suggested, and merchants are increasingly liable.
- Key escrow clarification: the math can support multiple keys, but managing extra keys across a large system is the practical failure, which is why experts oppose mandates. Facebook allowed users to upload a PGP key for encrypted notification emails.
