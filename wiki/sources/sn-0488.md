# Summary

Steve joins Leo in the studio for the last show of 2014. He explains that his SQRL implementation now works as a login alongside usernames and passwords but is not yet public because he wants the first impression right. He then covers the Sony attribution debate: after the US president blamed North Korea, security experts such as Marc Rogers of CloudFlare and Bruce Schneier called the public evidence weak, pointing to public proxy IP addresses, a later-added anti-North-Korea angle, the mass data dump and hardcoded internal server names that suggest insiders or a long-running intruder; Steve and Leo expect the truth may never be known. A short, poorly coordinated outage of North Korea's tiny Internet allocation and the Christmas DDoS of Xbox Live and PlayStation Network by the Lizard Squad lead to a talk about SYN floods, spoofed packets and egress filtering. Apple forced out an automatic patch for a remote code execution bug in the NTP daemon that runs by default on Macs. Der Spiegel's Snowden documents show the NSA rating tools like TrueCrypt and OTR as major obstacles, especially combined with Tor. The rest is a chronological recap of 2014: Target and Home Depot, a router backdoor on port 32764, Goto Fail, TrueCrypt's abrupt end, Heartbleed and the broken revocation system, BadUSB, Google's early SHA-1 sunset, Shellshock, POODLE, Regin and the POODLE variant in load balancers. Steve closes by predicting the industry will move toward application whitelisting.

# Key points

- SQRL status: working but private; supports coexisting with password logins so a user can later delete the site-held username and password; the site-side check is a single signature verification, with Drupal and PHP implementations and an Android client already available.
- Sony attribution: experts argue the evidence for North Korea is weak (proxy servers in many countries, the film angle only appeared after the press raised it, simple extortion demands in poor English, a huge indiscriminate dump, possible insider access and a March 2014 compromise). Government claims of secret intelligence cannot be verified, and attribution of network attacks is inherently hard.
- North Korea has about 1,024 IP addresses reached via China, so its brief outage after the US statement may not have been a state attack; Steve doubts it was sophisticated.
- Lizard Squad's Christmas DDoS of Xbox Live and PlayStation Network used decades-old techniques such as SYN floods; they stopped after Kim Dotcom gave them vouchers for his Mega service. Defenses mentioned: large proxy services like CloudFlare and ISP egress filtering against spoofed source addresses.
- NTP flaw: a buffer overflow in the Network Time Protocol daemon (fixed in version 4.2.8) allows remote code execution when the service is exposed; Apple pushed its first forced automatic update for it; home users behind routers are not exposed and Windows does not run an NTP server by default.
- NSA threat ranking from Der Spiegel: trivial (Skype, which Microsoft made interceptable in 2011), minor, moderate, major (TrueCrypt, OTR) and catastrophic (OTR combined with Tor). Leo reads it as good news that math still protects some tools; weak points are the certificate authority system and VPN or Tor endpoints.
- Tor de-anonymization research suggesting about 80 percent of traffic could be unmasked is again deferred to a future "DeTor" show.
- Year recap: Target (40 million cards, 110 million records, banks suing for negligence) and Home Depot (56 million); a router backdoor at port 32764 later replaced by a LAN-only knock mechanism, tied to Sercomm; Goto Fail in Apple's TLS code on 21 February; TrueCrypt's graceless shutdown in spring, with Matthew Green's audit and forks such as CipherShed and VeraCrypt; Heartbleed, an OpenSSL heartbeat bug leaking 64K of server memory including private keys, which forced mass re-keying and exposed that certificate revocation does not work; BadUSB at Black Hat, where reprogrammable USB controller firmware lets a device pose as other devices (not a worm, Steve says); Google moving to penalize SHA-1 certificates valid past 2017 even though SHA-1 is not broken; Shellshock in bash, exploitable via CGI environment variables and used by worms; POODLE, the SSL 3 padding oracle with a protocol-downgrade step; and Regin, state-grade four-stage malware that antivirus firms kept quiet about.
- GRC's own SSL Labs grade is C because Steve keeps SHA-1 (expiring end of 2016 so Chrome will not warn), SSL 3 and RC4 for old XP clients; he intends to reboot and turn off SSL 3 and RC4 to reach B.
- Steve's conclusion: Snowden's leaks made strong security a market feature; large networks cannot be fully secured against human error; he expects whitelisting (AppLocker-style) with signed apps to become the norm, though it breaks things as ASLR/DEP and NoScript did.

# Other segments

- Brief talk about consumer drones (Parrot Bebop), the Libretto laptop on Steve's stair climber, and the TWiT New Year's Eve charity broadcast for UNICEF.
