# Summary

The Q&A episode starts with a Today show clip of thieves silently opening locked Acura and Honda vehicles with a palm-sized gadget in California; police were stumped, and Steve guesses it attacks the car's internal network near the passenger door rather than the rolling-code crypto, so he advises not leaving valuables in cars. He reminds listeners the Zeus banking trojan is thriving through Facebook links, injecting extra fields into bank login forms. The EFF filed an amicus brief arguing APIs are not copyrightable in Oracle v. Google, which Steve strongly supports, and the EFF also began taking bitcoin again after getting 726 coins back. Steve clarifies that IE8 on XP lacks SNI, which matters after Google's certificate change, notes LinkedIn added two-step login (with reported SMS spam), and shares a "dumb security story" of a state health portal telling a user his chosen password was already in use, implying plaintext or at least unsafe handling. Listener questions cover Astrill VPN blocking GRC (because ShieldsUP shows the VPN server's ports), why a site must be HTTPS from the first request to avoid SSL stripping even with HSTS afterwards, a household of three routers in China, third-party cookies breaking job sites, quantum "envelopes", pumped-storage hydropower, a request for lock/unlock in BitTorrent Sync, adware browser plugins that hyperlink words, Deutsche Telekom's throttling plan versus net neutrality, and contactless-card skimming risk.

# Key points

- Keyless car door thefts of contents (not cars): Acura and Honda models opened in seconds on the passenger side, no alarm; Steve suspects a signal injected into the car's CAN-like network below the rolling-code layer; do not leave valuables inside.
- Zeus: stealth banking trojan, linked to Russian organized crime, spreading on Facebook via fake profile links; modifies login forms to harvest extra identifiers and captures keystrokes before encryption.
- EFF amicus brief (32 computer scientists, including Tim Paterson and Larry Roberts) supports the district judge's ruling that APIs are not copyrightable; Steve says Java succeeded because its API was open and clones like FreeDOS, ReactOS and POSIX depend on that.
- IE8 on XP has no Server Name Indication, so servers that need SNI will not work with it; SNI was added to TLS in 2006.
- LinkedIn added second-factor authentication, joining Twitter and Evernote; users report SMS spam after enabling it; Steve wants authenticator-app TOTP standards. He used Pay With Amazon successfully.
- EFF resumed accepting bitcoin through BitPay; it received 726 BTC (about 95,000 dollars) that the Bitcoin Faucet's Gavin Andresen returned from an earlier EFF donation.
- iPad app lets a locket emit an audio code to unlock a secret diary.
- A state employee health site told a user his password was "already in use" and displayed it, suggesting poor storage or database-key design; there is no reason to require unique passwords and doing so leaks information.
- Astrill VPN blocks GRC because ShieldsUP scans show the VPN server's open ports; Steve says a VPN user running ShieldsUP sees the exit server, not their own machine.
- Why HTTPS everywhere and HSTS rather than switching to HTTPS only at login: a man-in-the-middle on an HTTP page can strip the HTTPS links (SSL stripping); SEO is no reason to avoid SSL, and session caching makes TLS cheap.
- A multi-router home in China: ShieldsUP results depend on which router's WAN is tested; open Telnet and HTTP ports may belong to the ISP's NAT or to each router.
- Third-party cookies were never meant to exist; sites that break without them (job boards) could use the Referer header; Steve supports default blocking.
- A "quantum envelope" cannot work because routing observes the data and breaks the quantum protection.
- Pumped-storage hydro at Ludington on Lake Michigan stores energy with eight 24-foot pipes.
- BitTorrent Sync: a listener wants to lock and unlock the share; Steve says lock status would reveal that a share exists, the protocol spec is still awaited, and sensitive files should be pre-encrypted (for example in a TrueCrypt volume), a "pre-Internet encryption" approach.
- Adware browser plugins can hyperlink words on pages (seen in Firefox and Chrome); removing the plugin fixes it.
- Deutsche Telekom plans to throttle DSL users but exempt its own services; a German petition passed 50,000 signatures and EU commissioner Neelie Kroes proposed rules banning throttling and blocking. Steve notes traffic from outside an ISP costs it transit fees, but net neutrality is still needed.
- Contactless cards can be read at greater distances than designed, so skimming risk is real; Steve prefers contact chips.
