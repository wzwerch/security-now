# Summary

Steve frames the episode around the post-Snowden mood: widespread hand-wringing over possible cryptographic backdoors is misplaced because governments already have trusted root certificates and need no cracking. He cites a blogger's breakdown of Apple's iOS 8 root store, which contains 222 roots including five from the US Department of Defense, the Hong Kong Post Office and China's CNNIC, with many still SHA-1 signed and a few MD2 and MD5 signed. He notes Apple's claim that iOS 8 protects data so that it cannot be handed to authorities, promises a whitepaper review later, and explains the difference between today's encryption and true Trust No One. News items: CloudFlare's Keyless SSL, which lets it terminate TLS without holding a customer's private key (prompted by a bank's demand); the Google and Dropbox initiative Simply Secure; and a malvertising incident in which an ad from the Zedo network, served via Google's DoubleClick on sites such as Last.fm, delivered a drive-by Zemot downloader until Google blocked it. Steve thanks Android users for third-party keyboards now on iOS 8. Listener feedback covers archive-episode currency, the dead-battery bounce mystery, the 100 m Ethernet limit that settles the building-link debate, MasterCard's Canadian postal-code workaround at gas pumps, XP in a VM, whether encrypting everything protects privacy, HTTPS as a search-ranking signal, why a full format does not repair a disk, point-of-sale update policies and rising ECC rates on an SSD.

# Key points

- Apple's iOS 8 root store (222 roots): 138 use 2048-bit RSA, 44 use 4096-bit RSA, 12 use 384-bit elliptic curve; 149 are SHA-1 signed, 42 SHA-256, 17 SHA-384, one SHA-512, three MD2 and ten MD5. Roots include several governments (US DoD, China, Hong Kong Post Office, Japan, Netherlands, Taiwan, Turkey), so any of them can mint certificates for any site.
- Steve's point: agencies do not need to break crypto when they can issue certificates; true Trust No One means you do not trust any certificate authority and share a key out of band.
- Apple says iOS 8 data is encrypted so that Apple cannot hand it to the government, and Tim Cook positions this against Google's data-driven business.
- CloudFlare Keyless SSL lets the DDoS-protection provider stand in for a site's TLS without holding its private key, motivated by a large bank that must disclose any key loss to regulators.
- Google and Dropbox created Simply Secure to bring lab security to real users; Steve compares this gap to SQRL being plain standard crypto that is simply unused.
- Malvertising: a malicious ad passed through Zedo and DoubleClick, hit sites including Last.fm, and ran a script that downloaded the Zemot malware with no user action; up-to-date antimalware detected it. Steve notes how pages pull content from many third parties and that ads cannot be vetted by checksum.
- Spam harvesting tools guess common first names at mail domains; hMailServer can temporarily blacklist hosts that guess nonexistent recipients.
- Third-party keyboards on iOS 8 (Swype, SwiftKey) have a privacy warning; Steve prefers Swype.
- Archived episodes remain useful for fundamentals, but news-specific product advice ages; the hosts will not retroactively update every old recommendation.
- Dead batteries bounce because their electrolyte has dried out, unlike charged ones.
- Ethernet over twisted pair is limited to 100 m (328 ft), which settles why 400 ft between houses should use fiber.
- A MasterCard workaround for Canadian users is to convert a postal code to digits and pad with zeros for US pumps; zip codes are weak, guessable factors but better than none.
- Installing XP in a VM still needs an Internet connection for activation, so apply all patches. XP updates via the XP Embedded registry trick still worked.
- Kirill argues routine encryption of everything hides who is hiding something; Leo and Steve agree broad adoption helps but email is hard to encrypt, and Steve uses encrypted offsite archiving.
- Google may rank HTTPS sites higher as one signal of a serious site.
- A full format in the old sense checked sector headers and marked bad clusters; today it is impractically slow (days on 2 TB) and drives remap defects themselves.
- Many POS systems run unpatched XP Embedded because vendors forbade Windows Update after updates once broke applications; Microsoft's monthly patch schedule came from IT requests for predictability.
- A reader's SSD shows an increasing ECC correction rate even after a full erase and rewrite, which Steve reads as a warning sign; SpinRite exposes SMART data under load.
