# Summary

Steve opens with light news: a new marketplace menu in Firefox, a Podcast Awards nomination push, Vivaldi as a new Chromium-engine browser from Opera's cofounder, and Apple's OS X 10.10.2 and iOS 8.1.3 updates that fix issues disclosed by Google's Project Zero after its 90-day deadline, including the Thunderstrike firmware problem. He notes Tim Cook agreed to let China audit Apple products for backdoors. A longer segment explains how SQRL works for users: one client registers the sqrl:// scheme, a click or QR scan logs you in, and the identity can be transferred to phones, with websites adding it alongside passwords. In the Q&A, Steve recommends Bruce Schneier's "Applied Cryptography," the Elaine Farris transcription service, and explains color-laser printer tracking dots. Listeners press him on the multiple-key government access idea from episode 491, and he clarifies he meant only that extra keys do not weaken the math, and that a realistic law would simply force vendors to hold keys and respond to orders, as the Skipjack escrow design did. Other topics: blocking executables at proxies, whether renewing a certificate should include a new key pair, CryptoWall spreading through ad networks and Flash with Tor-based key servers, why Enigma would be trivial for modern computers, and the free Sophos/Astaro UTM firewall.

# Key points

- Project Zero starts a 90-day clock when it notifies a vendor and publishes afterwards; Steve sees it as part of Google's pattern of forcing security change (like penalizing SHA-1 early), though Google's own pre-4.4 Android is unpatched. The disclosed Apple bugs required local code execution and were fixed in OS X 10.10.2.
- Thunderstrike is called an "Evil Maid" attack (requires physical access); Steve dislikes the term.
- Apple agreed to cooperate with China's State Internet Information Office audits, with Tim Cook saying Apple provides no backdoor.
- Vivaldi is a Chromium (Blink)-based browser aimed at power users with keyboard shortcuts and tab features; Steve still uses Firefox with hundreds of tabs.
- SQRL user model: no browser extension needed, a client in the tray registers the sqrl scheme, a click logs you in, a confirmation dialog verifies the user, the identity can be exported by QR to mobile devices, and cross-device login lets a phone log in an untrusted kiosk PC without typing credentials. Adoption likely begins with sites that can add it next to passwords, such as Drupal and blog platforms.
- Printer tracking dots: color laser printers print nearly invisible yellow dots encoding the printer's serial number, which law enforcement uses to trace pages.
- Learning crypto programming: Steve recommends Schneier's "Applied Cryptography," still relevant because the field moves slowly.
- Key access follow-up: Steve repeats that extra recipient keys do not weaken ciphers, that his 196-governments objection (from a listener) is a policy problem, and predicts that legislation, if any, would just require vendors such as Apple to keep a ghost-recipient key and respond to specific court orders; Leo prefers a third-party escrow and doubts there is the political will. Skipjack and its key escrow are cited as precedent.
- Blocking executables at the proxy plus proxy-based antivirus (with TLS interception) is one listener's corporate policy; Steve found even a legitimate old SpinRite executable was blocked in transit to a customer.
- Renewing a certificate can reuse the existing key pair; Steve says low-value sites can do that, but high-value sites should rekey on renewal to retire the old public key.
- CryptoWall 2 spreads through ad networks including Flash ads and uses Tor-hosted servers for keys; Steve advises current backups and not running as an administrator.
- Enigma versus modern crypto: it was a polyalphabetic cipher that today's computers would break instantly, but it was good for its time; the bombe tested rotor positions against constraints and rejected impossible ones.
- Sophos UTM (formerly Astaro Security Gateway) is free for home use on an old PC with two network cards and offers deeper filtering than a NAT router; Leo's TWiT uses Astaro.

# Other segments

- Steve is back on Firefox 35 and plans to demo SQRL with Leo soon; the iPad sales decline and Apple earnings are mentioned briefly.
- Steve recommends the film "Predestination" (Heinlein story) and follows up on "Automata."
- Listener SpinRite story: an Oregon veterans' department employee has restored dozens of "dead" systems with it.
- A Ghost vulnerability (in glibc) published on Openwall is deferred to next week.
- Next episode planned: the Tor de-anonymization ("DeTor") deep dive.
