# Summary

The Spectre story continues: after Intel told partners to stop shipping its faulty variant 2 microcode, Microsoft released an out-of-band update (obtainable from the Update Catalog) that disables only the branch-target-injection mitigation, and also documented registry settings doing the same, which Steve's InSpectre already toggles; Steve updated InSpectre to release 6 with a 64-bit probe to work around a bug in the 32-bit ntdll of Windows 10 1703 and with inverted yes/no indicators, reaching about 355,000 downloads. He advises that with no known exploitation, anyone seeing instability should switch the mitigation off until sound microcode arrives. The news covers Google's Tavis Ormandy finding that Blizzard's game-client updater could let any website run code, followed by Blizzard first stonewalling him and then apologizing; Checkmarx showing Tinder sends photos unencrypted and leaks swipe actions via traffic patterns; the Strava global heat map exposing military sites; voting machine makers threatening eBay resellers ahead of DEF CON's Voting Village; the Coincheck exchange theft of about $532 million; Confiant's Zirconium malvertising operation and Coinhive miner ads on YouTube; the shrinking BSD ecosystem; and CrossRAT being detected by only two AV engines. New York, Montana and (pending) California use state purchasing power to enforce Net Neutrality. Steve answers criticism that he was unfair to Intel engineers by saying his complaint is Intel's vague communication.

# Key points

- Microsoft KB for disabling the Spectre variant 2 mitigation: not pushed automatically; covers Windows 7 SP1, 8.1 and Windows 10; Microsoft acknowledges instability can cause data loss. Apple avoided Intel's microcode and patched WebKit and recompiled code instead.
- InSpectre release 6 fixes false results on Windows 10 1703; Steve wrote his first 64-bit assembly code for a probe.
- Blizzard (World of Warcraft, Overwatch, Diablo III, StarCraft II): update/maintenance component lacked strong authentication so a malicious web page could cause arbitrary code to run; Blizzard's first fix was inadequate; lesson is to work with Project Zero researchers.
- Tinder iOS/Android: images sent over plain HTTP, other traffic encrypted but distinguishable by byte patterns; proof-of-concept app "TinderDrift" reconstructed a victim's session for people on the same Wi-Fi.
- Strava heat map aggregated fitness-tracker GPS data worldwide and revealed activity at military sites, even though individual users were not named; dark areas also carry information; militaries revised policies.
- Voting machines: manufacturers threaten resellers though resale and hacking are legal; last year no machine survived the Voting Village; Steve argues voting software should be open source and academically vetted.
- Coincheck (Tokyo) lost about $532 million in digital tokens, surpassing Mt. Gox; offline storage is the only real protection.
- Zirconium: 28 fake ad agencies, a billion malicious ad views in 2017, redirects to tech-support scams and fake Flash updates. Coinhive-based miner ads ran on YouTube for days despite Google's claim of a two-hour takedown.
- BSD decline: OpenBSD, FreeBSD, NetBSD suffer from too few eyes on the code; Steve expects Linux to be the lone winner.
- CrossRAT needs Java installed; only 2 of 55 engines detected it at the time, which Steve expects to improve; advice is to remove Java if not needed.
- Net Neutrality: governors of Montana and New York and California's Senate act through procurement rules.
