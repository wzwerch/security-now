# Summary

Steve reviews the week's hacks: the Syrian Electronic Army took over the Associated Press Twitter account with a fake White House bomb report that briefly crashed the stock market, then hit the Guardian's accounts, and Twitter began contacting news organizations about account security. LivingSocial lost a database of 50 million records with encrypted passwords but separately stored payment data. He quotes a Slate report that the FBI wants CALEA-style wiretap obligations with escalating fines for web firms, and that a Houston magistrate refused to let the FBI install a spy trojan on an unidentified suspect's computer, which revealed the tool's capabilities. PayPal added OAuth sign-in, and Steve points listeners to the EFF's "Who Has Your Back" ratings (Twitter and Sonic.net scoring best). Steve also notes OpenVPN for iOS, explains that time-based one-time passwords depend on correct clocks and that each service needs its own secret, and reports that Internet Explorer's extended validation display can be faked while Firefox and Chrome pin EV roots, so a green EV bar there rules out interception. The main topic is BitTorrent Sync, a new alpha peer-to-peer folder synchronizer using shared secrets, direct encrypted connections, LAN discovery, DHT or tracker lookup and relay as last resort; Steve finds the design sound but wants a protocol write-up.

# Key points

- The Syrian Electronic Army hijacked the Associated Press Twitter account and posted a false White House bomb claim; trading bots reacting to keywords caused a brief market drop. It then claimed 11 Guardian accounts.
- Steve argues many accounts were created when they did not matter and kept weak passwords; Twitter is working on multifactor login, presumably standards-based OATH.
- LivingSocial breach: about 50 million names, emails, birthdates and hashed passwords stolen; financial data stored separately and not taken; users were advised, not forced, to change passwords.
- FBI "going dark" push: proposal to extend CALEA and the Wiretap Act to web and chat providers with fines that double daily after 90 days unpaid; other countries' similar bills stalled. Silent Circle's CEO said he would shut down rather than add a backdoor.
- Houston Magistrate Judge Stephen Smith denied an FBI request to plant spyware on an unknown suspect's computer, which would have recorded webcam images, browsing, email, chats and passwords. Steve notes endpoint trojans bypass encryption in transit.
- PayPal now offers OAuth login; Steve is wary of phishing by look-alike login pages but likes fewer accounts. Leo mentions a phishing page at a look-alike Twitter domain.
- EFF "Who Has Your Back" report: Twitter and Sonic.net score six stars; MySpace and Verizon none.
- Time-based one-time passwords require accurate device clocks; sharing one authenticator secret across sites would recreate the password-reuse problem, which is why separate secrets are better than the old VeriSign central-token model.
- Extended validation: Firefox and Chrome check the EV root and certificate policy OID against built-in data, outside the normal PKI, so an interception proxy cannot produce a green bar; Internet Explorer lets organisations make EV show green via registry settings, making it meaningless. Bitdefender and Kaspersky intercept HTTPS and remove EV. GRC's fingerprint page now shows which sites should be EV.
- SpinRite on SSDs: Level 2 is gentle (writes only recovered data), Level 1 never writes; a listener revived an Intel X25-M with Level 1 because it triggers the drive's own sector relocation.
- BitTorrent Sync (alpha, free, not open source, no third party or cloud): sync of unlimited folders using a pseudorandom shared secret (about 21 bytes base-32 shown), with read-write, read-only, and 24-hour one-time secrets; traffic is encrypted with keys derived from the secret; blocks of 4 MB are synced; UPnP optional, bandwidth throttles, DHT or tracker discovery, LAN broadcast, relay when direct connection is impossible; runs on Windows, Mac, Linux and NAS platforms, mobile clients promised.
- Weaknesses noted: no key revocation short of re-keying, secret must be shared securely, details of key derivation not yet disclosed; Steve is in contact with the vendor for a protocol description.
