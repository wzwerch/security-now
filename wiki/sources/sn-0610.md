# Summary

The lead story is Intel's disclosure on May 1, 2017 of a critical remotely exploitable vulnerability in its Management Engine and Active Management Technology, a secret out-of-band subsystem present in business-class Intel chipsets since 2008 that cannot be disabled. Steve explains the Ring -3 concern, notes that exposure depends on vPro/AMT hardware, and recommends checking for firmware updates. Other items include University of Michigan research finding open listening ports in many Android apps, a Fitbit that disproved a murder suspect's timeline, Ghostscript vulnerabilities, SNI as another way ISPs can see destinations, and a fresh Edge same-origin bypass disclosed without a fix by researcher Manuel Caballero. He also covers Chrome 58 removing the certificate common-name fallback, the self-propagating Hajime botnet that secures devices instead of attacking, a proposed "textalyzer" for police use, a Karmen ransomware-as-a-service kit, the FCC's plan to reverse Title II Net Neutrality, Cloudflare's IoT proxy service Orbit, and the continuing Symantec certificate dispute.

# Key points

- Intel Management Engine: a separate ARC processor in the chipset runs always-on firmware with direct memory and network access that bypasses OS firewalls (Ring -3). Versions from roughly 6 through 11.6 were affected, and Intel called the AMT flaw critical and remotely exploitable and issued firmware fixes the day before. Leo notes it requires vPro-class hardware, and mitigations include using a secondary NIC. Steve points to the Intel ME verification utility and says BIOS updates do not install themselves; a SemiAccurate writer had long warned Intel. Leo suggests this is an opening for AMD and coreboot-friendly designs.
- Android open ports (University of Michigan, "Open Doors for Bob and Mallory"): a static analyzer over 100,000 apps found nearly half of open-port usage unprotected, 410 vulnerable apps and 956 potential exploits, allowing theft of contacts, photos and credentials from malware on the device, a LAN attacker, or web scripts. Steve advises turning off file-sharing style apps when not in use and port-scanning your own phone.
- A Fitbit's timestamps contradicted a husband's staged home-invasion story in a murder case, another example of IoT evidence.
- Ghostscript, used for PostScript and PDF rendering, had memory safety flaws (use after free, divide by zero, null dereferences, command parameter handling) found by Kamil Frankowicz, affecting Ubuntu releases.
- Server Name Indication is sent in clear in the TLS client hello, so ISPs can see which host you visit; only a VPN hides it.
- Edge same-origin-policy bypass via domainless pages such as about:blank was published with proof of concept and no patch, allowing theft of cookies and passwords for other sites, including through malvertising. Steve suggests avoiding Edge until fixed.
- Chrome 58 stops falling back to the certificate common name and requires the Subject Alternative Name; old self-signed certificates lacking it will fail, and Firefox is following.
- Hajime is a rapidly growing (about 300,000 devices) peer-to-peer botnet using BitTorrent DHT and signed, RC4-encrypted control, which closes Telnet and other ports on infected IoT devices and shows a "whitehat" message but performs no attacks; Steve speculates a state actor and notes it does not persist after reboot.
- A "textalyzer" from Cellebrite is proposed in New York to show a driver's recent phone activity after crashes.
- Karmen is a $175 ransomware-as-a-service kit sold in Russian-language forums by DevBitox.
- FCC chairman Ajit Pai proposed reversing the 2015 Title II classification for Net Neutrality, with a vote on May 18; Steve urges listeners to comment. Cloudflare Orbit places a proxy in front of IoT devices to apply virtual patches and mutual TLS, protecting over 120 million devices.
- Mozilla and Google continued negotiating consequences with Symantec.
- Errata and feedback: Unicode skin-tone modifiers, EternalBlue came from the NSA-linked Equation Group (not Vault 7), cookie settings differ between sending and storing, DMZ hosts can expose SMB, password vault master passphrases built from song or poem initials plus numbers, and Chrome can restrict location access.
