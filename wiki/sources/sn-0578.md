# Summary

Recorded with guest co-host Fr. Robert Ballecer while Leo is on vacation. The first topic is the pending amendment to US Federal Rule 41, which lets a single warrant authorise remote searches of many computers regardless of judicial district, created via a rules process rather than legislation and due to take effect on 1 December unless Congress acts; Steve sees the case for botnet takedowns but dislikes the lack of oversight and suggests ISP service termination and anti-spoofing as alternatives. The US contract letting ICANN run IANA functions will lapse on 30 September, which Steve supports as it counters authoritarian arguments. ICANN will generate a new, 2048-bit DNSSEC root signing key, and Steve is bullish on DNSSEC and DANE as an alternative to the CA system. Google announces that from January 2017 Chrome 56 will label HTTP pages that collect passwords or credit cards as not secure, as a step towards labelling all HTTP. A researcher successfully mirrored the iPhone 5c NAND flash to bypass passcode retry limits, uncovering Apple's obfuscated bus traffic. Steve closes with the story of an XSS bug on GRC reported through OpenBugBounty, how he chose Tinfoil Security over Acunetix and Beyond Security, what the scanner found, and the fix.

# Key points

- Rule 41 amendments: allow warrants for remote access to computers whose location is concealed, and for victim machines in botnets across many districts; critics (including Sen. Wyden and Wired) say a rules process bypassed Congress; Steve recalls an earlier era when cleaning infected machines remotely was illegal; notification of owners is only a best-effort requirement.
- Steve suggests service termination of misbehaving machines and ISP egress filtering against address spoofing as less invasive.
- IANA transition: IANA manages protocol and number registries and DNS root functions; ICANN, already multinational, takes over after the NTIA contract ends; major tech companies support it; Fr. Robert notes transparency criticisms of ICANN.
- DNSSEC root key rollover: first-ever change, key size to double from 1024 to 2048 bits, generated in an October ceremony; the new key appears in DNS in July 2017 and is used for signing in October 2017; DNSSEC with DANE could replace certificate authorities for server keys.
- Chrome: labels HTTP forms with passwords or cards as not secure from January 2017, later all HTTP, with more than half of desktop page loads already HTTPS; Let's Encrypt makes the cost argument weaker.
- iPhone 5c NAND mirroring: desoldered the flash chip, reverse-engineered its bus (including hidden address mappings and very short bursts Apple used to obscure traffic), and cloned it so six failed passcode attempts could be repeated; a four-digit code could fall in about 20 hours.
- Steve reminds listeners of GRC's DNS Benchmark; recommends running your own or a fast DNS; points to Afraid.org for free dynamic DNS; Fr. Robert warns against exposing an entire host via DMZ.
- Public service tip: fill in emergency contact information in the iOS Health app so responders can reach family from a locked phone.
- SpinRite anecdotes: a stuck Windows 10 update fixed after a SpinRite run; SSD maintenance value.
- GRC XSS incident: a white-hat reported a cross-site scripting hole through OpenBugBounty; it was in the SpinRite sales-support page that echoed a not-found transaction ID without escaping; the fix escapes dangerous characters on all output and adds Content Security Policy.
- Scanner comparison: Acunetix and Beyond Security disappointed (one withheld findings behind payment, the other reported an A+); Tinfoil Security found 64 items including missing subresource integrity, insecure and non-HttpOnly cookies, exposed robots.txt, private IPs and email addresses in transcripts, and unfiltered user-tag echoing in a bundled NNTP web front end that Steve locked down with strict Content Security Policy. Steve now recommends Tinfoil Security.
