# Summary

Steve notes that Microsoft postponed February's Patch Tuesday over a last-minute issue, which he suspects relates to the SMB client crash flaw disclosed earlier. uBlock Origin has arrived for Safari on macOS, and Steve recounts a university attacked by its own IoT devices: thousands of vending machines and lamp posts on an isolated network were infected by a botnet that brute-forced default passwords, and a DNS flood from them disrupted the whole campus until staff captured the malware's cleartext password changes and used them to reset the devices. Other items: PHP voting to include libsodium in version 7.2, the Linux takeover.sh tool, the spread of fileless in-memory malware against banks, using AES Crypt to send sensitive tax documents, the point that numeric PIN pads are also vulnerable to visual-motion attacks, fires at a Samsung battery recycling site, and pfSense UPnP access control lists. The main segment covers a big multi-institution study of TLS interception, which found that roughly 4 to 11 percent of observed connections were intercepted by middleboxes or antivirus and most of those were less secure as a result; Steve concludes that third-party antivirus HTTPS interception is a poor trade and suggests browsers should tell users when a non-public root is in use.

# Key points

- Delayed Patch Tuesday: Microsoft cited a last-minute flaw; Ars speculated about the SMB client bug (a Python proof-of-concept had been published after Microsoft missed the 90-day deadline), which could crash Windows 8.1, 10 and recent servers and perhaps lead to kernel code execution.
- uBlock Origin on Safari: now available; Steve recounts that the extension is a wide-spectrum blocker rather than just an ad blocker, relies on community filter lists and shares most code across browsers; Firefox also supports script-tag filtering.
- University IoT outbreak (from a Verizon report): over 5,000 vending machines and similar devices on an IoT segment queried odd seafood-related subdomains, overloading campus DNS because the IoT network used the main DNS servers. The malware changed device passwords; responders captured the cleartext control password with a packet capture and scripted a mass reset. Lessons: separate IoT network zones, avoid direct internet access, change defaults, monitor logs, inventory IoT devices. Steve again endorses the Ubiquiti EdgeRouter X for isolation.
- PHP 7.2 will include libsodium in the core, voted unanimously; Steve praises its one-right-way design.
- takeover.sh: an experimental script that replaces a running Linux system's root filesystem from memory over SSH without rebooting; for experts only.
- Fileless malware: Kaspersky reported in-memory attacks, using PowerShell, Metasploit and Mimikatz, against at least 140 banks and enterprises in 40 countries, including ATM infrastructure; such malware survives on devices that rarely reboot.
- Tax documents: encrypt with AES Crypt (free, open source, cross-platform) before sending, share the key by a different channel, delete afterward; Steve corrects his earlier mention of AxCrypt, which moved to a subscription. Leo argues for PGP/GPG; Steve thinks PGP's adoption hurdle is too high.
- Numeric keypad entry on phones is as susceptible to the video-based motion attack as pattern locks.
- A Samsung Note 7 battery recycling site caught fire; Leo relays that recycling plants often suffer lithium-ion fires.
- pfSense's UPnP access control lists can restrict which LAN devices may open ports.
- TLS interception study (University of Michigan, Illinois, Mozilla, Cloudflare, Google, Berkeley, ICSI): servers can spot interception because the TLS handshake does not match the browser's user-agent. Across nearly eight billion handshakes, interception rates were about 4 percent for Firefox update servers, 6.2 percent for ecommerce sites and 10.9 percent for US Cloudflare traffic, roughly ten times earlier estimates. Of intercepted connections, 97, 32 and 54 percent respectively had weaker security, and 10 to 40 percent advertised broken ciphers. Eleven of 12 corporate middleboxes had unsafe defaults and 24 of 26 client antivirus products reduced security. The authors urged community consensus and antivirus vendors to reconsider interception; Steve would remove third-party AV in favour of Windows Defender and wants browsers to flag non-public trust anchors.
