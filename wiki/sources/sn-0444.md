# Summary

A pure-news episode named after the single duplicated line "goto fail" that broke certificate checking in Apple's TLS code. Steve first notes that Belkin quietly fixed the WeMo flaws discussed the week before. He then walks through Apple's bug: in the routine that verifies a server's signed key exchange, a doubled goto statement made the function skip the real check and return success, so a forged certificate was accepted in iOS 7.0.5 and earlier (and 6), and in OS X until 10.9.2; Chrome and Firefox on the Mac were unaffected because they use NSS. He and Leo discuss missing regression tests, C's pitfalls and the unproven conspiracy theories. Next, FireEye showed a proof of concept in which an app approved for the App Store could monitor taps and button presses across apps in the background. Facebook's purchase of WhatsApp sent people to alternatives, and Steve takes apart Telegram's homegrown cryptography, its non-end-to-end default chats and its bounty contest, recommending Threema or Moxie Marlinspike's TextSecure instead. Further segments explain the Netflix, Comcast, Verizon and Cogent peering disputes and Netflix's Open Connect cache program, Mt. Gox's shutdown after claiming hundreds of thousands of lost bitcoin, a study showing most critical 2013 Microsoft vulnerabilities would be blunted by running as a standard user, and malvertising on YouTube. Steve's advice is to get off administrator accounts (XP has 41 days left), keep bitcoin off exchanges, and prefer simple, standard end-to-end designs.

# Key points

- Belkin fixed the WeMo issues (signing key removed from firmware, certificate checks, other bugs) with a January firmware update, though it never announced it to researchers.
- Goto fail: Apple's SSLVerifySignedServerKeyExchange returned success because a duplicated goto line jumped to the cleanup label with a no-error status, so signature checks were bypassed; Apple fixed iOS (7.0.6) first and OS X 10.9.2 days later without highlighting it. A test site (gotofail.com) let users check Safari. Steve argues a basic negative test would have caught it.
- Speculation: the flaw appeared in iOS 6, and a leaked PRISM slide listed Apple as added around the same time; Steve and Leo treat the link as unproven. Leo recalls the 2003 Linux kernel backdoor attempt using an assignment in place of a comparison.
- FireEye demonstrated background keystroke and touch monitoring across iOS 7 apps through an app that passed Apple's review; Apple was informed.
- WhatsApp's acquisition by Facebook drove signups at Threema (user base doubled) and Telegram (millions of downloads).
- Telegram critique: custom protocol using SHA-1 over plaintext, MAC-then-encrypt style design, the obscure AES-IGE mode, key derivation invented in-house; default chats are encrypted only to Telegram's servers, only optional secret chats are end-to-end; a flaw in the secret-chat key exchange was found and patched. Moxie Marlinspike and Taylor Hornby argued the 200,000 dollar cracking contest proves nothing. Steve's verdict: avoid.
- Steve's description of sound end-to-end messaging: exchange public keys verified in person (Threema's three-dot trust), random symmetric key per message, authenticated encryption, and for groups wrap the message key to each member's public key.
- Peering dispute: Netflix, a Cogent customer, accounted for about 32 percent of evening downstream traffic in North America; Comcast and Verizon wanted payment or refused to take Netflix's free Open Connect cache servers, congesting peering points and degrading streams. Leo notes Netflix reported falling throughput to Comcast and wonders whether the dispute relates to net neutrality.
- Mt. Gox halted trading and a leaked document claimed 744,408 bitcoin were lost to long-running theft; the price fell below 500 dollars. Steve advises holding bitcoin offline rather than on exchanges.
- Avecto's analysis of 2013 Microsoft bulletins: 92 percent of critical vulnerabilities, 100 percent of those affecting Internet Explorer, and 100 percent of critical remote-code-execution ones would be mitigated by removing admin rights. Steve urges XP users to use a standard account.
- YouTube in-stream ads redirected users to the Styx exploit kit using an old Java flaw to install the Caphaw banking trojan; Google investigated.

# Other segments

- SpinRite: a listener cloned a failing laptop drive after a Level 2 run, and also reports it cleared slow sector reads on an SSD.
- SQRL: Steve finished the user-interface walkthrough for creating a new identity; the UI work forced technology changes.
- Recommendations: the Steve Jobs "Lost Interview" and the iPad train-routing game Rails.
- Steve mentions a pistachio cholesterol experiment (promised for next week).
