# Summary

Steve reports that newly published Snowden documents about an NSA tool called QWERTY match code found in Regin, according to Kaspersky researchers, which suggests Regin is a Five Eyes tool and not Russian as had been assumed. He then explains the GHOST vulnerability found by Qualys in the glibc gethostbyname function: a small heap overflow present since glibc 2.2 in 2000, fixed unknowingly in 2013, that was nonetheless turned into a full remote exploit against the Exim mail server. A listener asks what happens to a SQRL user if a site is breached; Steve says a stolen public key is useless because login requires signing a fresh server challenge with the private key, so sites hold no secrets. He also notes a listener's SSD was repaired with SpinRite Level 2, since SSDs also depend on error correction. The main segment revisits Tor, first covered in 2008: its onion-routing cryptography is sound, but it assumes a limited adversary and does not defend against traffic confirmation. Academic work, including a paper claiming 81 percent of users can be de-anonymized using router NetFlow data when traffic is deliberately perturbed, and the FBI's apparent unmasking of Silk Road 2 servers, show that a nation-state-scale observer can correlate traffic timing and volume. Steve concludes Tor is useful only as one layer, that illegal activity is unwise, and that real anonymity today needs real-world measures like paying cash and working far from home.

# Key points

- Regin and QWERTY: Kaspersky found identical code in Regin and the NSA's QWERTY keylogging module shown in Der Spiegel's documents, tying Regin to the Five Eyes (UKUSA) alliance; earlier attribution to Russia rested on target selection.
- GHOST: a glibc 2.2 to 2.17 heap overflow in gethostbyname that can overwrite only a pointer-sized value with digits and dots, yet Qualys demonstrated remote code execution against Exim defeating ASLR and no-execute protections; vendors released patches before disclosure, but old packages remain exposed.
- SQRL and breaches: site databases hold only public keys, so a breach does not enable impersonation; the protocol is a challenge-response, not a stored secret.
- SSDs also operate on the edge of error correction as density grows; a Level 2 SpinRite pass is non-destructive maintenance for them.
- Tor design: a client picks a three-node circuit, negotiates keys with each node and wraps data in layered encryption so each relay knows only its neighbors; traffic is re-chunked into fixed 512-byte cells, padded and queued round-robin, and there are about 5,000 relays. The cryptography has not been successfully attacked.
- Weak point: Tor is low latency and designed against a partial, non-global adversary that cannot observe or manipulate much of the network; it does not defend against traffic confirmation. Statistical analysis of packet timing and volume narrows candidates, and active manipulation (delaying or dropping bursts) can confirm a pairing quickly.
- The cited NetFlow paper claims 81 percent of users can be de-anonymized with router flow records; Steve considers its method soft but its references to scores of earlier attacks decisive.
- A real-world example: FBI court filings said an unidentified source supplied IP addresses for Silk Road 2 hidden services and visitors to its vendor site, leading to identification of the infrastructure and an administrator, and to other Tor black markets. Other techniques such as persistent cookies or Flash may also have been used.
- Steve's advice: Tor is fine as a layer but not a guarantee; avoid anything you would not want the government to know; for real anonymity combine Tor with old-school tactics (distant location, cash, a clean laptop with a changed MAC address, no repeat visits, more than three nodes spread across regions).
- The deeper issue is that the Internet was never designed for anonymity; high-latency services such as email can do better than interactive ones.
