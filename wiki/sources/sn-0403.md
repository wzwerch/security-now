# Summary

The Q&A episode starts with Steve deflating a widely tweeted claim that a government lab had run a quantum Internet for two years: the system is really a hub using a quantum link only to deliver one-time pads, and a compromised hub defeats it. He then covers an unpatched Internet Explorer 8 zero-day that was being used in watering-hole attacks against nuclear-research and defense-industry workers to drop the Poison Ivy trojan and was already in Metasploit. British Telecom joined other ISPs in trialing Carrier Grade NAT because of IPv4 exhaustion, with up to ten customers sharing one address, and Syria briefly dropped off the Internet. Steve previews a future episode on Ron Rivest's "honeywords" idea. GRC went 100 percent HTTPS and added a one-year Strict-Transport-Security header, and Steve asked Google to preload GRC in Chrome. He also introduces a new SpinRite S.M.A.R.T. explainer page. Listener questions cover the value of stealthed ports, SSL Labs grades for the wrong hostname, DMA risk in Thunderbolt and Firewire versus USB 3.0, running an OpenVPN server on a Tomato router for mobile use, whether Carrier Grade NAT improves privacy, whether VPN exit points concentrate risk, the age of routers, Butterfly Labs and bitcoin mining difficulty, odd ShieldsUP results from ICMP rate limiting, reuse of hardware authenticators, and the collision odds of BitTorrent Sync's 168-bit secrets.

# Key points

- The "quantum Internet" story: quantum links cannot be routed without breaking their security, so the demo used a hub; the quantum channel only distributes one-time pads that encrypt return traffic over ordinary links, which makes the hub a single point of total compromise. Steve considers it a lab curiosity.
- IE8-only zero-day: exploited via watering-hole sites to install Poison Ivy against nuclear research and aerospace/defense staff; Microsoft confirmed it and advised against IE8, and a Metasploit module appeared before a patch; many XP users are stuck on IE8.
- BT/PlusNet Carrier Grade NAT trial shares one address among up to ten users, which breaks hosting servers, hurts gaming and dynamic DNS. Steve says ISPs must keep extra mapping records, so law-enforcement and copyright requests can still be answered, but anonymity among users increases.
- Syria was cut off from the Internet for about 19.5 hours; the government blamed a cable fault.
- Honeywords (Ron Rivest): plant fake passwords alongside real ones so use of a cracked decoy reveals database theft; Steve will devote an episode to it.
- GRC moved to HTTPS only and sends Strict-Transport-Security for a year, so browsers upgrade connections automatically; he requested inclusion in Chrome's preload list (shared with Firefox), and noted LastPass is already listed. He left media.grc.com off HSTS because Podtrac redirects only support HTTP. TLS session caching makes HTTPS cheap, but for non-security sites there is little benefit.
- Stealth ports: being stealthed costs nothing, and IPv6 scanning will rely on allocation maps and leaked addresses; ignore unsolicited probes.
- SSL Labs grading: test the real online-banking hostname, not the marketing front page, because a failing SSLv2 server on one site may not be the banking system. Many banks quickly fixed poor grades after listeners complained.
- Thunderbolt and Firewire are DMA interfaces giving attached devices potential access to system memory; USB 3.0 is preferable for a security-conscious server.
- An OpenVPN server on a Tomato router, listening on common web ports such as 80 or 443, lets a phone with the OpenVPN iOS client tunnel home and get a home address.
- VPN service exit points concentrate traffic and trust, like Tor exit nodes; proXPN follows the laws of the server country. A home VPN keeps traffic under your own control but a nearby commercial server gives better performance for travelers.
- Old routers are fine if they do what you need; modern devices with UPnP are often the problem; an old PC running pfSense is also reasonable.
- Butterfly Labs: a listener explains that bitcoin mining difficulty adjusts every 2016 blocks so profit estimates for new ASIC miners will shrink as the network hash rate grows, and ASICs will push out GPU mining.
- ShieldsUP showing a diagonal pattern of closed ports on a FRITZ!Box results from ICMP rate limiting dropping the router's "port unreachable" replies.
- Authenticators: Blizzard-style hardware tokens use a vendor-held key, whereas Google Authenticator exposes a per-site secret as a QR code that can be cloned to several phones; secrets must never be reused between sites. Steve expects public-key based authentication to follow.
- BitTorrent Sync secrets are about 168 bits, about 3.7 x 10^50 possibilities, so guessing or accidental collision is negligible, provided the random generator is good.
