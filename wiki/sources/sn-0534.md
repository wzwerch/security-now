# Summary

The episode opens with a joke MIT study on tinfoil hats and then covers revelations that Carnegie Mellon researchers had been paid by the FBI to run a months-long deanonymization attack on Tor hidden services, used in Silk Road 2 and other prosecutions; Tor and cryptographer Matthew Green object that this bypassed ethical review and warrants and hit innocent users. A French and German team automatically analysed nearly 2,000 firmware images from 54 vendors and found critical flaws in 185 images. Police body cameras sold by one vendor were found preinstalled with the Conficker worm, raising chain-of-custody issues, and researchers decrypted metadata in WhatsApp's new calling feature. Steve recommends PC Engines hardware for pfSense, shares iPad Pro impressions, a puzzle game and a TV recommendation, and a SpinRite story. The second half responds to media coverage after the Paris attacks: officials offered no evidence encryption was used, and Steve repeats that strong encryption is just math, cannot be recalled, and that backdoor mandates would weaken everyone's security without stopping determined criminals, who can use steganography or other tools.

# Key points

- Tor/Carnegie Mellon: the Tor Project said researchers were paid to attack hidden services indiscriminately, apparently without a warrant or ethics board review, a traffic confirmation attack run for months; Matthew Green argues computer security research needs ethics oversight like medical research. A planned conference talk was quietly pulled.
- Firmware study: about 1,925 firmware images from 54 vendors emulated in a cloud lab with static and dynamic analysis and exploit tools; 185 critical vulnerabilities in images from roughly a quarter of the vendors, mostly in embedded web interfaces; vendors were notified under responsible disclosure. Steve sees promise for scalable pre-release testing.
- Police body camera: iPower Technologies found newly bought Vid-Shield cameras carrying Conficker.B; only 40 of 54 antivirus engines detected it on VirusTotal. Ars commentary: infected cameras could undermine evidence and police networks. Conficker background: 2008 worm with domain generation algorithm, up to 15 million machines.
- WhatsApp: researchers decrypted traffic of the calling feature to recover metadata (phone numbers, server IPs, codec, duration) though not content keys; Steve warns that encrypted does not mean secure.
- Hardware: PC Engines multi-NIC boards are a cheaper platform than Soekris for pfSense gateways.
- Paris coverage: major outlets stated or implied terrorists used encryption although the New York Times quoted officials admitting no evidence. Steve cites Matt Blaze (metadata and compromised endpoints remain visible) and Bruce Schneier pointing to Glenn Greenwald's rebuttal of blaming Snowden.
- Steve's argument: encryption has no raw materials to track; forced weaknesses in mainstream apps would only push bad actors to unlisted strong tools or steganography; forward secrecy is incompatible with later key escrow; convenient messengers like iMessage rely on provider-managed keys, whereas Threema requires out-of-band key exchange.
- SpinRite: a listener's PC slowdown after power glitches cleared after a long Level 2 scan where the drive finally rewrote a mis-written sector.
- Steve notes he abandoned his CryptoLink commercial product earlier partly over fears encryption could be outlawed.

# Other segments

- iPad Pro first-week impressions: fast and loud but too large, poor use of screen space, short battery at high brightness; waiting for the Pencil.
- Obituary mention of computer pioneer Gene Amdahl and an amusing anecdote of a child calling a floppy disk the Save icon.
- Puzzle game recommendation (Auralux), the film Spectre breaking explosion records, praise for the TV series The Good Wife and Fargo.
- Leo's PiDP-8 kit order mix-up.
