# Summary

Steve opens with a law that took effect the day before in the United Kingdom: the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure (PSTI) Act, which bans universal default passwords on consumer smart devices and imposes further baseline security duties on manufacturers, importers and retailers, with fines of up to 10 million pounds or 4% of worldwide revenue. He walks through the underlying ETSI baseline requirements for consumer IoT, stressing that almost every provision is mandatory, and quotes at length from the passwords and software-update sections. He briefly defers coverage of Google's change of plan on third-party cookies in Chrome to the next week, then answers listener feedback about race conditions, ownership and deadlocks in multithreaded code, and follows up on General Motors enrolling millions of drivers in a driving-data programme. After a science-fiction book plug and word that GRC's email system is being brought up, the main topic is a blog post by William Brown, author of the Rust WebAuthn library webauthn-rs, who argues Passkeys have been enshittified by platform vendors and will fail with ordinary consumers. Steve agrees with most of the criticism, notes he himself has not adopted a single Passkey, and recommends keeping credentials in a cross-platform password manager such as Bitwarden, which has just begun beta-testing Passkey support.

# Key points

- UK PSTI Act took effect 29 April 2024: manufacturers of consumer smart devices must not ship default passwords, must provide a security-contact point for vulnerability reports, and must state how long security updates will be provided; importers and retailers are also covered.
- Penalties are criminal-grade: up to 10 million pounds or 4% of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is greater. The law was about five years in the making.
- Scope covers anything consumer-grade that connects to the Internet or a home network: speakers, TVs, doorbells, baby monitors, cameras, phones, consoles, wearables, bulbs, plugs, kettles, fridges and similar.
- The law is based on the ETSI "Cyber Security for Consumer IoT: Baseline Requirements" (a 34-page document); topics include unique or user-set passwords, vulnerability reporting, updates, secure storage, secure communication, minimised attack surface, software integrity, data protection, resilience, telemetry, easy data deletion and input validation.
- Most provisions are tagged mandatory; "constrained devices" (battery sensors, window contacts) get limited exemptions but no blanket pass.
- Password provisions require per-device or user-defined credentials, sufficiently random pre-installed values (no counters, nothing derived from MAC address or SSID), brute-force and credential-stuffing throttling on non-constrained devices.
- Update provisions require securely updateable software, authenticated update channels with anti-rollback, simple updates (preferably automatic and on by default), recovery mechanisms such as dual-bank flash, and timely fixes for critical flaws.
- Contrast drawn with the US FCC's voluntary "Cyber Trust Mark" label, which Steve considers unlikely to change manufacturer behaviour.
- Google's Chrome third-party cookie deprecation will not happen on the original timetable; Steve promises detail next week, noting UK regulators' concerns seem to be about whether Google's replacement goes far enough.
- Listener feedback on multithreading: shared-object ownership works only by cooperation, a thread that dies while holding ownership can stall others, deadlocks arise when two threads each hold what the other needs, and timeouts or structured exception handling are mitigations.
- The New York Times reported General Motors enrolled millions of customers into its OnStar Smart Driver+ programme without clear consent, with unenrolment requiring a call to support.
- William Brown's blog post "Passkeys: A Shattered Dream" describes his partner losing a Passkey after Apple Keychain deleted it, and argues that Chrome's veto power over the WebAuthn spec (for example the dropped authenticator-selection extension), vendor lock-in, QR-code-centric UI, tiny resident-key capacity on hardware keys and unreliable platform stores have undermined the standard.
- Brown's advice: use a password manager you control (Bitwarden or self-hosted Vaultwarden), store Passkeys there if at all, and use hardware keys only to protect the password manager and email; his Kanidm identity project is looking at device certificates and smartcards.
- Steve's view: the underlying public-key cryptography is sound, but opaque, non-exportable, platform-bound implementations are a lock-in play; he prefers long unique passwords plus TOTP, which he can see and control. Bitwarden's Passkey beta for iPhone and Android (announced 10 April) is encouraging.
