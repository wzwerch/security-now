# Summary

A quieter week before Black Hat and DEF CON. Steve Gibson promotes a Humble Bundle of security ebooks from Wiley, then discusses the Net Neutrality fight, where tech companies and ISPs submitted opposing comments to the FCC; Steve favors Congress settling the matter instead of agency or executive reversals. MySpace account recovery turned out to need only a username, birth date and one other detail, and a researcher disclosed it after 90 days of silence. Steve explains why websites that block pasting into password fields (supposedly to stop brute forcing) only hurt security. Australia's prime minister claimed national law outranks mathematics when asked how encryption mandates could work. Other items: pacemaker data admitted in an Ohio arson case, India's 40-bit encryption licensing language, Steve's speculation about how a future lawful-interception model could rely on a trusted certificate-issuing intermediary, an Enigma machine found at a flea market, ads appearing on the HTC keyboard, and details of SQRL's self-updater, which verifies the Authenticode signer.

# Key points

- Humble Bundle "cybersecurity Wiley" offers 14 security ebooks (Social Engineering, Web Application Hacker's Handbook, Applied Cryptography, Secrets and Lies, and others) in pay-what-you-want tiers, supporting charity.
- Net Neutrality: Internet Association members (Alphabet, Facebook, Amazon, Microsoft, Netflix and others) urged the FCC to keep the 2015 rules; AT&T, Comcast, Charter and Verizon asked for repeal; 8.4 million comments filed with doubts about bot noise. Leo clarifies the rule rests on FCC Title II reclassification, not an executive order; both agree Congress should decide.
- MySpace: the recovery form for lost-email users accepts as few as three correct fields (username, name, birth date) to hand over an account; Motherboard confirmed with volunteers' consent; advice is to delete old MySpace accounts ("zombie data").
- Windows 10 Fall Creators Update adds an "I forgot my password" option on the lock screen, guided through Cortana and secondary verification.
- Paste-blocking and form-fill-blocking on password fields (and the Cobra Effect analogy from Troy Hunt) is counterproductive: attackers post directly to the server, while users of password managers are harmed.
- Australia: planned law modeled on the UK Investigatory Powers Act would require warranted help with encrypted messages; Turnbull said Australian law prevails over the laws of mathematics and denied it meant a backdoor. Steve notes perfect forward secrecy is designed to defeat retroactive decryption.
- Ohio arson case: the judge allowed pacemaker data (obtained by search warrant) as evidence; EFF warns about medical-data privacy erosion.
- India's DoT licensing text restricts ISP customers to 40-bit symmetric encryption without key deposit; Steve notes 40-bit is trivially breakable and TLS downgrade attacks are why it was removed.
- Steve's speculative thinking: an ISP or law-enforcement hub could be given a trusted CA-capable intermediate certificate for warranted interception (similar to Google's), though he dislikes the idea and notes Chrome's pinning would alarm.
- A cryptography professor bought a German Enigma machine at a flea market for 100 euros and sold it for 45,000 euros.
- Ads appeared on the HTC 10 keyboard (HTC later called it a mistake).
- SQRL update: integrated installer/updater in a roughly 287 KB executable; the updater checks TLS, the GRC download location, and that the Authenticode certificate is issued to Gibson Research Corporation by DigiCert (pinning issuer and subject instead of the fingerprint).
- SpinRite roadmap: 6.1 with direct AHCI access and 32 MB buffers (about half a terabyte per hour), 6.2 for USB controllers, 7 for file-level awareness; SpinRite runs the same on blank or full drives.
- Listener Q&A: DNSthingy praised; SMS 2FA still beats none; Apple patched the Broadcom WiFi flaw in iOS 10.3.1; skepticism about all-in-one smart card crowdfunding; Steve argues DRM like Macrovision, DVD CSS and HDCP burdens legitimate users and fails against pirates, relating to the EME decision.

- Errata: SpinRite is 30 years old, not 40.
