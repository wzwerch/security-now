# Summary

Steve first covers CISA and FBI's third "Secure by Design" alert, aimed at SOHO router makers: automate updates, remove web management from the WAN side, and require a physical manual action before security settings can be lowered, which Steve calls brilliant. Qualys disclosed four glibc flaws, headlined by CVE-2023-6246, a heap overflow in syslog handling that gives local users root on Debian, Ubuntu and Fedora releases, plus a qsort memory-corruption bug dating back to 1992; Steve walks through the coordinated disclosure timeline as a model of how it should work. CDN Fastly is replacing OpenSSL with Google's BoringSSL to cut the volume and operational cost of CVEs, which leads Steve to reconsider using the heavy GitLab for SpinRite issue tracking. Other news: Roskomnadzor's hosting-provider registration law (no foreign provider registered), Google's passkey-upgrade promotion based on an open /.well-known endpoint, and post-quantum Kyber key exchange in Firefox Nightly. Listener feedback covers signing cameras, app storage bloat, and a detailed explanation of why PLCs end up on the Internet. The main topic, "unforeseen consequences", is Steve's reading of a Financial Times story: as Google removes third-party cookies, publishers and advertisers (Amazon with Reach) will push mandatory registrations to obtain first-party identity data, which could be worse for privacy than cookies.

# Key points

- CISA/FBI Secure by Design alert for SOHO routers: automatic updates, no WAN-side web management, and manual physical override (for example a hardware "enable configuration changes" button) to remove security settings. Prompted by Chinese Volt Typhoon abuse of routers.
- glibc (Qualys TRU): CVE-2023-6246 heap overflow in _vsyslog_internal() introduced in glibc 2.37 (August 2022) and backported to 2.36; local privilege escalation to root. Two further minor syslog flaws and a qsort() bounds-check problem affecting all versions since 1992. Disclosure timeline ran November 2023 to 30 January 2024, delayed past the holidays at glibc's request. Only local attackers are affected.
- Fastly moves its edge TLS termination from OpenSSL to BoringSSL: smaller (under half the code), more modern, extensively fuzzed, similar performance; OpenSSL remains the experimentation platform. Steve notes AWS also runs its own small TLS stack.
- Russia: 266 hosting providers registered with Roskomnadzor, all domestic; foreign providers host about one third of Russian sites and may be cut off.
- Google Password Manager passkey upgrades on Pixel with launch partners; the underlying open mechanism is a passkey-endpoints JSON file under /.well-known/ with enrol and manage URLs, usable by any password manager (Steve nudges Bitwarden).
- Firefox Nightly adds post-quantum (Kyber) key exchange behind the about:config flag security.tls.enable_kyber.
- Listener tip: a GitHub script can regenerate TOTP QR codes from a LastPass export; Steve prints his QR codes as an offline backup.
- Large iOS app "documents and data" can be cleared by delete and reinstall.
- Sync.com acknowledged and fixed its iOS Files-app integration bug.
- Signed-camera images: a photographer-owned removable HSM key was suggested; Steve and Leo stress the threat model (it proves post-capture handling, not that the scene was real; photographing a screen defeats it).
- Google's Privacy Sandbox needs a native browser engine, which explains why Google has worked on a non-WebKit Chrome for iOS.
- PLCs on the Internet (listener Dylan): management wants production telemetry, PLC vendors add web servers and TCP/IP stacks to old designs, and programmers under deadline pressure disable authentication and leave default passwords; IT staff are not involved. Fix is network segmentation or newer platforms, but unbroken devices rarely get replaced.
- Main topic: after Google removes third-party cookies, Amazon and UK publisher Reach struck a first-party contextual data deal; advertisers like S4 Capital's Martin Sorrell expect more sign-ups to gather "consented data". Steve predicts sites will demand registration (identity, not payment) and share data with advertisers, making visits fully identified, and suggests the EFF should back Google's more private approach.

# Other segments

- SpinRite 6.1: GRC code-signing rewritten as a separate client/server service and moved from SHA-1 to SHA-256; Ventoy incompatibility traced to missing High Memory Area, to be fixed by shipping the 81K DOS-only executable in the boot ISO; no new bugs from over 1,000 testers.
- Steve considers replacing GitLab with a lighter tracker such as Redmine.
- Picture of the Week: a high-voltage box being hit by a lawn sprinkler.
- Leo mentions the (widely repeated) story of three million smart toothbrushes in a Swiss DDoS.
- Aside: the beeping water alarm that annoyed listeners finally died; Stephen/Steven gathering joke; LearnDMARC follow-up; Windows Defender warnings on SpinRite pre-release.
