# Summary

The episode opens with the fallout from the UK's secret order demanding that Apple weaken Advanced Data Protection (ADP), iCloud's end-to-end encrypted tier. Senator Ron Wyden and Representative Andy Biggs wrote to the Director of National Intelligence asking her to reconsider US-UK intelligence arrangements, and Wyden began circulating a CLOUD Act amendment. Steve objects, pedantically, to the word "backdoor": a backdoor is secret by definition, whereas what the UK wants is a locked front door that only Apple can open, and he challenges listeners to coin a better term. He then covers February's Patch Tuesday (63 flaws, two exploited Windows zero-days, a CVSS 9.0 HPC Pack bug and a Windows LDAP race-condition RCE) and a profile of RansomHub, the most active ransomware-as-a-service group of 2024. Further news: Android 16's block on risky settings changes during phone calls (a defence against Telephone-Oriented Attack Delivery, TOAD), Texas opening an investigation into DeepSeek, Apple's fix for a flaw that could switch off USB Restricted Mode on locked devices, and James Howells' lost 8,000 bitcoins. After a sci-fi author update and a listener note on DNS caching, the main topic is Wladimir Palant's blog post "Chrome Web Store Is a Mess", which documents weak moderation, fake reviews, meaningless "Featured" badges and cloned or malicious extensions. Steve's conclusion is that Google cannot or will not curate the roughly 135,000 listings, so users are on their own, and he personally sticks with Firefox and uBlock Origin.

# Key points

- UK order under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016 demands a blanket ability to access encrypted ADP data worldwide; it is a criminal offence for Apple to confirm it. Apple can appeal but cannot delay compliance.
- US response: Wyden (D) and Biggs (R) call it effectively a foreign cyberattack through political means and ask the DNI to review intelligence sharing; a draft CLOUD Act change would require home-country judge orders and bar foreign demands to alter US firms' encryption.
- Steve's terminology point: "backdoor" implies secrecy; a mandated capability is a "front door" with an escrowed key. Apple refuses to hold keys at all, and ADP is opt-in so most users already have Apple holding their keys. Geofencing the UK is impractical (travellers, cross-border conversations).
- February 2025 Patch Tuesday: 63 flaws plus 23 in Edge; two exploited zero-days (Windows Storage elevation of privilege, CVSS 7.1; Ancillary Function Driver for WinSock, CVSS 7.8). Both were added to CISA's KEV catalog. Similar AFD.sys bugs were linked to North Korea's Lazarus Group, and such native-driver bugs avoid the need for Bring Your Own Vulnerable Driver.
- Other Microsoft fixes worth noting: CVE-2025-21198 (HPC Pack remote code execution, CVSS 9.0), a Windows LDAP RCE at 8.1 limited by a race condition, and an NTLMv2 hash disclosure at 6.5. Advice: patch soon.
- RansomHub: top ransomware group of 2024 with 600+ victims; built from the Knight/Cyclops RaaS source code, recruits affiliates from LockBit and BlackCat, offers affiliates an 80 percent share, and encrypts Windows, ESXi and SFTP targets.
- Group-IB case: a Palo Alto PAN-OS exploit attempt failed, then brute-forcing a VPN with a default backup-solution account succeeded; unpatched 2020/2021 Active Directory flaws (ZeroLogon, noPac) gave domain controller control, with exfiltration and encryption inside 24 hours.
- Steve's view: ransomware persists because insurer payouts make it too lucrative; law enforcement takedowns just cause groups to rebrand and merge.
- TOAD: scammers phone victims (often after an SMS lure) and talk them into sideloading apps or granting accessibility access; Android 16 Beta 2 blocks those settings changes during a call. Steve praises the friction; Leo notes Zelle/Chase blocking payments originating from social-media contacts.
- Texas AG Ken Paxton opens an investigation into DeepSeek and asks Apple and Google for their app-store vetting documents; New York and Virginia ban it on government devices, and a bipartisan federal bill follows. Leo shows the open model running on US infrastructure without censorship, and Steve sees a growing "technology cold war" of trusting only domestic hardware and software.
- Apple patched a bug (affecting iPhone XS and later and several iPad models) that could disable USB Restricted Mode on a locked device, a step usable in Cellebrite-style physical attacks; Restricted Mode otherwise blocks data over the port after an hour locked.
- James Howells' discarded drive with 8,000 BTC (about 800 million USD) in a Newport, Wales landfill: he lost his court case and the site is to become a solar farm.
- Palant's Chrome Web Store critique: Google's reporting channels are confusing, reporters get no feedback, the Developer Data Protection Reward Program ended in August 2024, spam clusters (one of 108 extensions in 2023, 96 still live in January 2025; 920 more recently) are not enforced against, fake and incentivized reviews persist, and the "Featured" badge appears to rest on automated checks only.
- Examples cited: refoorest (made-up tree-planting claims), Karma (browsing data sold), the PDF Toolbox cluster and cloned VPN extensions that do not work yet are "Featured".
- Palant's history: Chrome launched with automated review and sandboxing while Mozilla required unobfuscated source and no remote code; Manifest V3 only recently banned remotely hosted code. Broad "read and change all data" permissions make permission prompts meaningless. He argues Google should invest in manual cluster take-downs now, while the store has under 150,000 extensions and about 60 percent are abandoned.
- Steve's advice: understand the risk and decide per extension; stick to well-known extensions (Leo lists Bitwarden, Kagi, Raindrop.io, Snowflake, uBlock Origin); supply-chain risk also applies to apps and SDKs.
