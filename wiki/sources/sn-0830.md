# Summary

Steve starts with Firefox's slide from about 254 million to under 200 million monthly active users and the risk of a Chromium browser monoculture, then notes Chrome 93 will drop the HTTPS padlock because secure is now the default. He summarises KELA's year-long study of initial access brokers (IABs), the specialised sellers of network footholds to ransomware affiliates, with average listing prices around $5,400 and a median near $1,000. He lays out why researchers think the new BlackMatter group is DarkSide rebranded (identical custom Salsa20/RSA code, per Emsisoft's Fabian Wosar) and notes other renames such as DoppelPaymer to Grief and Avaddon to Haron. A Windows Active Directory researcher's "Microsoft Won't-Fix List" supports Steve's view that July 2021 changed the picture: many flaws are "by design", so applying Microsoft's patches no longer makes an enterprise safe. He introduces Tailscale as an easy overlay on WireGuard, tells a story of a lost day caused by a stale HOSTS-file entry that broke GRC's e-commerce system, and finishes with the Recorded Future interview of a BlackMatter representative, read with scepticism.

# Key points

- Firefox lost roughly 56 million monthly active users from its January 2019 peak. Leo and Steve discuss Chromium dominance and the danger of a browser monoculture, as well as Firefox's dependence on Google search revenue.
- Chrome 93 removes the padlock; "Not Secure" remains for plain HTTP, though Steve notes some static sites have no real need for TLS.
- KELA's IAB report (July 2020 to June 2021): over 1,000 listings, RDP and VPN access most common, price driven by privilege level (domain admin at least 10x a standard user), top examples near $465,000, buyers often become regular customers and trade privately. Some brokers withdrew offers against healthcare after criticism.
- BlackMatter = DarkSide 2.0: Emsisoft found the same unusual Salsa20 state initialisation and 1024-bit RSA implementation. The group solicits brokers directly and claims to avoid critical infrastructure, healthcare and oil/gas. FBI recovered 63.7 BTC of the Colonial Pipeline ransom earlier.
- Microsoft "Won't-Fix List, July 2021" by Christoph Falta collects PetitPotam, SeriousSAM, PrintNightmare and AD CS issues, many of them NTLM-based. Steve's conclusion: patching alone no longer secures a Windows enterprise; administrators must disable legacy features by policy.
- Tailscale: a mesh VPN built on WireGuard that handles key distribution, NAT traversal, relays, ACLs and MagicDNS; free for one user and up to 20 devices; trade-offs are reliance on its coordination service and userspace WireGuard performance on Linux. WireGuard itself uses fixed modern primitives (Curve25519, ChaCha20, Poly1305, BLAKE2).
- Steve's GRC outage: a forgotten hard-coded HOSTS entry pinned a payment processor's old IP address; removing it fixed the TLS timeouts. Moral: undocumented workarounds bite later.
- BlackMatter interview claims: developed for six months, borrowed ideas from LockBit, REvil and DarkSide, encrypts only the first 1 MB per file (LockBit reportedly 256 KB), will avoid critical infrastructure, prefers direct high-privilege access over VPNs, and expects insurance not to change victim behaviour. Steve stresses it is self-serving, not credible.
- Password managers that match exact URLs avoid phishing auto-fill, correcting Steve's remark from the previous episode.
