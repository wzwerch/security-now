# Summary

Co-hosted by Ant Pruitt while Leo is away. Steve opens with the 2021 Oldsmar, Florida water-plant "lye attack", which the former city manager now says was simply an employee clicking the wrong button, with an FBI inquiry reaching the same conclusion. He then covers AI policy confusion: a US Chamber of Commerce report and Italy's temporary GDPR-based ban on ChatGPT, plus Twitter publishing its recommendation code on GitHub. India is seeking lower-profile alternatives to NSO Group's Pegasus, just as a 12-nation Summit for Democracy statement condemned commercial spyware. The FDA now requires cybersecurity plans and software bills of materials for new medical devices, and the "Hack the Pentagon" bug bounty has a dedicated site after finding 2,100+ flaws. Steve follows up on listener reaction to his Exchange rant, discusses sci-fi book series and SpinRite 6.1's nearing release, and ends with the main topic: Rezilion's finding that over 15 million systems still expose vulnerabilities in CISA's KEV catalog. He uses the top entries, an Apache mod_proxy SSRF and an eight-year-old IIS flaw, to define "zombie software" and criticizes the industry habit of shipping buggy code and relying on updates.

# Key points

- Oldsmar water treatment (Feb 2021): reported sodium hydroxide change from 100 to 11,100 ppm was, per the ex-city manager, operator error resolved in two minutes; Steve says the publicity may still have improved other plants' hygiene.
- US Chamber of Commerce AI Commission report calls for a risk-based regulatory framework; Steve thinks policymakers do not understand the technology but fear falling behind China.
- Italy's data protection authority temporarily banned ChatGPT, citing the 20 March data breach, lack of a legal basis for training data, inaccurate personal data and missing age verification. Steve argues crawling public data is comparable to Google's indexing.
- Twitter released its timeline-ranking code; commentary suggests a typical engagement-driven filter-bubble design.
- India plans to spend roughly $120 million on lesser-known spyware to replace Pegasus, motivated by a "PR problem" from forensic traceability.
- Twelve nations signed a statement on countering commercial spyware (export controls, information sharing, policy alignment).
- FDA guidance (30 March 2023): new "cyber device" submissions need a vulnerability management plan, patching process and an SBOM; enforcement from 1 October 2023.
- "Hack the Pentagon" results: more than 2,100 vulnerabilities found by 1,400+ hackers; new site hackthepentagon.mil.
- Firefox 110+ offers about:third-party to list and block injected third-party DLLs, useful against injected malware and crash causes.
- Listener feedback: forcing upgrades of old Exchange servers does improve internet safety; Steve agrees it is effective but still views the licence revocation as wrong.
- Rezilion scan: more than 15 million internet-exposed systems vulnerable to 896 KEV entries; number one is CVE-2021-40438 (Apache mod_proxy SSRF, CVSS 9.0) on about 6.45 million IPs; number five is CVE-2015-1635 (Windows HTTP.sys remote code execution, CVSS 10.0) on about 120,000 servers, still patchable via Windows Update.
- Steve's thesis: much software is on perpetual life support; "zombie" software endangers its own operators, not remote email recipients, contradicting Microsoft's Exchange blocking rationale.

# Other segments

- Picture of the week: cartoon about Congress finally knowing TikTok is not a breath mint.
- Book recommendations: Scott Jucha's Silver Ships series finished, now starting the Gate Ghosts series; Ryk Brown's Frontiers Saga continues.
- SpinRite 6.1: about 668 alpha testers, only edge cases around badly damaged drives remain; SpinRite 7 planned on a now-unsupported embedded 32-bit RTOS that Steve considers finished and bug-free.
- GRC DNS Benchmark: 8.2 million downloads, never had a bug.
