# Summary

Recorded on the last Patch Tuesday that includes Windows XP, the episode drops its planned entropy-harvesting topic to explain Heartbleed, announced the day before. Steve first covers the XP milestone: he again calls the end of patches a tempest in a teapot because most attacks hit applications, not the OS, and notes the Office 2003 RTF zero-day was finally patched. Miscellany includes a rave for the documentary "Particle Fever", Mark Russinovich's novel "Rogue Code", AnyDVD's legal troubles, the PushBullet app, and SQRL's 54 languages with 362 volunteers. The core segment explains Heartbleed: a TLS heartbeat extension (added in 2012 as keep-alive support for TLS over UDP) was implemented in OpenSSL 1.0.1 through 1.0.1f without a bounds check, so a client could ask a server to echo back up to 64 KB of its memory per request, repeatedly, leaving no log trace. That memory can hold usernames, passwords, session data and, worst, the server's private keys. Steve reviews the responses from Tor and LastPass, how Netcraft estimated that most HTTPS sites never offered the extension, tests such as SSL Labs, and the real remediation: patch OpenSSL, revoke and reissue certificates, and confirm that browsers actually check revocation. He sees certificate expiry as useful because it bounds the damage of stolen keys.

# Key points

- XP's last monthly update arrived; Microsoft's countdown clock and large paid support deals are mostly marketing, says Steve. The Office 2003 RTF flaw from the previous month was fixed.
- Heartbleed mechanics: the TLS heartbeat message includes a payload and its claimed length; OpenSSL trusted the length and returned adjacent memory (up to 64 KB), affecting 1.0.1 through 1.0.1f and 1.0.2-beta. Version 1.0.1g fixes it.
- Exposure window of two years for sites running the then-new 1.0.1 series; attacks leave no trace. Clients (such as software using OpenSSL) can also be attacked by malicious servers, but servers are the main risk.
- Researchers who found it stole their own test server's keys, credentials and messages without any trace; Heartbleed.com summarized the issue.
- Netcraft: about 82.5 percent of SSL sites never offered the extension and were not vulnerable; the "two thirds of the web" figure counts OpenSSL use overall rather than vulnerable versions.
- Tor Project advice: the Tor Browser is not affected (it uses NSS) but relays and hidden services could leak keys; users needing strong anonymity were told to stay off the internet for a few days.
- LastPass patched, restarted, revoked and replaced its certificate, and explained its trust-no-one design limits the impact; GRC was not vulnerable. SSL Labs added a Heartbleed check; Filippo Valsorda's tester and a command-line tool were released.
- What users should do: change passwords only after a site says it has patched and replaced its certificate; a recently reissued certificate is a good sign. Stolen private keys also enable impersonation and man-in-the-middle attacks, and could let a past recorded session be decrypted if forward secrecy was not used.
- Revocation: servers should revoke and reissue certificates; browsers must check revocation (Chrome's server certificate revocation option was off by default), and Steve wants a test site with a revoked certificate. Short certificate lifetimes limit damage.
- No advance private notice was given to vendors the way Dan Kaminsky coordinated the DNS flaw, so sites raced attackers.

# Other segments

- "Particle Fever" (documentary on the Large Hadron Collider and the Higgs boson) recommended; "Rogue Code" book about high-frequency trading started.
- AnyDVD (disc copy-protection removal) faces a legal judgment; Steve says buying discs and using such a tool is a personal choice.
- Steve notes his earlier "toy" description of XP and ran a VM to check GDI+ availability.
- PushBullet suggested for sending items between Windows and iOS.
- SQRL: 54 languages (adding Lithuanian and Latvian) and 362 volunteers; Steve is writing the entropy harvester and promises a show on it.
- SpinRite: a screenshot where early maintenance shows only recoverable sectors, which Steve contrasts with running it too late.
