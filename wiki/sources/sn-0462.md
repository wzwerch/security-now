# Summary

Fr. Robert Ballecer replaces Leo Laporte as co-host this week. In the news, Microsoft briefly announced it would stop emailing security bulletins because of Canada's new anti-spam law (CASL), even though the law exempts security alerts, and then reversed itself. PayPal froze the account of the crowdfunding email startup ProtonMail, with a representative reportedly asking whether it had government approval to encrypt email; the account was unfrozen after an outcry. Steve gives a first look at ProtonMail (browser-based end-to-end encryption, Swiss servers) and objects to its "self-destructing" message claim, noting key management is the hard part. Other items: the Supreme Court requiring warrants to search phones, iOS 7.1.2 security fixes, Google's weak certificate revocation handling (Steve believes this is one engineer's stance and will change), a survey showing 58 percent of the top 50 sites work over HTTPS, and an IBM report that Android 4.3's KeyStore skipped buffer bounds checks "to keep things simple," a stack overflow affecting about 10 percent of devices. Steve explains the earlier Google Docs "anonymous viewers" puzzle (signed-out viewers show up as anonymous users), mentions ammonia cracking as a hydrogen carrier, and gives SQRL and SpinRite updates (including SSD recovery). The main topic opens a multi-week series on Trust No One cloud storage with a public comparison spreadsheet and a list of about 25 providers.

# Key points

- Microsoft's CASL scare: the law exempts security and safety messages, security experts called the move panicky, and Microsoft resumed notices.
- PayPal froze ProtonMail's crowdfunding funds without notice; Steve thinks it was a low-level mistake, Ballecer calls the precedent alarming.
- ProtonMail: JavaScript-based end-to-end encrypted email, non-users get a link plus shared password; concerns are protocol documentation, key exchange and the unrealistic "expiring email" feature. Threema's green level (QR scan in person) is cited as better key authentication.
- Blackphone (Silent Circle bundle) mentioned as a hardened Android phone.
- Supreme Court ruled police generally need a warrant to search a phone's contents.
- iOS 7.1.2 fixed email-attachment encryption, a Find My Phone bypass, lock-screen bypasses and WebKit flaws and refreshed root certificates; updates re-enable Bluetooth.
- Google distrusts online revocation checking (OCSP) and uses its own CRLSet; Steve expects it to change, notes CDNs make OCSP fast, and says hard-fail Firefox users see no slowdown.
- Alexa top-50 analysis: 29 sites work fully over HTTPS.
- IBM disclosed an Android 4.3 KeyStore stack overflow (comment said bounds checks are omitted because buffers are always large enough); it could leak lock credentials and master keys to malicious code. KitKat is not affected; disclosed after ten months.
- Anonymous Google Docs viewers are created each time a link is opened while signed out.
- Cracking ammonia cheaply into hydrogen could make ammonia a practical hydrogen carrier.
- SQRL: 414 translators and 60 languages signed up; entropy harvesting is so effective that UI panels disappeared.
- SSDs also develop errors and slowdowns, and SpinRite helped Samsung SSDs; Steve expects a SpinRite 7.
- Cloud storage series goals: compare TNO clients and providers on platforms, cost models (pay-as-you-go such as Amazon S3 at about 3 cents per GB-month versus tiers), turnkey versus toolkit, and hybrid use of providers like Google Drive with client-side encryption.
- Cloud sync is not backup (a single device deletion propagates, providers can be unreachable); Ballecer suggests a redundant array of independent clouds.
- Candidate list includes Arq, Bitcasa, BoxCryptor, Backblaze, CloudBerry, CrashPlan, Duplicati, Jungle Disk, Mega, OneDrive, Tresorit, SpiderOak, Syncthing, Tarsnap, Viivo, Wuala and Zoolz.
