# Summary

After deferring the topic for a week, Steve delivers his privacy survey. The news starts with Symantec's finding that 40 past targeted attacks on organizations in 16 countries match tools described in the Vault 7 documents, the CA/Browser Forum's decision that CAs must check the new DNS CAA record by September 2017, and April's Patch Tuesday fixing an Office OLE2Link zero-day that FireEye had seen used through phishing documents. Radware's Brickerbot, a permanent denial-of-service worm that wipes exposed IoT devices through Telnet default credentials, is discussed along with the October 2016 hijack of a large Brazilian bank's DNS registration for about six hours. Additional items are Garadget disabling a customer's garage door opener after a bad review, the joke ransomware Rensenware, fake VPN phishing, Vista reaching end of support, and many listener notes. The main segment lists the tracking hooks (IP address, cookies, browser metadata and fingerprinting, DNS queries, major web players' beacons) and recommends concrete steps: disable third-party cookies, use DNSCrypt, use a reputable VPN and incognito mode, and change IP address and browser when going dark.

# Key points

- Symantec reviewed old telemetry after Vault 7 and attributed 40 targeted attacks at organizations in 16 countries to the leaked CIA toolset, showing the documents explain previously unattributed intrusions.
- DNS CAA (Certificate Authority Authorization): domain owners publish which CAs may issue for their domain; the CA/Browser Forum required CAs to check it within six months. It is only a hint (not cryptographic) and does not stop malicious issuance, but it is easy to deploy; servers that lack support can publish record type 257 manually, and an empty list (";") blocks all issuance.
- April Patch Tuesday patched an Office zero-day in which a Word document with an OLE2Link object fetched an .hta file disguised as RTF; a registry workaround had circulated beforehand.
- Brickerbot (Radware): Telnet-brute-forcing bot that seeks Dahua-style default credentials and then destroys the device's storage, firewall and network configuration. Radware's honeypot saw 1,895 attempts in four days; a second variant hid behind Tor exit nodes, and early attack sources were vulnerable Ubiquiti airOS gear. Leo notes the "malware for good" angle.
- Kaspersky documented a Brazilian bank (about 5 million customers) losing control of the DNS settings for all of its domains for five to six hours after an attacker apparently got into its registrar account; the fake sites even had valid Let's Encrypt certificates. Steve urges strong registrar logins with time-based two-factor and notes Let's Encrypt's 90-day certificates, issued for automation, still do not stop fast domain-validation abuse.
- Garadget, a smart garage door opener maker, cut off a customer after an abusive Amazon review, then restored access after a backlash.
- Rensenware demanded a high score on a Touhou bullet-hell game instead of money, and was a joke gone wrong whose author supplied an unlocker.
- Motherboard's reporter exposed a "MySafeVPN" phishing campaign using email lists from old Plex and Boxee breaches; Steve warns that fake VPNs exist and recommends checking a provider's reputation, WHOIS age and log policy. Sonic's free VPN and Leo's remarks about ISP privacy are mentioned.
- April 2017 was end of support for Windows Vista.
- Listener notes: Firefox autoplay off still permits playing YouTube if you click the progress bar; Steve defends JavaScript's use for browser-side tools while calling the language an abomination; ransomware decryption tools only help against badly implemented ransomware; ShieldsUP can show stealth ports during full scans because the router's scan protection blocks the scanner IP; IoT devices could embed their own long-lived certificates, so ISP-forced root certificates may not break them; the libsodium Random.org story was an April Fools' joke.
- Privacy survey: tracking vectors are IP address (even household-level), first- and third-party cookies (Safari blocks third-party cookies by default; GRC's data showed 79 percent of visitors allow them), redirect chains that make trackers first-party, plug-in storage such as Flash cookies, browser metadata and fingerprinting (EFF Panopticlick showed Steve's Firefox as unique), and DNS queries sent in clear.
- Countermeasures: disable third-party cookies, use DNSCrypt, use a VPN that encapsulates DNS (check with GRC's DNS Spoofability Test), use incognito mode, change browser, wipe or use a dedicated VM or "black ops" laptop, and change public IP before going anonymous. Steve says Tor Browser strips fewer headers than he would like and VPN exit nodes concentrate trust; a crowdfunded "Filter" router claiming to inspect traffic is overselling.
