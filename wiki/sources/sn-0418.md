# Summary

Steve starts the ninth year of the show by revisiting Lavabit, where NBC reported that the Justice Department hinted its founder might have violated a court order by shutting down, with Steve inferring the real demand was ongoing wiretap-style access for all users. He then covers the British government's pressure on The Guardian, which included supervised destruction of hard drives in its basement, and the nine-hour detention at Heathrow of Glenn Greenwald's partner under terrorism law. Groklaw's Pamela Jones announced she was shutting down over email privacy fears and recommended a Swiss mail host that, as Steve points out, offers no real protection. He reads Ben Adida's open letter to President Obama arguing that mass surveillance is like a full-body scan, since a low base rate and false positives make it counterproductive, and that a perfectly enforced law would block social progress. He mocks Google's newly announced encryption of its own drives as "zero-value encryption" because Google holds the keys, notes Facebook's handling of a researcher who posted on Mark Zuckerberg's timeline to prove a bug, and warns again about XP's end of support in April 2014. The main segment explains PGP: its name, Phil Zimmermann's early BassOmatic cipher mistake, the OpenPGP standard, and the sign-then-encrypt hybrid scheme with a one-time symmetric key, plus ASCII armor via radix-64. Steve backs PGP's cryptography but says that authentication and adoption remain the hard parts.

# Key points

- Lavabit: the government reportedly wanted ongoing access across all accounts; the founder had complied with about two dozen user-specific orders earlier. Prosecutors suggested shutting down might itself violate the order.
- The Guardian's editor described UK officials demanding return or destruction of the Snowden files and threatening prior restraint; staff destroyed laptops under watch by GCHQ experts. David Miranda was held under Schedule 7 of the UK terror law.
- Groklaw's Pamela Jones cited the impossibility of private email and quoted Alan Westin's four states of privacy (solitude, anonymity, reserve, intimacy).
- Ben Adida's argument: mass surveillance is like a whole-body scan, generating false positives and scope creep, and a society that detects all lawbreaking cannot progress, since civil disobedience helps align laws with morality.
- Google's drive encryption is server-side with provider-held keys, which Steve calls zero-value encryption (ZVE) compared with TNO and PIE; client-side tools such as TrueCrypt or Boxcryptor give real protection.
- XP support ended April 8, 2014; Steve notes that patches for newer Windows versions will reveal bugs that remain unpatched in XP.
- A Palestinian researcher found a Facebook flaw allowing posts on any wall; after support dismissed it, he posted on Zuckerberg's wall, and was then denied the bounty for breaking the terms of service.
- PGP basics: named after a Garrison Keillor grocery; version 1.0 used Zimmermann's own cipher BassOmatic, soon found weak; later versions used standard algorithms (IDEA, 3DES, AES), and the formal standard is OpenPGP (RFC 4880) with GnuPG as an implementation.
- PGP data flow: compress, optionally sign (hash then encrypt hash with sender's private key), then generate a one-time random symmetric key, encrypt the data with it and encrypt that key with the recipient's public key; the recipient reverses the steps.
- ASCII armor uses radix-64 to turn each 3 bytes into 4 printable characters (a one-third size increase) so binary survives email gateways.
- Key hierarchies exist but the web of trust, where users sign each other's keys with stated confidence levels, is the common model; key servers, key expiry and revocation certificates are supported. Email headers and the fact of communication remain visible.
- Steve's verdict: PGP's fundamentals are as good as any known and he endorses it without reservation, but authentication and mobile endpoint security remain the weak points. Leo adds that always signing and encrypting helps and that Apple was rumored to be adding a fingerprint reader.

# Other segments

- Leo plans a 24-hour New Year's Eve broadcast with countdowns across time zones.
- Praise for Leo's Triangulation interview with Esther Dyson; Steve's enthusiasm for Breaking Bad.
- SpinRite 6.1: test releases are starting to transfer data, with testing on 133 MHz Pentiums and possible early inclusion of AHCI controller support.
- Correction that Bitmessage does not use the Bitcoin blockchain, and messages expire after a couple of weeks.
