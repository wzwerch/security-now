# Summary

The episode opens with the four time-limited free telecommuting offers (Microsoft Teams, Google Hangouts Meet, LogMeIn, Cisco Webex) made at the start of the COVID-19 lockdowns; Steve notes that Microsoft's six months is the longest and suspects some companies will keep remote work. He then covers the Pentagon's DC3 Vulnerability Disclosure Program annual report for 2019, which shows the hack-us-please approach working at scale. A Sophos/Which? story on aging Android phones leads to Steve's view that phones should warn users when updates end. Graz University researchers published "Take A Way", showing AMD's L1D cache way predictor leaks memory-access patterns, while Positive Technologies disclosed an unfixable flaw in the Intel CSME boot ROM affecting about five years of chips. A 17-year-old critical overflow in the Linux pppd daemon (CVSS 9.8) matters mainly to embedded devices. Browsers will soon replace quiet warnings with full-page errors for TLS 1.0/1.1 sites, and SETI@home is going into hibernation. The main topic is Google's FuzzBench, an open service to benchmark fuzzers fairly.

# Key points

- Free telework tools: Microsoft Teams free for six months; Google Hangouts Meet premium features free until 1 July 2020; LogMeIn emergency kits for three months; Cisco Webex with no meeting time limit for 100 participants plus 90-day licenses. Steve expects the crisis may permanently shift work habits.
- DOD DC3 VDP 2019 report: 4,013 reports from 1,460 researchers, 2,836 validated; record year, 12,489 reports total since 2016. Most common classes: information exposure, secure design violations, XSS, business logic errors, open redirects. Run via HackerOne; plan to extend to all DOD information systems and the defense industrial base.
- Android support gap: Which? tested older phones (Android 7 and earlier) and infected them with known malware via old flaws such as BlueFrag and Stagefright; Google removed 1,700 Joker-infected apps. Steve argues Android should nag users once a device stops receiving updates; Leo leans toward recommending iPhones.
- AMD "Take A Way" (Graz University): reverse-engineering the L1D cache way predictor (2011-2019 designs) gave two techniques, Collide+Probe and Load+Reload, used for a covert channel (up to 588 kbps), kernel data leakage, key recovery and weakening ASLR. AMD said these are not new attacks; the researchers disagreed.
- Intel CSME boot ROM flaw (Positive Technologies): a hard-coded ROM bug in the Converged Security and Management Engine undermines the root of trust (Boot Guard, TPM firmware, EPID, DRM) on all Intel chips of the last five years except 10th generation. Intel knew of it (CVE-2019-0090) and only patched one attack vector; exploitation likely needs local or physical access.
- pppd flaw (CVE-2020-8597): stack overflow in the EAP packet parser, present in versions 2.4.2 to 2.4.8, exploitable by unauthenticated peers over any PPP transport. Matters for embedded gear (Cisco CallManager, TP-Link, OpenWrt, Synology products); desktop users can simply remove pppd.
- TLS 1.0/1.1: browsers plan full-page errors later in March 2020; Netcraft counts over 850,000 sites still offering nothing newer. Steve expects the topic to be back in the news.
- SETI@home stops distributing work on 31 March 2020 after about 21 years, citing diminishing returns and the need to finish analysis and publish.
- FuzzBench (Google OSS-Fuzz team): free, open-source service that runs submitted fuzzers for 24 hours against many real-world libraries (curl, OpenSSL, SQLite3, libpng, PHP and others) with statistical comparison; integrations are small Python modules; ten fuzzers already integrated, coverage is the metric.
- Leo adds that property-based testing and formal proof are the opposite end of the reliability spectrum from fuzzing.
