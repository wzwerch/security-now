# Summary

The episode opens with the EU's proposed regulation to make scanning of private communications for child sexual abuse material and grooming mandatory for all providers, replacing the voluntary regime; Steve and Leo discuss how it could be implemented technically and why Signal-style services are unlikely to comply. A Finnish suspect was detained in France over the Vastaamo psychotherapy breach, which prompts a point about excessive data retention. Steve learns that .dev (and 39 other top-level domains) are on the browser HSTS preload list and explains HSTS, notes Chrome 110's early-stable rollout, Russia's plan to fine and jail IT staff after breaches, crypto-drainer phishing kits, 98% unpatched QNAP devices, and VulnCheck's finding that vendors often rate their own bugs lower than NIST. Listener questions cover OTP apps and vendor push authentication. The main story is ESXiArgs: over 3,200 ESXi servers encrypted through a two-year-old OpenSLP heap overflow (CVE-2021-21974) that VMware had patched in February 2021; Steve argues VMware could and should have scanned and warned its own customers and criticizes the software industry's lack of accountability.

# Key points

- EU CSAM regulation (document 52022PC0209): proposes mandatory, permanent detection, reporting, blocking and removal of CSAM and grooming by all providers, with an "EU Centre" maintaining indicator databases; a parliamentary vote was expected in March 2023 with obligations from about April 2024. Steve notes 72% of polled Europeans object and that the Court of Justice has ruled general automatic analysis of private communications violates fundamental rights, but courts act slowly. Open technical questions are where scanning would run (OS, app or hardware) and how Signal or Telegram could comply.
- Leo predicts pressure on Apple, Google and Samsung and a drift toward open platforms; Steve restates that "the math has already escaped".
- Vastaamo: a 25-year-old Finnish national detained in France over the 2018-2019 theft of psychotherapy records and extortion of about 30,000 patients (200 euros each). Steve's lesson: records that are not actively needed should move to offline archives and be destroyed after retention periods; he found no mandatory deletion rule under HIPAA.
- HSTS review: Strict-Transport-Security header, max-age, includeSubDomains and the preload list (hstspreload.org) solve first-contact interception; 40 top-level domains are preloaded, including .dev, .app, .bank, .google, .microsoft and .zip, so any site there needs valid HTTPS. New TLDs can be preloaded as a whole.
- Chrome 110 introduces an early stable release to a small percentage of users one week before general release; Steve plans similar staged release for SpinRite 6.1.
- Russia is advancing legislation for fines (300,000 to 2 million rubles) and up to 10 years in prison for IT administrators and managers after major data breaches; Steve warns of punishing the messenger.
- Crypto drainer phishing templates, sold on cybercrime forums per Recorded Future, trick victims into connecting wallets to "mint NFTs" and drain them.
- QNAP: after a patch for SQL injection CVE-2022-27596, Censys found about 98% of roughly 30,000 tracked devices still unpatched, with ransomware abuse expected. Steve praises Synology (he bought a DS418 after a Drobo failed).
- VulnCheck compared about 25,000 NVD entries and found 14,000 where vendor and NIST CVSS scores differ, mostly from 39 vendors, suggesting systematic downgrading of severity, which distorts patch prioritization.
- OTP advice: Steve uses the iOS app OTP Auth (iCloud app-data sync, encrypted backup, widget); vendor push approval beats SMS but is vulnerable to notification fatigue, while TOTP is open-loop but can be relayed by a phishing site. Steve and Leo agree there is no single right master-password scheme.
- ESXiArgs: ransomware derived from leaked Babuk code encrypted over 3,200 ESXi servers over a weekend (France hit hardest, many via hosting provider OVHcloud), demanding about 2.03 bitcoin (roughly 50,000 dollars) per server and using a Tox ID for contact; wallets were partly reused, so payment is doubly uncertain. It targets virtual machine files such as .vmdk and leaves .args files; one victim found no evidence of exfiltration.
- Root cause: CVE-2021-21974, a heap overflow in VMware's own OpenSLP service on port 427, found by Trend Micro ZDI's Lucas Leong, patched in February 2021 (after incomplete earlier patches for related bugs), with a later third-party write-up following his approach. The service ran by default although rarely needed; VMware disabled it by default from ESXi 7.0 U2c / 8.0 and recommends disabling it. CISA investigated; VMware said no zero-day was involved.
- Steve's argument: VMware could scan for and notify exposed customers, as governments now do; software vendors escape the accountability that drives improvement in other industries.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a heavy chain with a padlock and an oddly redundant nylon zip tie.
- SpinRite 6.1 release plan: final beta announced to listeners first, then general release; reserved spinrite.dev domain.
- ChatGPT "Astonishing Reply of the Week": it correctly flagged an obfuscated JavaScript snippet as likely malicious; Steve cautions that it is often wrong yet sounds authoritative and expects chatbots to reshape search (Microsoft Bing/Edge announced that day).
