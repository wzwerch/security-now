# Summary

Hosted with Fr. Robert Ballecer (Leo is travelling). Drupalgeddon2 continues as predicted: mass scanning, coin miners, PHP backdoors and the Tsunami botnet adopting the exploit, plus a separate cross-site-scripting issue in the bundled CKEditor for Drupal 8. Symantec's Orangeworm report describes a multi-year campaign using the Kwampirs trojan against healthcare organisations and their suppliers, mainly through machines that control imaging equipment. The FDA signals it will require built-in update capability and a software bill of materials for medical devices. Microsoft led 34 companies into the Cybersecurity Tech Accord. A Swedish datacenter fire-suppression gas release damaged hard drives, illustrating acoustic vibration risk. A UK survey shows most consumers never touch their routers. The episode closes with Steve's enthusiasm for Microsoft's Azure Sphere: a license-free secure IoT chip (MediaTek MT3620), secure Linux OS and cloud security service.

# Key points

- Drupalgeddon2 status: exploitation began within days of the patch; coin miners, PHP backdoors, Perl bots and the Tsunami botnet in the wild. Steve calls the lingering exploitation "Internet background radiation" and notes cryptomining gives attackers immediate payoff, unlike earlier ransomware.
- A follow-up Drupal 8 flaw in CKEditor's image plugin (cross-site scripting) was rated only "moderately critical", which the hosts consider too low.
- Orangeworm (Symantec): trojan Kwampirs active since January 2015 against healthcare, pharma and suppliers in the US, Europe and Asia; targets control systems for X-ray/MRI machines as a pivot point; Symantec thinks it is not a nation-state and may be after patient data.
- FDA medical-device safety plan: future premarket authority to require updatable designs and a software bill of materials so users can see which devices contain vulnerable components. Steve says voting machines need the same.
- Cybersecurity Tech Accord (announced at RSA): four principles (strong defense, no offensive development support, capacity building, collective action); signatories include Microsoft, Cisco, Facebook, GitHub, Oracle, Symantec and others; Amazon, Apple, Google and Intel are absent. Ballecer worries about the "no material support" clause given US government demands.
- Hard drives are acoustically sensitive; a fire-suppression gas discharge at a Swedish datacenter on April 18 knocked out drives and disrupted Nordic NASDAQ operations. Writes during vibration can corrupt low-level formatting.
- WiFi SSIDs like "Remote Detonator" have triggered police responses at a gym and on flights; naming is not illegal but disruptive. Ballecer notes devices auto-join familiar SSIDs like "Starbucks Free WiFi".
- Broadband Genie survey (2,200 UK adults): only 14% updated router firmware, 18% changed network name and admin password, 30% check connected devices, 31% changed the WiFi password. Steve's own new ASUS router had UPnP enabled on the WAN. Argument: routers should auto-update; one vendor reportedly cited legal liability for not applying firmware automatically.
- Google "Chat" built on RCS Universal Profile, supported by many carriers, offers iMessage-like features but no end-to-end encryption. Steve speculates Google avoids the encryption legality fight (Russia, China).
- Amazon launches in-car trunk delivery for GM and Volvo vehicles in 37 cities using connected-car unlocking, with package limits (50 lb, no signature, under $1,300, Amazon-sold only).
- Azure Sphere: Microsoft Research design with MediaTek MT3620 (Cortex-A7 application core, Cortex-M4 cores, dedicated security subsystem called Pluton, onboard WiFi), a custom secure Linux OS and a cloud security service usable with any cloud. Built around the "seven properties of highly secure devices" (hardware root of trust, small trusted computing base, defense in depth, compartmentalization, certificate-based authentication, renewable security, failure reporting). Steve is strongly positive.
