# Summary

The episode follows up on the CNNIC and MCS Holdings incident: Google announced that Chrome will stop recognising the CNNIC root and EV certificate authorities, with a temporary public whitelist for existing customers, and CNNIC publicly called the decision unacceptable. Steve then explains Firefox 37's opportunistic encryption, in which an HTTP response advertises an alternative TLS service via an Alt-Svc header, and notes that a flaw in how it handled those headers was fixed within a day. He points out that Google's Gmail SMTP service failed when an intermediate certificate expired, and that the replacement certificates were again signed with SHA-1 even as Chrome flags SHA-1 on his own site. Further items cover Microsoft's retreat from enabling Do Not Track by default and the growing aftermarket for IPv4 address space. The main topic is the second-phase audit of TrueCrypt, which found no backdoor and four issues that Steve judges unimportant in practice, so he continues to regard the original as usable.

# Key points

- Chrome pins Google's certificates, which is how the forged one was found; as a result Google announced removal of CNNIC's root and EV CAs in a future Chrome release, with a public whitelist for existing certificates and a requirement for Certificate Transparency before any reinclusion. CNNIC called the move unacceptable.
- Leo and Steve criticise the lack of detail in Google's explanation; Steve notes HSTS sites cannot have certificate warnings bypassed.
- A listener could not download TWiT shows because his employer blocked old SSL; Steve and Leo suspect an HTTPS-enforcing browser extension, since the CDN did not serve HTTPS.
- Firefox 37 opportunistic encryption (OE TLS): an Alt-Svc header lets a plain HTTP connection upgrade in the background to an encrypted connection using a self-signed certificate. It offers no authentication, can be blocked by an attacker, and was patched in 37.0.1 after a header-chaining issue. Steve expects little adoption given free trusted certificates.
- On Saturday 4 April 2015 the smtp.gmail.com certificate chain failed because Google's intermediate CA certificate (issued by GeoTrust) expired; it took hours to reissue. The new certificates are signed with SHA-1, which Steve finds hypocritical given Chrome's warnings about SHA-1 on other sites.
- Microsoft announced that Do Not Track will no longer be on by default in Windows Express Settings, citing the W3C draft requiring the signal to reflect a deliberate user choice. Steve reads it as a retreat and recalls his phrase "tyranny of the default."
- IPv4 aftermarket: nearly two million addresses transferred in one month of late 2014; a Romanian organisation sold roughly half of all blocks transferred in 2014 at around ten dollars per address, and Saudi Telecom bought about 1.5 million. Steve notes fragmentation burdens router tables.
- TrueCrypt audit phase 2 (NCC / iSEC Partners, announced by Matthew Green): no deliberate backdoor and no severe design flaw. Four findings, from worst to least:
  - Windows CryptoAPI use could silently fail in rare configurations (for example Windows XP under restrictive group policy), but TrueCrypt also pools many other entropy sources, so Steve considers the risk negligible.
  - AES table lookups may be vulnerable to cache-timing side channels, requiring local attacker code while a volume is mounted; hardware AES instructions would avoid it.
  - Keyfile mixing used a CRC-based construction rather than a cryptographic hash; Steve calls it embarrassing but not exploitable in a realistic scenario.
  - Volume headers were checked only with CRC32 instead of a message authentication code.
- Steve's conclusion: none of the findings are deal killers, forks now have a roadmap for fixes, and he continues to use TrueCrypt himself while expecting it to age out with UEFI Secure Boot.

# Other segments

- Configuration Mania, a Firefox add-on that gives about:config a browsable interface.
- A joke from a listener that alien communications look like noise because they are encrypted, and a mention of a telomere-length saliva test crowdfunding campaign.
- Listener SpinRite story from a 1980s Marine Corps PC that would not boot until SpinRite was run.
- Leo's new phone and the Apple Watch launch; a SQRL mention in a TED talk; a tip on sharing a timestamped YouTube link.
