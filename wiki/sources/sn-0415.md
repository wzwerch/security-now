# Summary

Steve begins with the latest Snowden disclosure, XKeyscore, a Guardian-published slide set describing a globally distributed Linux cluster at over 150 sites that keeps a rolling buffer of roughly three days of unfiltered "full-take" traffic, extracts metadata (email addresses, phone numbers, user activity, files) into an index, and lets analysts run one federated query across all sites without prior authorization. He sees this as confirming his theory that the NSA taps raw feeds upstream of providers rather than relying on provider cooperation. Other news: Microsoft dropped the SkyDrive name after losing a trademark case to British Sky Broadcasting, and UK courts blocked publication of a paper by researchers who had broken the Megamos Crypto RFID immobilizer algorithm used in many Volkswagen-group luxury cars, even though the algorithm had been online since 2009. Steve explains why the DHS "do not click" memo reflects standard rules on classified "spillage". A segment praises Michael Ossmann's HackRF open-source software-defined radio Kickstarter, and Steve spends time raving about Peter F. Hamilton's Void trilogy. The ten listener questions cover Lavabit and email encryption in transit, VPN versus Tor, NSA code contributions such as SELinux, whether the NSA has GRC's certificates, home-made password schemes, third-party cookies in iframes, the Newsroom's prediction of mass surveillance, the Tox.im project, Mailvelope for PGP in webmail, and publishing PGP keys. The episode sets up a multi-week series on PGP and pre-Internet encryption.

# Key points

- XKeyscore: NSA slides describe collecting nearly everything a user does online, a three-day rolling buffer of all unfiltered data, plugins that index emails, phone numbers, files, webmail and chat activity, over 500 to 700 servers at 150 plus sites, and a federated query system. Steve notes the NSA used many program names, which lets officials deny that one named program does something.
- Leo wonders whether the NSA benefits from exaggerating its reach (a panopticon effect); Steve thinks the leak has hurt the intelligence community.
- The House vote to cut funding for bulk phone collection narrowly failed; Steve thought it was premature.
- SkyDrive: after a British court found infringement of the Sky trademark, Microsoft agreed not to appeal and to rename the service.
- Megamos Crypto, used in the transponder keys of VW-group makes such as Porsche and Lamborghini, was cracked by UK and Dutch researchers who gave nine months' notice; a judge ordered details withheld pending trial because the code came from an unauthorized source. Steve notes the flaw is probably in silicon that cannot be updated and that a per-car brute force takes about two days.
- A listener-supplied explanation of the DHS memo: leaked classified documents remain classified until the originator declassifies them, and viewing one on an unclassified system is "spillage" requiring the system to be cleaned; contractors may not even search for the leaked material.
- HackRF (Michael Ossmann): a roughly 200-dollar open-source software-defined radio peripheral able to implement many wireless protocols; Leo adds that a license is required to transmit.
- Lavabit encrypts stored mail so that it is unreadable without the user's password, but mail in transit by SMTP is usually unencrypted and can be downgraded by stripping the STARTTLS announcement; of the major providers, only Google was said to support SMTP encryption. Hence draft-folder sharing within one provider keeps mail off the wire.
- VPN versus Tor: a VPN hides traffic from the local network and ISP efficiently but one server sees both ends; Tor uses multiple hops to resist correlation at the cost of speed.
- The NSA contributes SELinux and publishes hardening guides for many operating systems; Steve recommends taking a secure Unix and locking it down, and compiling code yourself.
- A DigiCert-signed certificate only involves the public key, so the NSA gains nothing from DigiCert's records; the private key never leaves GRC's servers. A rogue CA remains a theoretical worry.
- Home-grown password algorithms leak the scheme when one site's password is exposed; Steve points to his Off The Grid paper system and uses LastPass.
- Third-party cookies are needed when one company's data is displayed in another company's iframe; a server-to-server design would avoid them.
- Mailvelope (Chrome/Firefox, based on OpenPGP.js) adds PGP to webmail. S/MIME predates PGP and is built into email standards.
- PGP keys can be published on key servers (letting others sign them) or on one's own site; Steve's principle is pre-Internet encryption so that transit and storage security no longer matter.

# Other segments

- Steve's SpinRite 6.1 work paused because he read Hamilton's Void trilogy for days; a listener story about SpinRite recovering a whole-disk-encrypted drive.
- Tox.im announced as an open-source Skype alternative, not yet released.
- Leo will be away from mid-September to early October with guest hosts.
- Next week begins a series on PGP.
