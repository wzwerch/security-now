# Summary

Steve first explains how advanced persistent threat groups such as Turla have used satellite-based Internet links since at least 2007 to hide command-and-control servers: malware sends a packet to a satellite-ISP customer address, the satellite rebroadcasts it over a huge footprint, and a passive receiver anywhere in range replies over the normal Internet, creating an untraceable "air gap". Short news items follow: Apple will require developers to justify use of APIs that enable device fingerprinting from iOS 17, Google's unknown-tracker alerts for Android (with confusion over what was postponed), a feasibility study for a seventh U.S. military branch, Russian bills barring citizens from working with unregistered foreign nonprofits (with side effects for open source), and VirusTotal's 2023 report on OneNote, JavaScript/HTML and ISO attachments replacing Office macros. A long feedback section includes satellite-industry listeners, cyber-insurance exclusions for health-site tracking, the Voyager 2 bit-flip fix in 2010 and the July 2023 antenna misaim, and a DHH essay on leaving the cloud. The main topic is TETRA:BURST, five flaws found by Midnight Blue in the TETRA radio standard, including a deliberately weakened export cipher and an unauthenticated time-sync weakness. Steve's conclusion is that secret, home-grown cryptography keeps failing and that legal reverse engineering is essential.

# Key points

- Kaspersky's 2015 "Satellite Turla" report: APT groups hijack downstream-only DVB-S satellite Internet traffic (dish, LNB, a tuner card and a Linux PC suffice) so that the true location of the C2 receiver cannot be found or seized.
- Steve defines APT: advanced means sophisticated, persistent means a lasting foothold (e.g., in printers or edge devices) that periodically phones home.
- Apple's iOS 17 policy: developers must give valid reasons for "required reason" APIs that enable fingerprinting; Leo comments that first-party tracking is the real competitive aim and that personalized advertising's effectiveness is doubtful.
- Android 6.0+ unknown-tracker alerts for AirTag-style trackers began rolling out; the postponed part was Find My Device support for third-party trackers pending an Apple-Google specification.
- The NDAA includes a study on a U.S. Cyber Force as a seventh branch; Army and Air Force have their own cyber teams.
- Russia's parliament passed bills banning participation in unregistered foreign nonprofits, which would incidentally block contributions to open-source projects.
- VirusTotal 2023: email attachments remain the main malware vector; OneNote and HTML-delivered JavaScript grew fastest, ISO images are used as disguised installers, PDF spikes persist, while Excel, RTF, CAB and Word declined.
- Listener report: cyber insurers are excluding claims from web tracking of personal health information, pushing healthcare sites to remove trackers.
- Satellite experts among listeners: about 7,700-8,400 active satellites (90 percent in LEO), jamming is the most common real-world attack, DoD satellites are better protected, and single-event upsets (bit flips) are one reason satellites accept memory-write commands (NASA used this to repair Voyager 2 in 2010).
- Voyager 2's antenna was mispointed by 2 degrees after a July 2023 command sequence; an October 15 reset was expected to restore contact.
- DHH of 37signals/Basecamp argued that cloud rental is a poor deal for steady workloads and moved off AWS; Steve agrees, Leo disagrees.
- TETRA:BURST (Midnight Blue, Black Hat 2023): five flaws in the TETRA radio standard used by police, military and critical infrastructure; the commercial TEA1 cipher reduces its effective key to 32 bits and was cracked in under a minute, and unauthenticated network time broadcasts allow keystream replay and message injection; TEA2 showed no weakness.
- Researchers extracted the secret ciphers from a Motorola radio's secure enclave using zero-days; ETSI disputed the "backdoor" label and had replaced the algorithms with new secret ones; the research was coordinated through the Dutch NCSC.
- Steve stresses that legal reverse engineering is what makes this research possible and that nobody should keep encryption secret or roll their own.
