# Summary

The first episode of the second 500 opens with Steve admitting that he still builds each episode page by hand and that the transcript for 500 was delayed. The news starts with the large DDoS against GreatFire.org and then GitHub, in which an intermediary inside China swapped Baidu's analytics script for code that made visitors' browsers around the world flood two GitHub-hosted anti-censorship pages. Next is a hotel and conference router family (ANTlabs InnGate) that exposed an unauthenticated rsync service with full file-system access, found on a few hundred devices in many countries. Steve then walks through a WP Engine analysis of about 10 million leaked passwords. The ten listener questions cover recovering files after CryptoWall, Windows auto-sharing of Wi-Fi credentials, how brute force knows it has succeeded, UEFI recovery and Linux dual boot, the miniLock encryption tool, Firefox tracking protection, UEFI versus drive-firmware attacks, how to safely send links in company email, and why users cannot easily prune certificate authorities. A closing aside finds Bank of America preferring RC4 in its TLS cipher order.

# Key points

- GitHub suffered what it called its largest DDoS ever: a man-in-the-middle replaced Baidu Analytics JavaScript in transit so that visitors to many sites unknowingly requested two anti-censorship projects on GitHub (greatfire and a Chinese New York Times mirror). Traffic hit about 2.6 billion requests per hour; GreatFire.org, hosted on Amazon's CDN, faced large pay-as-you-go bills. Steve suspects Chinese authorities and notes browsers could rate-limit or block such behaviour.
- ANTlabs InnGate routers used in hotels and convention centres exposed an rsync daemon on TCP 873 with the whole file system readable and writable without authentication (reported by Cylance; 277 reachable devices in 29 countries). Impact: traffic sniffing and malware injection against guests. Advice: use a VPN on hotel and open Wi-Fi; sites must use HTTPS for the whole session.
- WP Engine's password analysis: 123456 and password lead; when forced to add a digit, almost a quarter of users append "1"; keyboard-walk patterns such as qwerty and phone-keypad patterns are common. Steve: we must move away from passwords.
- Steve recommends running SpinRite on new drives at Level 2, because factories do not read the assembled drive end to end; he also notes denser multi-level-cell flash stores more voltage levels per cell.
- CryptoWall: a listener recovered many files from an infected drive imaged beforehand with an undelete tool because the malware deleted rather than overwrote the originals; recovery is partial.
- Windows 8 and 10 preview builds can share saved Wi-Fi credentials across a user's linked devices; Steve worries that an infected device could reach the network, and recommends a separate guest network.
- Brute-force decryption works because proper encryption is authenticated, so a wrong password is detected immediately.
- UEFI databases live in motherboard non-volatile memory, not on the disk; signed recovery media still boots, and mainstream Linux distributions work with Secure Boot.
- miniLock (by Nadim Kobeissi): browser-based file encryption using Curve25519, where an email address plus a passphrase of at least 100 bits entropy deterministically produces the key pair; public keys are short enough to tweet; supports multiple recipients and hides identities in the file.
- Firefox tracking protection (built on the Disconnect block list) can be enabled via a hidden setting before it appears in the UI in Firefox 39; it blocks trackers rather than ads and reportedly speeds page loads.
- Secure Boot does not protect against drive-firmware rewrites such as the Equation Group's, since those happen after boot.
- Telling staff not to click email links cannot be solved with a symbol or padlock; Steve advises giving a fixed internal web page address or search phrase and following the maxim to only do things you went looking for.
- Users can prune their trusted certificate authority list to about 30 to 40 CAs without much breakage, but Steve does not recommend it for ordinary users; Chrome already detects fraudulent Google certificates.
- Leo found that BankofAmerica.com listed the weak RC4 cipher first; Steve says RC4 and SHA-1 certificates are being phased out and that he will keep a SHA-1 certificate through 2015 and then move to SHA-2.
