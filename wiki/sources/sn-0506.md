# Summary

The episode begins with the so-called Pixie Dust finding that WPS PIN setup on several router families can be cracked offline because they use predictable or zero values where unpredictable nonces are required; Steve recommends turning WPS off. He relays Mozilla's announced intent to phase out non-secure HTTP by withholding new browser features from plain-HTTP sites, and Leo objects that this feels heavy-handed. A CloudFlare post on JavaScript-based DDoS prompts a discussion of how scripts are injected through ads, compromised sites, shared libraries or man-in-the-middle tampering of plain HTTP, and of the W3C Subresource Integrity proposal that lets pages pin a hash for a loaded script. Steve also describes a spyware strain with extreme anti-analysis and self-destruct behaviour, tells of a failed two-disk RAID mirror at his office that SpinRite recovered, and then covers the House oversight hearing on encryption. Matt Blaze's written testimony argues that software cannot be made reliably correct, that key escrow adds unquantifiable risk, and that law-enforcement access schemes would be costly and abuse-prone; Jonathan Mayer's arguments show such mandates are practically unenforceable on open platforms. Steve notes the FBI witness denied wanting a backdoor while asking for guaranteed access.

# Key points

- WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup) was already weak in 2012 due to its split PIN check; the new research by a security researcher found real routers using zero, linearly generated, or time-based values for the supposedly secret nonces (Ralink, Broadcom with eCos, Realtek examples), letting an attacker recover the PIN from one exchange. Advice: disable WPS.
- Firefox disabled RC4 by default in recent versions with a whitelist; other browsers can also be configured.
- Mozilla's Firefox security lead announced plans to phase out non-secure HTTP by making new features available only to HTTPS sites; the blog drew hundreds of mostly heated comments.
- Steve explains that plain HTTP lets an in-path attacker simply insert a script tag; with HTTPS an attacker needs a trusted certificate, and canaries such as Chrome make forged certificates risky.
- Cisco's analysis of a targeted spyware family: thousands of unused functions, huge write loops to break sandbox logging, constant self-hashing, and a destructive fallback (overwriting the boot record or encrypting home-folder files with discarded keys) if it detects analysis.
- CloudFlare's "JavaScript-based DDoS" post: attackers need many visitors, so they target popular sites via malicious ads, injected pages, or widely linked libraries; jQuery's site was compromised the previous year.
- Subresource Integrity: a script tag can carry an integrity attribute with a hash that the browser verifies before running; it guards against a tampered library even over HTTPS; Chrome and Firefox implementations were in progress.
- Steve published the edited House hearing video on a new YouTube channel and recommended Video ReDo for frame-accurate MPEG cuts.
- Steve's SpinRite story: an office mirrored RAID card had warned "critical" for a long time, the user pressed Escape each boot, the second disk then failed, and SpinRite recovered the remaining one in about three hours; he wants a "refuse to boot when degraded" RAID option for non-experts.
- Matt Blaze's testimony, part 1: digital systems are increasingly vulnerable because computer science cannot build complex large software with reliably correct behaviour; the practical defences are encryption and minimal attack surface.
- Part 2: law-enforcement access requirements (key escrow, as in the Clipper Chip he broke in 1994) destroy end-to-end design, create new points of failure, require expensive 24-hour secure operation across thousands of agencies, and invite fraudulent requests by hostile nations; all earlier proposals later showed unanticipated weaknesses.
- Hearing dynamics: an FBI representative said they were not asking for a backdoor but for an access mechanism, a prosecutor failed to give an example of a crime unsolved for lack of phone access, and a panelist observed that strong encryption is the expected public reaction to surveillance overreach.
- Jonathan Mayer's argument: even if Android or Google were forced to provide decryption, apps, sideloading, web pages and users could apply their own strong encryption, so enforcement would be impractical and would resemble the failed War on Drugs by pushing the behaviour underground.
