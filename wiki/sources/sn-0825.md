# Summary

The episode begins with a bug-bounty success story: hackers testing a Russian site used Edge's built-in page translator and found it failed to sanitize HTML image tags, creating a universal cross-site scripting hole that Microsoft rewarded with $20,000 and fixed in three weeks. Steve revisits Google's FLoC, now delayed to about late 2023 after rejection by every other browser and new EU and UK competition probes, and argues Firefox's per-site cookie partitioning is the best option available. Ireland's health service estimated total recovery from the Conti attack at about $600M (immediate costs $120M) even though a free decryptor was supplied. The Western Digital My Book Live and Live Duo NAS devices were remotely factory-reset en masse; the devices had been out of support since 2015, had an unpatched 2018 command-execution flaw, and about 55,000 were still exposed; Steve places the responsibility mostly on users who lacked backups. He notes John McAfee's death in a Spanish jail and recalls a phone call with him. Long segment on Windows 11 requirements (TPM 2.0, CPU lists, PC Health Check, WhyNotWin11), plus tool tips (Ctrl+L, AutoIt, Cyotek WebCopy, a Packt cybersecurity Humble Bundle) and listener feedback explaining that TPM-held keys can be sniffed on the wire.

# Key points

- Edge translator UXSS: Cyber Xplore researchers, hunting bounties on Mail.ru, found Edge's built-in translation did not sanitize HTML image tags, allowing script to run in the origin of any translated page (including Windows Store web apps). Reported June 3, bounty $20,000 (Edge on Chromium program), patched June 24. Steve contrasts this with Microsoft's months-long ProxyLogon handling and encourages hands-on testing.
- FLoC: no other browser (Brave, Edge, Opera, Vivaldi, Firefox) adopted it; Amazon blocks it on its properties; Google pushed wider rollout out to roughly late 2023 amid the European Commission and UK CMA investigations of Privacy Sandbox. Steve still prefers cohorts to third-party cookies but notes that IP addresses and fingerprinting dominate tracking anyway; Firefox's Strict mode partitions third-party cookies per site.
- Ireland HSE: the Conti ransomware gang ignored the Irish High Court injunction; the director general put recovery at $120M immediately and about $600M including upgrades and a new security operations center; 75% of servers were decrypted using the decryptor Conti supplied a week after the attack; Steve is skeptical of the figure.
- Western Digital My Book Live and Live Duo: attackers abused a remote command-execution flaw (CVE-2018-18472, root RCE via the language configuration API parameter, unauthenticated) to install a PowerPC Linux trojan and, in some cases, trigger a factory reset that wiped data. Devices were introduced in 2010, last updated in 2015; roughly 55,348 were visible on the internet. Steve suggests two botnet groups were fighting and device owners were collateral damage; lessons are to stop exposing unsupported devices and keep multiple backups. Leo notes the devices were still on sale.
- John McAfee found dead in a Barcelona jail cell on the eve of extradition to the US on tax charges; Steve recalls an early call about computer viruses that preceded McAfee's antivirus launch in 1987.
- Windows 11 requirements confusion: Steve's Intel NUC (Skylake i7-6700HQ, TPM 2.0) was flagged as unsupported; Microsoft says it set requirements for the Insider preview except TPM 2.0 and CPU family/model and will adjust later. Steve proposes requiring security features to be enabled rather than blocking older hardware, and doubts Microsoft's claim of up to a 60% malware reduction with TPM-backed features. Leo explains Secure Boot and TPM roles; Windows 10 support runs to 2025.
- WhyNotWin11 (a community checker app) is poorly maintained and unsigned; Steve prefers Microsoft's PC Health Check eventually. He discovered AutoIt as a free scripting/compilation tool.
- Browser tip: Ctrl+L selects the URL bar. Tool tip: Cyotek WebCopy clones websites locally (Steve cloned the Ralf Brown Interrupt List).
- Listener feedback: the "commercial providers" remark last week likely means zero-days sold by commercial exploit vendors. On whether TPM holds secrets against the NSA, Steve says no for a discrete TPM chip because its bus signals can be captured and decoded with logic analyzers.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a mock-up of a Windows 95 PC failing the Windows 11 health check ("processor isn't supported").
- Humble Bundle: Packt Cybersecurity 2021 bundle (shortcut grc.sc/bundle) with up to 24 ebooks for $18.
- Steve replaced a swollen iPhone X battery for $69 and talks about lithium-ion swelling; Leo's Pixel 4 XL battery swelled; both note the iPhone's 14th birthday.
