# Summary

Steve examines CISA's known exploited vulnerabilities catalog, questioning the value of adding obscure and years-old entries such as a 2018 Zimbra XSS used against Ukraine, a three-year-old WhatsApp VoIP stack overflow and a February Print Spooler elevation bug, while still agreeing that agencies must patch. ESET found three Lenovo UEFI vulnerabilities across more than 100 consumer laptop models, two of them caused by manufacturing-time drivers named SecureBackDoor left in production firmware, which let a privileged process disable SPI flash protections or Secure Boot; older models will not be fixed. Check Point showed the web version of the Ever Surf crypto wallet used a constant "unknown" value as salt and a six-digit PIN, so wallet keys could be brute-forced in under an hour. A Java ECDSA verification flaw in versions 15 to 18 let attackers forge signatures for TLS, JWTs, SAML and WebAuthn; Oracle rated it 7.5 while ForgeRock called it a 10. Listener feedback covers why a freed-memory wipe does not stop use-after-free, why cheap IoT devices stay unpatched, and why login pages ask for the email first. Steve reports a SpinRite milestone: its own universal native drivers now work on all tested hardware. The main topic is Mandiant's decade of data showing zero-day exploitation jumped to 80 in 2021, with Chinese groups leading and ransomware actors now involved.

# Key points

- CISA catalog: contains only exploited bugs, but Steve notes old entries (Zimbra XSS from 2018 used against Ukrainian government mail, WhatsApp VoIP CVSS 9.8 from 2019) show a long tail of unpatched software and argues the list loses force as it grows; exploitation of patched bugs follows quickly after reverse engineering of patches.
- Lenovo (ESET report): CVE-2021-3970 (SMM memory corruption with arbitrary SMRAM read/write), CVE-2021-3971 (SecureBackDoor driver disables SPI flash write protection) and CVE-2021-3972 (ChgBootDxeHook disables UEFI Secure Boot); reported 11 October 2021, confirmed 17 November; over 100 models affected including IdeaPad 3, Legion 5 Pro, Yoga Slim 9; IdeaPad 330 and 110 are past support and will stay vulnerable; exploitation could enable persistent UEFI implants like LoJax; Insyde, HP and Dell firmware had more than 50 UEFI flaws that year.
- Context: BIOS began as masked ROM, then flash, and UEFI added power and attack surface (Leo notes enterprise management drives complexity); Lenovo ships software that keeps firmware updated, and OS updaters increasingly carry firmware.
- Ever Surf web wallet (Everscale): the DeviceInfo.getUniqueId call is undefined in a browser, so it returns the literal string "unknown" as the hash salt; PIN of six digits, brute-force speed of 95 per second, worst case about 175 minutes (38 minutes in test) to recover keys and seed phrase from local storage; the web version was shut down; Steve says private keys do not belong in a browser.
- Java ECDSA bug (Neil Madden, ForgeRock): affects JDK 15, 16, 17, 18 before the April 2022 Critical Patch Update; forged signatures can defeat SSL handshakes, JWT, SAML, OIDC and WebAuthn verification for servers running Java; Oracle rated 7.5, ForgeRock 10; versions 15 and 16 were already out of support.
- Use-after-free feedback: zeroing freed memory does not help because the danger is the stale pointer that now points at reused memory.
- IoT patching (via Bruce Schneier): teams that build cheap devices disband, leaving nobody to push patches, so regulation may be the only remedy; Steve argues proprietary code relies on "trust us" and on researchers reverse engineering it, and compares voting machines.
- Email-first login: many apps need the email to choose an identity provider or workflow (Okta, Duo, password, YubiKey), explaining the pattern; Google's email also reveals existence through bounce messages.
- SpinRite: after many months, new universal native drivers (IDE/PATA and AHCI/SATA) work on all testers' hardware without the BIOS; the I/O abstraction will support USB and NVMe later; data recovery should be much faster because BIOS resets are avoided; future versions should run on Macs.
- Mandiant zero-day data (exploited before a patch): counts by year 2012 to 2021 were 2, 3, 8, 15, 21, 17, 16, 32, 30 and 80; nearly one third of 2021 actors were financially motivated; Chinese groups led (ProxyLogon/Hafnium exploitation of Exchange); Russia's APT28 returned with an Excel zero-day; at least six 2021 zero-days were used by customers of commercial spyware vendors including NSO Group and QuaDream.
- Vendors: Microsoft, Apple and Google made up about 75 percent of 2021 zero-days; others in the top 12 include Accellion, SonicWall, Apache, Qualcomm, Trend Micro, Adobe, Linux kernel, Pulse Secure and SolarWinds; Flash caused about 20 percent of zero-days from 2012 to 2017.
- Causes named: more software and devices, a zero-day broker market, better detection, and exhausted low-hanging fruit; ransomware money now buys zero-days, while exploit kits stopped including them.
- Advice: Mandiant advocates prioritizing actively exploited bugs; Steve thinks that is unrealistic and recommends simply keeping everything patched routinely, because patches are reverse engineered and attacked quickly.
