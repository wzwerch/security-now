# Summary

Steve leads with "regreSSHion", CVE-2024-6387, a signal-handler race condition in OpenSSH's sshd that Qualys found. It is a regression of a 2006 fix reintroduced in OpenSSH 8.5p1 (October 2020), affects the default configuration on glibc-based Linux, and can give unauthenticated remote root. Exploitation is slow (around 10,000 attempts, hours to about a week depending on settings), but about 14 million exposed servers were identified, and OpenBSD is not affected. He advises patching or blocking SSH exposure. He then covers a Satoshi-era 50 BTC wallet moving after 14 years (with a wry note about his own lost 50 coins), a Voyager 1 recovery update, and the state of GRC's email system, from which he has removed all open and click tracking and which uses the nuevoMailer package he recommends. Listener topics include slow Syncthing relay servers (fixed by opening direct connections), how GRC uses DNS records in the 239.x.x.x range for freeware release checks, and another reading of Recall. The main story is the Chrome Root Program's decision to stop trusting new Entrust TLS certificates after a years-long pattern of compliance failures and stonewalling of Mozilla and Google.

# Key points

- CVE-2024-6387 (regreSSHion): sshd's SIGALRM handler, triggered when a client fails to authenticate within LoginGraceTime, calls async-signal-unsafe functions such as syslog (which uses malloc/free); the race can corrupt the heap and yield root code execution. Affects OpenSSH 8.5p1 up to the fix and older than 4.4p1 if unpatched; Qualys demonstrated 32-bit exploitation on Debian in roughly 1 week, 1-2 days or 6-8 hours depending on version and MaxStartups, and had begun work on 64-bit. Qualys counts around 700,000 exposed instances among its own customers and 14 million per Shodan/Censys. Mitigation: update, or restrict/disable exposed SSH.
- A dormant 2010 bitcoin mining wallet moved 50 BTC to Binance; Steve notes his own 50 BTC from February 2011 are long gone and recalls his 2011 episode on Bitcoin.
- Voyager 1: normal science operations resumed after the November 2023 fault, with all four instruments returning data; the probe is about 15 billion miles away and approaching 47 years of operation.
- GRC email: nearly 5,000 subscribers; automation of the weekly mailing nearly done; tracking pixels and link redirects removed; Steve recommends nuevoMailer (one-time cost $169, open PHP, author Panos) via the grc.sc/981 shortcut. Early stats showed three times more clicks on the Picture of the Week than the show notes.
- Errata: Kim Zetter, the Wired author quoted last week, is a woman.
- Syncthing: relay servers have become slow (about 1.5 Mbit/s for Steve); opening firewall ports to the specific remote public IPs restored direct connections and instant sync.
- Steve's freeware update check: each product has a DNS name under rel.grc.com whose A record (239.0.0.N, a non-routable multicast block) encodes the current release number; clients compare it to their own, giving anonymous, cached, load-spreading version checks.
- Recall: a listener suspects it began as an employee-monitoring idea; Steve calls the feature ethically neutral but thinks Microsoft will use it for local AI training and ad profiling; Leo notes Microsoft Viva already provides workplace analytics.
- Entrust: Chrome 127+ will distrust TLS server certificates chaining to nine Entrust roots whose earliest Signed Certificate Timestamp is after 31 October 2024; existing certificates keep working. Mozilla had asked for a root-cause report after about 18 incidents in four months; Entrust's answers were late and evasive, and it refused to stop issuing or revoke about 26,668 EV certificates missing a CPS URI. Community members (Amir Omidi, Watson Ladd) criticised the response; Steve sees arrogance and private-equity ownership (Thoma Bravo) as context, and notes Entrust is only about 0.1% of certificates, with Let's Encrypt around half. Upcoming ballot SC-75 would make pre-issuance linting mandatory from March 2025.
