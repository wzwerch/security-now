# Summary

Steve closes the Marcus Hutchins saga: the researcher who stopped WannaCry by registering its kill-switch domain was sentenced to time served with a year of supervised release for malware he wrote as a teenager. US Attorney General William Barr argued that "warrant-proof" encryption must yield to lawful access, and Steve and Leo discuss which technical approaches are plausible (provider-managed messaging versus end-to-end TLS). A Recorded Future study of underground forums shows demand centers on older, openly available malware, while high-end ransomware such as Ryuk is not sold. Four Louisiana school districts were hit by ransomware, prompting a state of emergency that also protects against price gouging on response services. Three NAS stories (QNAP ransomware via weak credentials, a Synology warning about dictionary attacks, and Iomega/LenovoEMC StorCenter's unauthenticated API exposing millions of files) lead to advice never to give a NAS a public address. The main topic is Armis's Urgent/11: eleven vulnerabilities in the IPnet TCP/IP stack in Wind River's VxWorks, six enabling remote code execution, present across about two billion devices and bypassing NAT and firewalls.

# Key points

- Marcus Hutchins (MalwareTech) sentenced July 26, 2019 to time served; the judge weighed that the WannaCry disruption he helped stop was far more damaging than his own earlier malware.
- Barr's speech: says technical solutions for lawful access exist without materially weakening security; Steve notes today's point-to-point encryption primitives do not support third-party decryption, though provider-managed systems like iMessage could add a participant; Leo expects endpoints and phone makers to be targeted rather than open tools like Signal.
- Recorded Future "Bestsellers in the Underground Economy": analysis of 3.9 million forum posts; popular malware is older, open or cracked; many ransomware posts are requests or sales of generic ransomware by low-level actors.
- Louisiana: governor declared a state of emergency after three (then four) school districts were hit just before school resumed, activating cyber response by the National Guard and state agencies and invoking price-gouging protections.
- QNAP NAS ransomware (Anomali): Go-based, under 400 lines, spread through brute-forced weak credentials and known flaws, appends ".encrypt", low antivirus detection; recommended restricting external access, patching, strong credentials.
- Synology (July 23 advisory): attacks used botnet-sourced dictionary attacks on admin accounts, not vulnerabilities; advice includes disabling default admin, strong passwords, Auto Block and Security Advisor; Leo describes his hardening.
- Iomega/LenovoEMC: unauthenticated API on older StorCenter NAS units exposed over 3 million files totalling 36 TB, found via Shodan by Vertical Structure; Lenovo released a patch.
- Urgent/11 (Armis): 11 flaws in VxWorks IPnet, six critical for remote code execution, five causing denial of service, information leaks or logic errors; includes IPv4 options handling and four TCP Urgent Pointer bugs and a DHCP-related issue; three flaws date to the 2006 Interpeak acquisition; affects devices such as SonicWall firewalls, Dell PowerConnect switches, Linksys WRT54G v5+, Drobo, plus medical, industrial and automotive systems; a patch was available about a month earlier and details came before a Black Hat talk on August 8.
- Steve's lesson: closed, unaudited network stack code went unscrutinized for 13 years.
