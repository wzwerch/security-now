# Summary

Steve starts with TsuNAME, a DNS misconfiguration (cyclic NS dependencies) that made some recursive resolvers, including Google Public DNS and Cisco OpenDNS, loop and hammer authoritative servers; researchers fixed the resolvers and released the CycleHunter detection tool. Google's announcement about "automatically enrolling" users in two-step verification was so vaguely worded that the press misreported it. Qualys disclosed 21 vulnerabilities ("21 Nails") in Exim, which runs roughly 60% of internet mail servers, ten of them remote, many dating to 2004; Steve urges immediate patching and expects heavy exploitation. Researcher Nusenu's tracking of malicious Tor exit relays showed one actor controlling up to about a quarter of exit capacity and rewriting unencrypted traffic, which leads Steve to advise pairing Tor with a VPN and HTTPS. After a Project Hail Mary recommendation and listener notes, the main story is the DarkSide ransomware-as-a-service attack on Colonial Pipeline, which shut down a pipeline carrying about 45% of East Coast fuel and triggered state emergencies. Steve describes DarkSide's business-like persona, its "ethics" rules and fast Salsa20/RSA-1024 encryption, and concludes that the incident is a wake-up call for critical infrastructure.

# Key points

- TsuNAME: cyclic dependencies between name server records make naive resolvers loop (up to 5,600 queries per second observed; two misconfigured .nz domains raised .nz authoritative traffic about 50%). Described already in RFC 1536 (1993). Google DNS and OpenDNS were fixed; Unbound, BIND, Knot, Quad9 and Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 passed. CycleHunter lets zone operators check.
- Google blog post by Mark Risher on World Password Day ("soon we'll start automatically enrolling users in 2SV if appropriately configured") was unclear; Leo suspects it refers to phone-prompt sign-in, similar to Microsoft's number-matching.
- Exim "21 Nails" (Qualys): 11 local and 10 remote flaws found by source audit, affecting versions since 2004, with working exploitation of three RCEs and four local privilege escalations (exploits not published but advisory detailed enough). Past Exim abuse: a 2019 Linux worm, Watchbog, and NSA-attributed Sandworm activity. Distribution packages patched; embedded appliances are a worry.
- Tor exit risk: exit relay sees plaintext; Nusenu documented a malicious actor running up to about 27.5% of exit capacity (peak January 2021) performing SSL stripping, bitcoin address rewriting, and potentially download tampering; countermeasures are HSTS preloading, HTTPS-only modes and a VPN.
- Steve's view: Tor must be used with awareness that exits may be malicious or monitored.
- DarkSide: first appeared August 2020, ransoms typically $200K-2M, Salsa20 plus RSA-1024, double extortion with leak pages, press-release style communications and a self-declared "ethics" list (no hospitals, schools, non-profits, government); after Colonial Pipeline they announced vetting of affiliates' targets.
- Colonial Pipeline: shut down operations on May 7 after an IT-side ransomware attack; 5,500 miles, 2.5 million barrels per day (about 45% of East Coast fuel); emergency declarations in DC and several states (Virginia, North Carolina); the FBI confirmed DarkSide.
- Steve connects this to last week's Ransomware Task Force and stresses lack of redundancy ("monoculture, mono pipeline").
- Listener notes: OpenMediaVault can replace QNAP firmware on QNAP hardware; a listener reported Scripps Health still on paper charts weeks after its attack.
