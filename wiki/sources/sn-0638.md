# Summary

The episode begins with Windows 1.0's 32nd anniversary and the FCC's plan to end Net Neutrality enforcement, to be voted on 14 December. Intel published a security review of the Management Engine, Server Platform Services and Trusted Execution Engine, a list of affected firmware versions and a Windows/Linux detection tool; vendors such as Lenovo began posting firmware updates. Researcher Chris Vickery found three exposed Amazon S3 buckets belonging to US military commands that held billions of scraped social-media posts. Quartz reported that Android phones sent cell-tower IDs to Google even with location off, which Google said it would stop. Princeton researchers documented session-replay scripts that record keystrokes and mouse movement on hundreds of top sites. Spain's 60 million Infineon-based ID cards are now untrustworthy, BlueBorne turned out to affect Echo and Google Home, and the Amazon Key camera could be disabled by a rogue courier. Firefox 57 "Quantum" is praised, SpinRite's error-correction handling is explained, and the main topic is the new Quad9 DNS service at 9.9.9.9. Leo closes with early news of Uber's concealed 2016 breach.

# Key points

- Windows 1.0 reached release to manufacturing on 20 November 1985; the group recalls Windows 3 as the first usable version.
- FCC proposal to roll back Net Neutrality and preempt state rules; vote expected to pass 3-2.
- Intel: affected ME firmware versions 11.x, SPS 4.0 and TXE 3.0 (about nine years of chipsets); an attacker could impersonate the ME, run code invisible to the OS or crash the system. Intel released a detection tool; OEMs must supply patches. Steve still wonders whether JTAG over USB was a bug or a design feature.
- Exposed AWS S3 buckets named like "centcom-backup" and "pacom-archive" reportedly held at least 1.8 billion scraped posts; officials said access was "unauthorized" and added protections.
- Android sent nearby cell tower IDs for push-message sync even with location permissions denied and no SIM; Google said the data was discarded and ended the request by end of November. Steve advises a low expectation of privacy on any phone.
- Session replay scripts (Princeton Freedom to Tinker): seven vendors including Yandex, FullStory, Hotjar, UserReplay, Smartlook, Clicktale and SessionCam appear on 482 of the top 50,000 sites, capturing typed text before submission; EasyList and uBlock Origin now block them.
- Infineon fallout: Spain issued about 60 million cards, now considered unusable; cost to factor a key fell to about $2,000. Steve argues buyers should demand independent security review.
- BlueBorne also affected roughly 20 million Echo and Google Home devices (patched automatically); Steve's moral is that anything with firmware must update itself.
- Amazon Key flaw (Rhino Labs): a courier with a laptop could knock the Cloud Cam off Wi-Fi so it freezes on the last frame, allowing undetected re-entry; Amazon planned faster offline alerts, but real prevention needs local video caching.
- Firefox 57 Quantum: new Stylo CSS engine in Rust, multicore use, claimed to be about twice as fast and lighter on memory than Chrome.
- Quad9 (9.9.9.9): free anycast recursive DNS from IBM Security, Packet Clearing House and the Global Cyber Alliance; blocks known-malicious domains using IBM X-Force and 18 partner feeds with a top-million whitelist; no personally identifiable data stored; DNSSEC validation; 70 locations at launch growing to 160; Steve's DNS Benchmark ranked it at the top. 9.9.9.10 is an unfiltered variant. Steve plans to set his router to use it.
- Leo reports Uber paid hackers $100,000 to hide a 2016 breach of 57 million accounts and fired its security chief.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: HR rejecting a security candidate who said too many scary things.
- ShieldsUP! is about to pass 100 million uses; bitcoin at roughly $8,200.
- SpinRite: error correction is bypassed during surface analysis and re-enabled for last-chance recovery; a failing flash drive can be revived only so many times.
