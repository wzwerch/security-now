# Summary

The show opens with retailers (Walmart-backed MCX and others) rejecting Apple Pay in favour of their own QR-code CurrentC app, which Steve and Leo see as a poor and privacy-invasive design with an unknown security track record. The main news is that Verizon and AT&T insert a persistent, unblockable tracking header (UIDH) into unencrypted outbound mobile traffic; advertisers can send it to Verizon to look up data about subscribers, and it survives cookie deletion. Steve verified it on his phones and notes that HTTPS or a VPN prevents insertion. He then covers Ron Rivest's Spritz, a one-line redesign of RC4 that restores its strength, and shares the iPad Air 2's disappointing battery life. The Q&A includes why unique MAC addresses matter for enterprises, why $4.80 certificates exist and who should get an HTTPS certificate, why DNSSEC is rarely used, a clarification that Adam Langley's claim about running JavaScript in any origin is wrong except where cross-origin rules are explicitly relaxed, why SSL 3.0 persists, a correct statement that OpenVPN does not leak the original headers, how FIDO U2F keys keep a symmetric secret and store wrapped private keys at the relying party, and SpinRite on iPod classic drives.

# Key points

- CurrentC (MCX): a QR-code, bank-account-based app wanted by large retailers to avoid card fees and collect customer data; the retailers reportedly agreed to block Apple Pay. Security details were undisclosed and a similar scheme had been abandoned in China.
- Verizon "Precision ID" and AT&T add a UIDH header to non-HTTPS mobile requests; the identifier is sticky for days or weeks, cannot be deleted by the user, bridges cookie clearing, and is sold to advertisers. Opting out of the monetisation program does not remove the tag. Steve checked with Wi-Fi turned off on both carriers; T-Mobile was not doing it at the time. HTTPS, VPN or cellular-only check pages prove or prevent it.
- Spritz: Rivest and Schuldt's revision of the RC4 stream cipher adds one assignment step and a stride variable to the 25-year-old design; it is slower than RC4 but extremely small, suited to constrained devices and simple code. Bruce Schneier is quoted noting RC4's best attacks were at the edge of practicality.
- iPad Air 2: thinner but shorter battery life, which Steve dislikes; owners of the original Air need not upgrade.
- Listener coinage: "spun," "SpinRighted" and "SpinRotten" for drives after SpinRite.
- MAC addresses are valuable in enterprises for asset management and Wake-on-LAN; Steve clarifies that the iOS 8 randomisation discussion applies only to passive Wi-Fi scanning.
- Low-cost certificates: a reseller sold domain-validated certificates for about 5 dollars a year, largely because multi-year terms and automated validation make them cheap; higher-assurance EV certificates take days of manual validation. Steve is glad HTTPS is becoming affordable but stays with DigiCert; a small business without e-commerce should ask its host to enable HTTPS.
- DNSSEC adoption lags because DNS works without it; it could someday allow certificate verification through DNS, and Steve notes that China's CNNIC being a trusted root shows the weakness of the CA system.
- POODLE correction: Adam Langley's blog statement that an attacker can run JavaScript in any origin to send authenticated requests is not true under the same-origin policy except where servers or extensions deliberately allow cross-origin access.
- SSL 3.0 stays because old clients (such as XP) still use it; Mozilla lost about 10 percent of downloads when it tried disabling it.
- A tunnel such as OpenVPN encrypts the entire inner packet including headers, so the ISP sees only traffic to the VPN endpoint.
- FIDO U2F: the key holds one master secret and each registration key pair is generated in the device, with the private key encrypted by that secret and stored at the website, then returned to the device for signing during login; Steve contrasts this with SQRL, which gives servers no secrets.
- Steve updates iOS quickly but holds back on Windows because upgrades are heavier, though he notes the iOS 8.0.1 update bricked some phones.
- SpinRite can revive iPod classic hard drives but the drive must be removed and attached to a PC.
