# Summary

Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte call the week "Inching Forward". DigiCert agreed to buy Symantec's certificate authority business for about $950 million in cash plus a 30 percent stake; Steve, a DigiCert customer, sees trusted stewardship replacing a distrusted owner. LogMeIn doubled LastPass Premium to $24 a year; Steve is staying. Marcus Hutchins, who stopped WannaCry, was arrested after DEF CON and charged over the Kronos banking trojan; legal experts called the indictment thin and the EFF is looking into the case. TrickBot gained SMBv1 propagation, and Bitdefender found about 175,000 exposed cameras with default logins and overflow bugs. A bipartisan Senate bill would set IoT security rules for government purchases. Also covered: physical rooting of 2015-16 Amazon Echoes, the Vault 7 Dumbo tool, Mozilla's encrypted Send service, and a complaint against Hotspot Shield VPN.

# Key points

- DigiCert-Symantec: roughly $950 million cash plus 30 percent of DigiCert; closes in a later fiscal quarter; DigiCert's CEO said the deal should satisfy browser makers' concerns about Symantec trust. Steve notes he has no inside knowledge but argues the transfer moves a distrusted asset to a trusted operator; DigiCert pre-validates EV renewals, among other conveniences.
- LastPass Premium rose from $12 to $24 per year after the LogMeIn acquisition; the free tier remains useful; Steve prefers paying for service-based software but dislikes subscriptions for software with no ongoing cost.
- Marcus Hutchins ("MalwareTech") was arrested in Las Vegas on charges of creating and selling Kronos in 2014-15, pleaded not guilty; a law professor called the charges a stretch absent proof of criminal intent; colleagues vouched for his work with law enforcement; a legal defense fund was set up. Steve and Leo stress the chilling effect on researchers.
- TrickBot (Flashpoint): added WannaCry-style SMBv1 spreading within LANs using server enumeration and LDAP; admins should disable SMBv1.
- Bitdefender: about 175,000 Shenzhen Neo security cameras (iDoorbell, NIP-22) are exposed via UPnP, with user/user and guest/guest defaults and stack overflows; Steve explains the proper design is an outbound UDP keepalive to a rendezvous server rather than opening inbound ports.
- Internet of Things Cybersecurity Improvement Act of 2017 (Senators Warner and Gardner): government-purchased IoT devices must have no hardcoded credentials, be patchable with signed updates, use standard encryption and have timely vulnerability fixes.
- MWR researchers could root 2015-2016 Amazon Echoes by attaching to debug pads under the rubber base and booting from SD to stream microphone audio; requires physical access; 2017 models mitigated it.
- Vault 7 "Dumbo": Windows tool to disable cameras, microphones and network adapters near a surveillance site and delete recordings; needs physical USB and system privileges.
- Mozilla Send (send.firefox.com): files up to about 1 GB are encrypted in the browser, uploaded, and shared as a link whose decryption key lives in the URL fragment and is never sent to the server; links expire after a day or one download. Steve endorses it.
- Hotspot Shield VPN: Center for Democracy and Technology filed an FTC complaint saying the free app logs connections, injects ads via iframes, uses tracking libraries, leaks identifiers and redirects e-commerce traffic.
- SpinRite insight: a drive's ECC and seek-error counts correlate with how long a scan takes; compare run times across drives. SpinRite 6 can run in a tiny DOS VM against an external drive.
- Listener items: it is data centers, not enterprises, that want TLS monitoring; ransomware usually only encrypts but a compromised system cannot be fully trusted; Steve now uses CloudBerry for encrypted S3 backups; a human-readable paper output would suit voting machines better than barcodes.
