# Summary

Steve explains why he will not rely on Face ID for security after early reports of lookalike and deliberate spoofing, arguing that faces are more public than fingerprints and that his phone is the gateway to his authenticator and password manager. A researcher's AVGator attack, combining NTFS directory junctions, antivirus quarantine restore and the DLL search path, led to fixes in several products. WikiLeaks' Vault 8 leak of the CIA's Hive command-and-control source is described, followed by renewed debate over device encryption after a Texas church shooting. A video-conference static-band data exfiltration technique, three Chrome anti-malvertising measures, and Positive Technologies' revelation that Intel chipsets since Skylake expose a JTAG debugging interface over USB (Direct Connect Interface) are discussed. Research shows nearly all of almost 2,500 cryptojacking sites serve just three operators, and WebAssembly is now in every major browser. After listener questions, the main segment walks through Bruce Schneier's House testimony on Equifax: the breach was avoidable, data brokers are numerous and opaque, existing law is inadequate, markets cannot fix it, and regulation is needed.

# Key points

- Face ID: Apple's claimed one-in-a-million false-match rate is already challenged by lookalike relatives and researcher spoofs; Steve will treat it as unproven and prefers granular locking for authenticator and password manager apps. Leo notes fingerprints are also easy to spoof and phones hold too much.
- AVGator: directory junctions let a quarantined file land in a DLL search path so restoring it plants a malicious DLL; Trend Micro, Emsisoft, Kaspersky, Malwarebytes, IKARUS and ZoneAlarm released fixes; blocking standard users from restoring quarantined items mitigates it.
- Vault 8 / Hive: a network of ordinary-looking web servers fronted by VPS machines and proxies; implants add optional authentication to requests, and only those get routed to a "honeycomb" control server while others see a cover site. Provenance cannot be verified.
- Encryption debate after the Texas shooting: the FBI complained about access while Apple said it offered help immediately; Steve restates that a per-device escrowed key under legal process is technically possible but Apple resists, and Leo raises foreign-government demands.
- Exfiltration through video calls: malware adds a band of "static" encoding data to a video stream, shown by PenTestPartners.
- Chrome 64 (January 2018) blocks script-driven redirects from iframes; Chrome 65 (March 2018) blocks "tab under" redirects; an Abusive Experiences Report will disable misleading UI elements (fake pause buttons, invisible overlays) on listed sites.
- Intel DCI: JTAG over USB on Skylake-and-later hardware gives physical-access control of the Management Engine (so-called god mode); Positive Technologies will detail it the next month. Steve predicts a USB key that disables the ME.
- Cryptojacking study by Willem de Groot: of 2,496 sites running Coinhive scripts, 85% credited just two accounts and the remainder one more group, indicating three operators exploiting compromised sites; Steve suggests Coinhive should shut down and gives a hosts-file block for coinhive.com and coin-hive.com.
- WebAssembly: now supported by Chrome, Firefox, Safari (iOS 11) and Edge; precompiled binary, much faster to load than JavaScript, with a readable text form; this is what makes in-browser mining practical. Godbolt compiler explorer recommended.
- Asus router firmware update fixing KRACK and several CVEs; Steve notes it required noticing a warning icon, reinforcing the need for auto-update, and side-by-side firmware has long existed in network gear.
- Code-signing time stamping: an expired certificate on a signed installer is fine if the signature was stamped while the certificate was valid.
- SpinRite on hybrid drives needs no special handling because it disables caching; Steve clarifies his advice is to never download things a website pushes at you, not to avoid downloads in general.
- Schneier's testimony: Equifax was solely at fault for failing to patch a known Struts flaw (patch 6 March, attacks from 13 May, patched 29 July); 145.5 million affected; thousands of data brokers hold similar data; opt-outs do not work; liability rules are too narrow; the market cannot fix this since individuals are the product; executives were barely penalised; he urges regulation, an app to freeze and unfreeze credit, and notes that the EU's GDPR shows compliance is feasible.
