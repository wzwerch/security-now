# Summary

The episode begins with Kaspersky's report on SparkCat, a stealer found in 10 Google Play and 11 App Store apps that uses on-device OCR (Google ML Kit) to scan photo galleries for crypto wallet seed phrases; about 242,000 Play downloads were counted and activity dates back to at least March 2024. Steve then covers the Washington Post report that the UK Home Office served Apple with a secret technical capability notice under the Investigatory Powers Act demanding access to all users' encrypted iCloud data, which Apple would likely answer by withdrawing Advanced Data Protection in the UK. He proposes on-device AI filtering of sent and received content as a way to defuse the "protect the children" argument, and notes that France is also pushing for backdoors. Further items include Firefox 135, the Five Eyes edge-device security guidance, Netgear router vulnerabilities, unfixed DLL-hijacking in Sysinternals tools, Google dropping its AI weapons and surveillance pledge, and CyberArk's FuzzyAI jailbreak of OpenAI o3. Steve previews the DNS Benchmark's first multi-protocol results. The main topic is The 74's investigation into how breach coaches and insurers keep school ransomware attacks hidden from students, parents and staff.

# Key points

- SparkCat: malicious OCR module hidden in bait messenger apps and some legitimate-looking apps (food delivery, news, crypto utilities); filters photos by keywords such as "mnemonic" and uploads matches; first stealer found in the App Store; cause (supply-chain library or deliberate) unknown; authors likely Chinese-speaking. Advice: grant access to selected photos only, never store seed phrases or documents in the gallery, install fewer apps, and move funds to a new wallet if a seed was exposed.
- UK technical capability notice: demands a blanket ability to read encrypted cloud content of any Apple user worldwide; secrecy is mandatory, appeals do not suspend compliance; Apple previously warned Parliament about this. Apple's Advanced Data Protection (since 2022) is opt-in and unavailable to Steve because of his older devices. Google says it cannot access Android encrypted backups. Reactions from Sen. Wyden and Signal's Meredith Whittaker were strongly negative.
- Steve's idea: local AI classifiers on devices that refuse to send or receive abusive content could remove the need for cloud access; Leo thinks governments want more than that.
- France: senators passed an amendment paving the way for backdoors into messaging apps.
- Firefox 135: PDF editing, more translation languages, certificate transparency enforcement, gradual CRLite rollout, history-API abuse limits, "Copy Clean Link", and an optional AI chatbot sidebar (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, HuggingChat, Le Chat Mistral).
- NSA and Five Eyes partners published three Cybersecurity Information Sheets on securing edge devices (firewalls, routers, VPN gateways).
- Netgear: WAX214v2, WAX206 and WAX220 access points have CVSS 9.6 authentication bypass; Nighthawk XR500, XR1000 and XR1000v2 have CVSS 9.8 unauthenticated remote code execution; only exposed if remote administration is enabled; patch released.
- Sysinternals: researcher Raik Schneider showed DLL search-order hijacking in most Sysinternals tools; reported 28 October 2024, Microsoft called it defense-in-depth and did not fix it; old copies cannot be updated automatically.
- Google removed its 2018 pledges not to pursue weapons, harmful surveillance or technologies violating human rights from its AI principles; Steve reads it as leveling the field rather than intent to do harm.
- CyberArk's open-source FuzzyAI fuzzer jailbroke OpenAI o3 into producing code for injecting into lsass.exe; Steve argues a clean boundary between malicious and benign requests is inherently fuzzy.
- DNS Benchmark preview: first simultaneous IPv4, IPv6, DoT and DoH benchmark; in Steve's location NextDNS over DoH and Quad9 over TLS were fastest; results are location specific.
- The 74 investigation: analysis of more than 300 school cyberattacks; districts rely on insurer-appointed "breach coaches" (law firms such as McDonald Hopkins) who run privileged investigations, delay notification (Minneapolis took seven months after a Medusa attack demanding $4.5 million; Camden's notice came 13 months later); sensitive records on abuse and mental health leak; 135 state student privacy laws are unfunded and breach notification is a patchwork; insurers' willingness to pay fuels ransomware. Steve and Leo call for a comprehensive federal breach disclosure law.
