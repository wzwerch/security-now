# Summary

Another news-heavy show where Steve skips Q&A. He corrects his Coin theory: the card does not proxy charges but uses a coil to emit an alternating field across the whole magnetic stripe, and because each of the three ISO 7811 tracks has its own format and parity scheme, the card can make just the intended track read cleanly; he stays sceptical of the product while admiring the trick. A Renesys report claimed 38 episodes of BGP traffic being diverted through Belarusian and Icelandic networks, which Steve thinks was likely router bugs but which underscores BGP's weakness and the need for HTTPS everywhere. Twitter enabled forward secrecy (75 percent of connections immediately used ECDHE), Microsoft is reportedly planning wide encryption after NSA interception worries, and the EFF published an "Encrypt the Web" scorecard. The big case study is TQP Development, a patent troll that extracted over 45 million dollars from roughly 140 companies for an expired modem-encryption patent, then won 2.3 million against Newegg despite testimony from Ron Rivest, Ray Ozzie and Whitfield Diffie that the technology was prior art; Newegg will appeal. For CryptoLocker, the file format has been reverse-engineered (per-file AES key wrapped with 2048-bit RSA behind a 20-byte header), an open-source decrypter exists that still needs the paid key, the ransom dropped to half a bitcoin as bitcoin passed a thousand dollars, and Steve posted live samples on GRC for researchers. He also describes the Safeplug Tor appliance.

# Key points

- Coin mechanics: a coil emits a field across the whole stripe so no swipe motion is needed; parity bits per character plus a longitudinal redundancy check make only the targeted track valid, so one card can emulate any of the three ISO tracks (alphanumeric 210 bpi, numeric 75 bpi, numeric 210 bpi). Limitations: no chip-and-PIN, and a risk that card networks could refuse it.
- BGP: routers exchange routing tables over TCP; weak or predictable TCP sequence numbers and trust between peers allow tampering; past incidents include the 2008 Pakistan Telecom YouTube outage and a DefCon demonstration of mass redirection. The reported diversions were traceable with traceroute, so Steve judges them probably accidental; the remedy is end-to-end encryption.
- Twitter reordered cipher suites to prefer ephemeral Diffie-Hellman, instantly giving forward secrecy to most clients; Microsoft reportedly plans to encrypt its internal and external traffic.
- EFF "Encrypt the Web" report grid tracks data-centre link encryption, HTTPS, HSTS, forward secrecy and STARTTLS per company, built on company survey responses; Steve notes it has no trust-no-one column.
- Level 3 and other carriers are suspected as the point where inter-data-centre fibre was tapped; Google's link encryption is still in progress.
- TQP v. Newegg: the plaintiff's patent on RC4-with-SSL-like modem encryption had been bought for 750,000 dollars; settlements ranged from about 25,000 to one million dollars, below the cost of defence, which is how the troll business works; the East Texas jury awarded 2.3 million dollars; the opposing lawyer attacked Diffie's lack of a doctorate. Steve notes he publishes SQRL's protocols to create prior art.
- CryptoLocker format: SHA-1 cookie, then a per-file AES key encrypted with 2048-bit RSA; it avoids re-encrypting its own output; Kyrus released an open-source decrypter and format documentation; the ransom fell to 0.5 bitcoin.
- GRC posted encrypted ZIP archives of live CryptoLocker (old and new builds) and a Zbot sample for experiments with Sandboxie and antivirus; Microsoft Security Essentials catches it when unpacked; a listener confirmed Sandboxie keeps encrypted copies inside the sandbox.
- Bitcoin crossed 1,000 dollars; a man who discarded a drive holding 7,500 coins was searching a landfill.
- Safeplug: a 49-dollar Tor appliance that can also act as a relay and whitelist sites or browsers by user agent; Steve cautions that cookies can still deanonymise users.
