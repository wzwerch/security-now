# Summary

The episode begins with a Wired report on rising GPS jamming and spoofing, concentrated around the Baltic Sea, the Eastern Mediterranean and conflict zones, with Russia widely blamed for interference around Kaliningrad that forced Finnair to suspend flights to Tartu. Steve explains that the attacks are local, that jamming differs from the far more sophisticated spoofing, and that the world's heavy dependence on GPS for position and time is a systemic risk. He then debunks the alarmist press framing of "TunnelVision" (a Leviathan Security technique with an assigned CVE) in which a rogue DHCP server on the local network uses Option 121 classless static routes to steer traffic around a VPN tunnel; he stresses it is a LAN-side attack that works as DHCP is designed, affects everything except Android, and cannot simply be blocked because enterprises use the option legitimately. Listener questions show the confusion in today's authentication landscape, especially Passkey backup and portability, and a listener's story suggests an old signed-in iPhone caused iCloud Keychain entries to vanish. The main topic explains why Google delayed third-party cookie deprecation: the UK Competition and Markets Authority's formal review of the legally binding "Commitments" Google gave around the Privacy Sandbox. Steve reads the CMA's Q1 2024 report, concludes both sides are negotiating in good faith, and expects deprecation to start in early 2025. He closes by teasing next week's topic, Microsoft's Zero Trust DNS.

# Key points

- Wired: GPS jamming and spoofing have surged; tens of thousands of flights in the Baltic region reported navigation problems between August 2023 and March 2024, and thousands of aircraft had their positions spoofed to airports such as Beirut and Cairo; ships appeared to be at an inland Crimean airport.
- Jamming overwhelms the signal; spoofing substitutes false positions and is harder and costlier. Pilots reported false terrain warnings, and agencies urge training on GNSS-interference effects.
- Steve notes the attacks are inherently local and suggests modern drones and aircraft can fall back on inertial navigation or vision, and that shielding antennas from below helps for aircraft but not ships.
- Leviathan Security Group's "TunnelVision" (a CVE was assigned) abuses DHCP Option 121 (classless static routes, the successor of Option 33) to inject more specific routes than the VPN's default route so selected traffic leaves unencrypted outside the tunnel while the VPN still reports connected.
- Requirements: attacker on the same LAN who answers DHCP before the real server; affected: Windows, Linux, iOS, macOS; Android is unaffected because it does not support Option 121. DHCP's original RFC 2131 already called itself insecure.
- Mitigation thoughts: Option 121 is widely used in enterprise networks so it cannot just be disabled; VPN clients could self-test their routing (for example by pinging themselves through the tunnel); the realistic risk is public Wi-Fi and hostile LANs.
- Passkey Q&A: Passkeys cannot be copied between people or shared across YubiKeys; keys are non-exportable (YubiKey stores at most 25 resident credentials), and Chrome's own FAQ admits Windows Hello, macOS profile and security-key Passkeys are not recoverable if lost. Steve advises using a single cross-platform Passkey manager (expecting Bitwarden's beta) and describes "solution spread": new authentication methods are added but old ones never die.
- iCloud Keychain: a listener found passwords and TOTP codes reappearing or vanishing until he removed an old iPhone 6S Plus running iOS 14 from his iCloud account; Steve guesses Apple deliberately limits what it keeps while an old device is attached.
- Google Privacy Sandbox: Google announced on 23 April that cookie deprecation would slip; the CMA (with the ICO) accepted binding Commitments in February 2022 covering non-use of personal data, non-discrimination and anti-circumvention, with a trustee and technical expert monitoring.
- CMA Q1 2024 report (26 April): Google has complied; outstanding competition concerns remain; industry test results are due end of June; a standstill period of at least 60 days (extendable to 120) precedes cookie removal; Google now aims to proceed in early 2025.
- Steve judges the ad-tech worry that smaller advertisers are disadvantaged has been examined and not borne out, and that the process is bureaucratic rather than obstructive.
