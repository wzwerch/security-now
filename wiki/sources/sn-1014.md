# Summary

Apple stopped offering Advanced Data Protection (ADP) to new UK users, which Steve reads as a deliberate first step in a standoff with the UK government, while Leo sees it as a capitulation that gives other governments a template. Steve then notes that paying ransoms can break US sanctions, using RansomHub's links to the sanctioned Evil Corp as the example. Shorter items cover X's brief blocking of Signal.me links and the mess in Spain where court-ordered IP blocking of Cloudflare by LaLiga knocked out unrelated sites on football weekends. Qualys disclosed two rare OpenSSH vulnerabilities (CVE-2025-26465 and CVE-2025-26466). Senator Mark Warner said the US may never fully evict Salt Typhoon from telecom networks, and Steve digs into how the group actually gets in: two old, patched Cisco IOS XE web UI flaws on exposed devices. North Korea's Lazarus Group stole about 1.5 billion USD in Ether from Bybit by subverting the multi-signature approval workflow, and former NSA chief Paul Nakasone warned the US is falling behind in cyberspace. Steve closes by announcing "master key" as the listener-chosen replacement for "backdoor" and spends the main segment on the internet-exposed Hirsch/Viscount "FREEDOM" apartment access-control system (CVE-2025-26793), where a documented default login worked on 43 percent of 742 exposed instances.

# Key points

- ADP is no longer offered to new UK users as of 21 February 2025; existing users are to be switched off later, with no date given. Matthew Green urged non-UK users to enable ADP to make blanket shut-offs harder. Leo notes Android has had default-on end-to-end encrypted backup, and that without ADP Apple already holds iCloud keys. The UK demand reportedly covers all ADP accounts worldwide, which Apple did not comply with.
- Ransom payments: a CISA report and Equinix analyst Will Thomas flagged that RansomHub affiliates include sanctioned Evil Corp (FakeUpdates/SocGholish infections leading to RansomHub payloads), so paying can violate US and UK sanctions; consult legal counsel first. Steve notes cash flow is what keeps ransomware alive.
- X briefly blocked Signal.me links with confusing errors; Leo could not reproduce it, suggesting a bug or reversed decision. Steve uses the episode to note he moved back to email for show-note distribution (about 16,350 subscribers).
- LaLiga/Cloudflare: Spanish ISPs block shared Cloudflare IPs on match days under a court order, taking down GitHub, Reddit and many businesses. Steve explains Server Name Indication (SNI) lets thousands of domains share one IP, so IP blocking causes collateral damage; Cloudflare says other European governments consider such blocking a Net Neutrality violation.
- OpenSSH: CVE-2025-26465 (machine-in-the-middle against the client when VerifyHostKeyDNS is enabled; introduced December 2014; FreeBSD had it on by default from 2013 to 2023) and CVE-2025-26466 (pre-authentication denial of service, introduced 2023). Fix: upgrade to OpenSSH 9.9p2; mitigate with LoginGraceTime, MaxStartups, PerSourcePenalties and keep VerifyHostKeyDNS at "no".
- Warner (Munich Security Conference): evicting Salt Typhoon could take 50,000 people and a 12-hour network shutdown plus tens of billions in equipment replacement, so he favours credible offensive threats against China plus telecom regulation. Steve finds it sad that the answer is deterrence rather than securing networks.
- Salt Typhoon (also RedMike, GhostEmperor, FamousSparrow): per Recorded Future it exploits CVE-2023-20198 and CVE-2023-20273 in Cisco IOS XE web UI to create a local user, gain root and add a GRE tunnel for persistence. The root cause is unpatched devices (fixed October 2023) with exposed management interfaces.
- Bybit heist (21 February 2025): about 401,000 ETH worth roughly 1.5 billion USD, attributed by Arkham and Elliptic to Lazarus. Attackers compromised each multi-sig signer's machine and altered the wallet UI so signers approved a different transaction than shown. Bybit offered a 10 percent bounty and saw 350,000 withdrawal requests. Steve's lesson: such systems need genuine air-gapping.
- Nakasone (DistrictCon): the US is falling "increasingly behind", cyber effects are bleeding into the physical world, AI agents and autonomous targeting are coming, and the top priority is hiring talent. Steve is unsettled by AI drones without humans in the loop.
- "Master key" is the listener-favoured term replacing "backdoor": it names a designed-in, openly known capability whose key is the secret.
- FREEDOM Administration Login: Hirsch Enterphone/Viscount MESH building entry systems ship with a documented default admin login and no forced change; researcher Eric Daigle found 742 exposed via a plain web search and logged into 43 percent, exposing resident names, unit numbers, phone numbers, multi-year fob logs and the ability to unlock doors. Rated CVE-2025-26793, CVSS 10. The vendor blamed customers for not changing defaults.
- Steve's design lessons: never bind admin UIs to the WAN, never ship default credentials (generate random ones at first start), and avoid identical, searchable page titles. Anyone notifying building owners should take anonymizing precautions because even using public credentials can be a crime.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a laptop powered via a screwdriver in the VGA port and a straightened paperclip to improvise a mismatched DC connector ("where there's a will, there's a way").
- GRC: fifth DNS Benchmark pre-release added DNS over HTTPS and DNS over TLS testing (IPv6 arrived with the first pre-release on 26 December); pre-release testers found several bugs.
- Steve notes the GRC shortcut for the National Public Data breach is by far the most-clicked (nearly 13,000) versus the credit-freeze shortcut (about 3,600).
- Asides on coffee prices, X login problems, ADP hardware constraints and Leo's concerns about politicised law enforcement.
