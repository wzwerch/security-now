# Summary

Steve opens with a Vitamin D chart as his picture of the week and a note on Firefox 79's password export. Charges in the Twitter hack were brought against a 17-year-old and two others, and Twitter explained that phone-based social engineering of employees led to its internal tools; Steve sees the incident as managerial inertia as Twitter's importance grew. BleepingComputer obtained Garmin's decryption package, indicating the ransom, initially reportedly 10 million dollars, was paid, with Emsisoft and Coveware involved. A researcher publishing Tor weaknesses after being brushed off prompts Steve to question the ethics of forcing fixes. Zoom's six-digit meeting PINs lacked rate limiting, Microsoft retired SHA-1-signed downloads, and QNAP's QSnatch malware infected 62,000 NAS devices. The main story is BootHole, a buffer overflow in how GRUB2 parses its unsigned config file that undermines Secure Boot, with a messy fix: new shims, a revocation list update and patches that left some Red Hat, CentOS, Ubuntu and Debian systems unbootable.

# Key points

- Firefox 79 adds Lockwise password export to CSV; Steve suggests storing exports only in an AES-encrypted 7-Zip archive.
- Twitter hack: charges of 30 felonies against the alleged 17-year-old, plus two other defendants; Twitter said phone spear-phishing yielded credentials and then knowledge of internal processes; Reuters reported over a thousand employees and contractors had tool access.
- Garmin: BleepingComputer saw a restoration package with a WastedLocker decryptor dated 25 July; Emsisoft says it only builds sturdier decryptors; Coveware refused WastedLocker negotiations because of Evil Corp sanctions. Another victim reportedly negotiated 10 million down to 4.5 million.
- Tor: a researcher says Tor TLS certificates form a fingerprint that lets firewalls block Tor traffic, which Tor classed as a known issue about bridges; Steve argues a vendor can decline to fix and researchers cannot force it, and notes Katie Moussouris's disillusionment with bug bounties.
- Zoom's six-digit meeting PIN had no brute-force protection; found by a UK researcher, the web client was pulled and fixed within about a week. Steve laments teams reinventing login logic.
- Microsoft retired SHA-1-signed content from its Download Center on 3 August 2020; Steve worries about bootstrapping SHA-256 support on Windows XP and 7 and keeps archived update kits.
- QNAP QSnatch: password logger, credential scraper, SSH backdoor, web shell and data theft; joint CISA and NCSC alert; assume all device secrets are compromised and keep firmware current.
- BootHole (Eclypsium): buffer overflow in GRUB2 grub.cfg parsing allows code execution during boot even with Secure Boot, affecting Linux distributions and Windows machines trusting the Microsoft third-party UEFI CA. Exploitation needs admin or physical access first, but gives persistence. Fix needs new GRUB2, shims, installers and updates to the dbx revocation list; Canonical found further GRUB2 bugs; Red Hat patches broke booting, so Steve advises waiting. HD Moore doubts Secure Boot's practical value.
- Benchmark update: fine-grained 32 MB transfers show used regions of SSDs running as slow as half speed, perhaps from error correction or fragmentation.

# Other segments

- Vitamin D discussion (5,000 IU daily, reference to the earlier Vitamin D episode) and a chart linking low levels to COVID deaths.
- The unreleased SpinRite storage benchmark is not ready; Steve will plan UEFI and signing work.
- Banter on the BootHole logo and anniversary celebrations.
