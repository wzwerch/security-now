# Summary

After two Heartbleed shows, Steve runs a Q&A and previews next week's long explainer on certificate revocation (browser revocation checking, Adam Langley's blog post saying not to enable it, and a call for Firefox users to turn on hard-fail checking). News: Lavabit's owner Ladar Levison lost his appeal of the contempt ruling on procedural grounds (after handing over SSL keys printed in tiny type), with the ACLU backing him; Eloi Vanderbeken found that the previously reported port 32764 router backdoor survives in updated firmware as a raw Ethernet listener that a magic packet on the local ISP segment can activate; Google reportedly explores PGP-style end-to-end encryption for Gmail; and OpenBSD developers forked OpenSSL as LibreSSL, cutting unneeded platforms such as VMS and Windows. Apple's same-day OS X update fixed an HTTP cookie flaw (incomplete Set-Cookie headers processed after the connection closed), a URL format-string bug in Mavericks, PDF font, JPEG and graphics driver bugs, among others. Steve closes the iOS series by explaining jailbreaking: any bug that lets code bypass Apple's mitigations can be chained, and the cat-and-mouse game continues. Q&A covers whether SQRL needs servers to keep secrets (it does not), client-side Heartbleed exposure, a scanner by Robin Keir for finding vulnerable devices on a LAN, caution in rolling out security fixes, paper shredding, and Chrome's revocation checkbox.

# Key points

- Revocation preview: Steve urges enabling Firefox's strict "must check" revocation and warns that captive portals break hard-fail checking; macOS Keychain shows OCSP/CRL settings greyed or "best effort".
- Lavabit appeal: Levison lost on procedural grounds without a ruling on whether the government could demand keys; ACLU filed in support. Steve says he would also refuse handing over keys because that would end the service's security claims.
- Router backdoor (Synacktiv researcher Eloi Vanderbeken): after the 32764 TCP backdoor was publicized in December, patched firmware kept a hidden service reachable via a special Ethernet frame type containing an MD5 of the model number, reachable only by a device on the same link (for example the ISP's side); enough to start the configuration manager and reopen the TCP backdoor. Steve advises replacing stock firmware with DD-WRT or Tomato where supported.
- Google exploring PGP in Gmail (VentureBeat): Steve thinks browser-side keys that Google never sees are technically feasible, though ad targeting conflicts; Leo cites Vint Cerf on wishing TCP/IP had included public-key crypto.
- LibreSSL: OpenBSD team purging OpenSSL of obsolete platforms, home-grown printf and other cruft; Steve fears the work could introduce bugs but finds it interesting.
- Apple fixes: cookie handling flaw reported by a Whisper Systems engineer (a MITM can truncate Set-Cookie before the Secure flag arrives); Mavericks URL format-string bug; PDF font and JPEG buffer overflows; Intel graphics and kernel bugs. Steve says update OS X immediately, iOS without panic.
- Jailbreaking: Apple's design may be sound, but implementation or design bugs, such as a local app learning kernel pointers, let attackers bypass protections; flipping one verification branch is the classic crack. Apple's iOS 6 hardening focused on blocking jailbreaks.
- SQRL gives servers no secrets to keep, so a server-side leak like Heartbleed could not expose user credentials.
- Heartbleed also affects clients that connect to malicious servers; firewall rules are not the main issue.
- Robin Keir's Heartbleed scanner is a self-contained tool that can scan a LAN range; Steve vouches for it. Phil Zimmermann's pharmaceutical analogy for rolling out new crypto fixes cautiously was cited, though Heartbleed's flaw was well understood.
- Shredding: cross-cut shredding is fine for low-value papers; Steve declines electronic funds transfer on his bank accounts so the bank bears fraud risk.
