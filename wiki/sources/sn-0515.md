# Summary

With Father Robert Ballecer again standing in for Leo, Steve drops the planned Q&A because the news volume is so high. He starts with a listener joke about Netflix merging with Carbonite to solve settlement-free peering imbalance, then covers Firefox 39 (Safe Browsing-based malware checks on more platforms, SSLv3 and RC4 removal, asynchronous plugin start-up). The ICANN privacy and proxy accreditation debate is explained: the working group tentatively said privacy services should stay available to commercial registrants, and a public comment tally by Google's Adam Langley showed roughly 90 percent supporting no change. A new UDP reflection DDoS vector via RIPv1 on small-office routers is described, as is Amazon's compact open-source TLS library s2n. ARIN has begun its IPv4 "unmet requests" policy as its free pool nears zero. The big stories are the leak of Italian spyware vendor Hacking Team's 400 GB of data (including an unpatched Flash zero-day and evidence of sales to repressive regimes and to US agencies), the Intercept's release of XKEYSCORE documents, WikiLeaks' publication of NSA targeting of German officials, and Windows 10's WiFi Sense sharing of Wi-Fi credentials with contacts. Steve also notes an Akamai proposal for TLS client puzzles and some listener tips on privacytools.io alternatives and uBlock Origin.

# Key points

- **Peering joke:** Netflix traffic is one-directional, which breaks settlement-free peering; Google reduced such tension by building its own fiber, and in-ISP caching is another remedy.
- **Firefox 39:** Safe Browsing malware detection for Mac/Linux and downloads, URL sharing in Hello, async plugin initialization, SSLv3 removed, RC4 disabled (with per-host exception), 13 security fixes. Steve notes Google Safe Browsing once falsely blacklisted his TrueCrypt files.
- **MailStore Home 8:** free local email archiving tool recommended again.
- **ICANN WHOIS privacy/proxy:** 98-page initial report; preliminary conclusion that privacy services should not depend on commercial status, but no consensus; about 10,000 comments, about 90 percent against change.
- **RIPv1 reflection DDoS (Akamai):** routers on port 520 that still answer RIPv1 are abused with spoofed sources; floods of about 12.8 Gbps seen from roughly 500 SOHO routers. Such devices are unlikely to be fixed; ISPs could block the port.
- **Amazon s2n:** a TLS implementation of roughly 6,000 lines versus over 500,000 lines in OpenSSL, deliberately omitting rarely used options; open source under Apache 2.0 and externally audited.
- **ARIN IPv4 exhaustion:** first use of the unmet-requests policy; only dozens of /23 blocks and a few hundred /24 blocks left; discussion of the gray market, IPv6 inertia, and Steve losing his old T1 lines.
- **Hacking Team breach:** 400 GB leaked; the FBI had spent about $775,000 since 2011 on its Remote Control System; a US front company shared its address; a previously unknown Flash zero-day with full exploit description was in the dump, so Adobe faces another emergency patch. The dumped passwords were weak.
- **XKEYSCORE:** documents describe a distributed system of about 150 sites and 700 servers on Linux, full-feed taps with only three to four days of retention, queries open to analysts, and a "micro-plugin" tagging system. Hosts note the tension between short retention and warrant timelines.
- **NSA and Germany:** WikiLeaks published intercept summaries and a targeting spreadsheet; Der Spiegel reports it was itself surveilled.
- **Windows 10 WiFi Sense:** opt-in feature shares Wi-Fi access with Facebook, Outlook and Skype contacts via Microsoft's cloud, without granularity; Steve will not enable it and keeps separate guest networks.
- **TLS Client Puzzles Extension (Akamai draft for TLS 1.3):** servers under attack could require clients to solve a proof-of-work style puzzle before connecting.
- **Listener recommendations:** Steve defends LastPass and Threema despite privacytools.io excluding closed-source tools; uBlock Origin's advanced-user mode reveals per-page request details.
