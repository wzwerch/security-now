# Summary

Steve opened with the milestone that Let's Encrypt had issued a million free certificates covering about 2.5 million domain names, while noting that domain-validated certificates carry little identity assurance and he still buys extended-validation certificates. The Justice Department appealed the New York ruling against its All Writs Act request, and the RSA conference produced a cryptographers' panel and the sobering fact that quantum factoring had only reached the number 15. A trojaned installer of the Transmission BitTorrent client introduced KeRanger, the first fully functional Mac ransomware; Apple revoked the signing certificate and updated its malware signatures within a day. Verizon was fined 1.35 million dollars for supercookie tracking, which Steve called a trivial cost of doing business. A researcher showed Facebook's password-reset code on its beta sites could be guessed without limit. Michigan State researchers spoofed fingerprint scanners using conductive ink printed on a circuit-printing inkjet. Steve mocked John McAfee's claims, recommended staying on Windows 7, and described his Healthy Sleep Formula. The main topic was CacheBleed: an attack on OpenSSL's RSA code that exploits L1 cache bank conflicts between hyperthreads sharing a core, recovering key bits even though the code avoids secret-dependent branches.

# Key points

- Let's Encrypt one million certificates in about three months; multi-domain certificates mean about 2.5 million names. Steve expects web servers to bundle it, but remarks it only proves domain control.
- Justice Department appeals the ruling that the All Writs Act could not compel Apple to unlock an iPhone 5c; Steve is hopeful because the NSA and cryptographers say strong encryption matters.
- KeRanger: Transmission 2.90 installers on the project's site were replaced for about a day; they carried a valid Mac developer signature, waited three days, contacted a Tor command server, encrypted user documents (including Time Machine backups) and demanded one bitcoin. Palo Alto Networks found it; Apple revoked the certificate and updated XProtect; Transmission shipped a cleanup. If a download from that window is suspected, disconnect the machine before the malware reaches its server.
- Quantum: researchers factored 15 with an ion trap of five atoms; post-quantum cryptography is already an active research area (Steve cites a book co-authored by Daniel Bernstein) and the NSA is preparing a standard.
- Verizon supercookies: fine of 1.35 million dollars from the FCC and a consent requirement; Steve wants periodic opt-in.
- Facebook: the password-reset flow limited wrong code tries to about ten on the main site, but beta.facebook.com variants had no limit, allowing automated guessing of the six-digit code; the researcher tested only his own account and Facebook fixed it.
- Fingerprint spoofing: a conductive-ink print of a fingerprint image on special paper unlocked a Galaxy S6 and Honor 7 in tests, easier than earlier gummy-finger methods.
- McAfee's claims about cracking the iPhone were admitted to be YouTube-traffic bait; Steve explains the register-swap puzzle he posed (three XOR operations).
- Steve recommends staying on Windows 7 (supported until 2020, reduced rights, supports TLS 1.2, SHA-256) over Windows 10; Leo suggests Ubuntu over Linux Mint and Chromebooks for simple users.
- CacheBleed: three rules for side-channel-safe crypto (no secret-dependent instructions, branches, or memory access patterns); OpenSSL's constant-time code followed the first two. Two hyperthreads share the L1 cache, split in 16 banks; collisions stall one thread, and an attacker thread running on the same core can time its own stalls and infer key-dependent data accesses. It is an active, close-quarters attack, so impractical across VMs, not a Heartbleed-scale emergency.
- Steve notes Chrome's weak revocation handling (his revoked.grc.com test) in contrast with Apple's quick revocation.

# Other segments

- Healthy Sleep Formula page published (a list of over-the-counter supplements Steve and Leo tried; both say it is experimental and recommend consulting a physician).
- SpinRite question: running SpinRite from a USB drive on its own partitions after a drop; Steve says it is safe and can help drives with physical defects.
- Printed-circuit paper hobby product mentioned in passing; a long aside on a cryptographers' panel video; Leo notes 550 in hexadecimal is 226.
