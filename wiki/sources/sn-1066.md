# Summary

Steve first returns to the shrinking validity periods set by the CA/Browser Forum: DigiCert's "urgent" email about domain revalidation on February 24 (domain validation reuse down to 199 days, organization validation reuse to 397/398 days) reflects the decoupling of qualification from issuance, and existing certificates stay valid until their expiry. He then covers California's AB-2047 (alongside Washington and New York bills) that would require DOJ-listed 3D printers with "firearm-blocking technology", using Michael Weinberg's technical rebuttal that printers cannot identify gun parts and are open and hackable. News items include ShinyHunters leaking about 967,000 Figure Technology customer records after a refused ransom (Okta-targeted campaign), an UpGuard find of an exposed Elastic database claiming 2.7 billion Social Security number records (mostly recombined and noisy), rumored Apple camera-equipped wearables, end of Firefox security updates for Windows 7/8, Russia briefly blocking kernel.org, and the planned US State Department Freedom.gov portal to show content blocked in Europe. A study shows LLM-generated passwords are predictable and must not be used; Huntress reports ClickFix accounted for 53 percent of malware loader activity in 2025. Listener items include a false alarm about DGA-like DNS queries that were really GRC's DNS Benchmark, Redis protected mode, and a TTL-based exposure idea. The main topic is the ETH Zurich paper attacking Bitwarden (12 attacks), LastPass (7) and Dashlane (6) under a fully malicious server model; Steve explains the causes (account recovery, sharing, backward compatibility, lack of vault-level integrity) and concludes the fixes make users safer and that open code made the audit possible.

# Key points

- Steve and Leo will be at Zero Trust World (ThreatLocker) in Florida the next week; that week's episode was prerecorded, and Steve's talk would also be released as a special episode.
- DigiCert and the CA/Browser Forum: from February 24, 2026 domain validation reuse is 199 days and organization validation reuse about 398 days; issuance must be decoupled from vetting because certificate lifetimes will step down to 200, then 100, then 47 days by 2029. Steve plans to drop OV certificates for Let's Encrypt DV after his prepaid DigiCert certs, and now holds a code signing certificate valid until February 2029.
- Steve bought a SmartCard-HSM (about $72) for code signing, cosigned five GRC freeware tools with the new IdenTrust certificate, and plans to publish documentation of the process.
- AB-2047 (California), HB 2321 (Washington) and S.9005 (New York) would ban sales of printers lacking state-approved blocking technology (California: from March 1, 2029, civil penalties up to $25,000, misdemeanor for circumvention). Weinberg's points: geometry analysis cannot tell gun parts from benign parts, printers lack compute and connectivity, hash blacklists are trivially evaded, printers are open source and self-buildable, and 2D currency-blocking is not a valid analogy. Steve: only outlaws would own unrestricted printers.
- Figure Technology (blockchain lending) breach: an employee was socially engineered; ShinyHunters leaked 2.5 GB after the company refused to pay; Have I Been Pwned loaded 967,200 customers' names, addresses, birth dates and phone numbers. Steve notes more victims now refuse ransoms and that ShinyHunters get nothing once data is public; freezing credit is the practical defense.
- UpGuard: an exposed Elastic database on Hetzner held about 3 billion email/password pairs and 2.7 billion SSN records (only about 450 million SSNs ever issued, so heavily duplicated or noisy); removed after abuse reports. Steve says personal data is already widely leaked.
- Mark Gurman reports Apple is working on a smart pendant, smart glasses and AI AirPods with cameras. Firefox support for Windows 7/8 security fixes ends this month; Firefox 148 adds a switch to disable AI features (per Leo).
- Roskomnadzor briefly blocked kernel.org and unblocked it after Russian engineers objected, since Russian distributions depend on Linux.
- Freedom.gov: Reuters reports the US State Department is developing a portal (possibly with VPN function) to let Europeans see content banned by their governments; State denied delays and legal concerns. Steve could not see it working yet from the US.
- Irregular research "Vibe Password Generation: Predictable by Design": passwords produced directly by LLMs show repeated patterns and are far weaker than they look; coding agents may silently embed such passwords. Use a real generator (Leo has Claude fetch passwords from GRC.com/passwords).
- Huntress 2026 Cyber Threat Report: ClickFix (fake CAPTCHA telling users to paste a command into Windows+R) drove 53 percent of malware loader activity in 2025; security training lags. Steve again urges Microsoft to stop pasting browser-originated clipboard content into Run.
- Listener: Claude Code's /security-review and the "Superpowers" workflow add-on help with AI-assisted security review and test-driven development.
- False positive: random 26-character .com lookups, queries to isc.org and rebindtest.com in a NextDNS log are GRC's DNS Benchmark (random nonexistent names defeat caching and measure resolver connectivity; rebindtest.com checks for DNS rebinding protection), not DGA malware.
- Redis protected mode (since 4.0.0) limited exposure to loopback; exposed instances fell from about 17,000 to 8,000. Steve restates that authentication must not be the only barrier, and wishes applications could set a low packet TTL so public-facing services are unreachable from afar.
- ETH Zurich (with Italian co-authors) "malicious server" analysis of cloud password managers (Bitwarden 12 attacks, LastPass 7, Dashlane 6; 60 million users combined): categories are key escrow/account recovery and SSO, lack of vault-level integrity (LastPass uses AES-CBC with no ciphertext integrity), sharing features, and backward compatibility downgrades; unauthenticated public keys and missing key separation enabled key substitution and cut-and-paste attacks. Vendors have remediated; Steve says the issues were mostly theoretical (need full server compromise), these three were chosen because their client code was available, and built-in browser managers now hold about 55 percent of the market. Steve explains TNO (trust no one) and PIE (pre-Internet encryption) as the simple ideal that practical features erode.
- Leo and Steve note passkeys avoid sharing a secret with the server, but stored in a password manager they inherit its risks.
