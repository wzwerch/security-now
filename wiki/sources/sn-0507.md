# Summary

Steve opens with legal and political news: a lawyer for the US director of national intelligence said his client simply forgot the phone-records program when he told Congress in 2013 that no wittingly collected data on millions of Americans existed, and the Second Circuit appeals court ruled that the Patriot Act's section 215 does not permit bulk collection of domestic call records. On the technical side he describes the Jellyfish proof of concept, a rootkit and keylogger that lives on a GPU and reads main memory through DMA, and the broken, home-made message authentication in the Open Smart Grid Protocol used by millions of meters. He covers the UK Snoopers' Charter returning after the election and a JEDEC-derived table suggesting SSDs left unpowered may lose data in months, advising cool storage and preferring hard drives for archives. Listener questions address certificate pinning against TLS proxies, verifying TrueCrypt binaries, whitelist versus blacklist, annoying ads, ARM versus Intel, Bluetooth proximity unlock, using a VPN to evade ISP interception, trusting expected email links, and signing instead of encrypting HTTP. The show ends with a teaser for next week's keyless-entry car hack.

# Key points

- Clapper "forgot" excuse: his attorney claimed he did not prepare for or recall the Section 215 program when questioned; Steve notes the questions had been supplied a day earlier.
- ACLU v. Clapper: the Second Circuit held Section 215 cannot be read to allow systematic bulk collection of domestic call records, without ordering the program stopped; Congress faces Patriot Act renewal in June. Leo notes the cut-and-paste legal language might affect other laws.
- Jellyfish: proof-of-concept GPU malware using OpenCL; because the GPU is a bus master with DMA, code loaded from user space can read system memory, survives warm boots, and is invisible to CPU-based scanners. No in-the-wild malware yet.
- Open Smart Grid Protocol (ETSI standard, more than four million smart meters): used a home-made authentication digest that researchers broke with few observed messages; Steve says never roll your own crypto.
- UK Conservatives returning to power plan a revived Snoopers' Charter and say no communication should be unreadable by government.
- SSD retention: a JEDEC/Intel-based table gives roughly one year of unpowered retention at 30 C for client drives and about three months at 40 C for enterprise drives, with better retention when stored colder. Steve is sceptical of the practical impact, recommends cool storage, prefers hard drives for archives, and expects SpinRite to find ECC-correctable decay in SSDs.
- Video ReDo creator wrote to thank Steve; KMTTG is a tool for downloading TiVo recordings (not protected content).
- Plastc is another programmable card project after Coin; Steve is sceptical about hardware startups' manufacturing.
- Bitcoin price review: Steve still holds 50 coins from early mining.
- Cert pinning cannot prevent a TLS-intercepting proxy but detects it by fingerprint mismatch; GRC's fingerprint page and DANE with DNSSEC are alternatives to the CA chain.
- TrueCrypt 7.1a binaries: independent builders reproduced them from source; Steve hosts hashes on another site (Defuse.ca) because hashes should not come from the same place as the binary. Google and Firefox started flagging his TrueCrypt download as malware with no change in the file.
- Whitelists and blacklists both fail in practice; even perfect software leaves user mistakes. A malicious ad campaign reportedly used a fashion brand's ad slot to spread ransomware.
- Takeover ads push people to block them; Steve notes pulling ads supports sites and he sees relevant ads when logged in to Google.
- Steve points listeners to his 2010 series on computer design, including episode 242 on RISC and ARM.
- Bluetooth proximity unlock on Android: pairing is safest when out-of-band confirmation exists; Steve refers to episodes 280 and 283 and likes unlock designs requiring an initial full unlock.
- A VPN can bypass ISP interception and any ISP-installed certificate because traffic is tunnelled to a server outside the ISP's control; ISPs are unlikely to block VPNs widely because businesses need them.
- Emailed links you are expecting (such as a parking pass after registering) carry less risk than unsolicited ones; Steve sometimes opens risky email on an iPad instead of his Windows PC.
- Signing HTTP content without encrypting is possible via null-cipher suites but those were removed because of downgrade attacks.
- Leo says TWiT's multi-vendor site is moving to HTTPS everywhere with a DigiCert wildcard certificate.
- Teaser: next week covers how thieves open cars with keyless entry by relaying the fob signal; garage door openers are not affected because they need a button press.
