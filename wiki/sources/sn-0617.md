# Summary

Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte (back from vacation) open with the news that the NSA asked permission to copy the show onto its intranet. The main theme is governments reacting to strong encryption: a UK-France action plan wanting proactive content removal, identification of subscribers behind shared IP addresses and access to encrypted content; Japan's "anti-conspiracy" law covering 277 crimes; a Russian bill tying messenger accounts to phone numbers and squeezing VPNs; and German plans to read messages before encryption. WikiLeaks' Vault 7 release described CherryBlossom, a CIA firmware implant for many brands of WiFi routers. Other items cover why laptops were banned from some flights, an HP printer firmware flaw that most printers never patch, a password-length analysis, Microsoft finally disabling SMBv1 by default, a record ransomware payout by a South Korean hosting firm, Google's new Backup and Sync, vape pens as USB attack devices, and listener feedback on SQRL, voiceprints and password lockouts.

# Key points

- UK-France plan: platforms should proactively remove terrorist content, retain traffic and location data, identify subscribers even behind shared IPs, and give governments access to encrypted content "without backdoors"; Steve notes the tension with providers who control keys (iMessage, WhatsApp) versus apps such as Threema or Signal that do not.
- Japan passed a law letting prosecutors act on planning of 277 listed crimes; critics fear surveillance and civil-liberties erosion.
- Russia's bill would require identity verification for messenger users, mandate retention, and force ISPs to cut VPN providers that do not block banned sites after set deadlines.
- Germany is drafting laws to let authorities see messages before encryption; Steve says a pre-encryption tap is effectively a backdoor, while adding a warranted key to a conversation would be more controllable.
- CherryBlossom (Vault 7): remotely controlled firmware implant for routers and access points from many vendors (Belkin, D-Link, Linksys, Cisco/Aironet, Apple AirPort Express and others), developed with SRI International; enables traffic monitoring, redirection and content injection.
- Laptop travel restrictions stemmed from Israeli intelligence learning ISIS cells were building laptop-battery-lookalike bombs; Steve finds the "turn it on" screening illogical.
- Tenable analysis of HP OfficeJet Pro printers: automatic firmware updates are off by default, the JetDirect port 9100 exposed a printer-job-language file interface allowing file access and persistence; a firmware fix had existed for two months. Advice: update HP printer firmware manually.
- Analysis of 32 million passwords: 8 characters is the most common length (about 23 percent), nearly 90 percent use only lowercase letters and digits; longer passwords and a special character help.
- WannaCry attributed to a North Korean group; Microsoft will disable SMBv1 by default in the Windows 10 Redstone 3 release; downgrade attacks make leaving legacy protocols enabled dangerous.
- Erebus ransomware hit South Korean host Nayana (153 Linux servers, 3,400 sites, very old kernel, Apache and PHP); it paid about $1 million in bitcoin after negotiating down from about $4.4 million.
- Google announces Backup and Sync (June 28), cloning chosen folders to Google Drive; Steve cautions about storing sensitive data without pre-Internet encryption.
- Hackable vape pens: a USB device can present as a keyboard or network adapter; use a data-blocking USB adapter ("USB condom") when charging.
- Listener Q&A: SQRL resists DNS spoofing and man-in-the-middle via client-provided session, but no software defends against local malware; password managers can be fooled by DNS spoofing; Apple's 2FA is better than SMS since it uses iMessage; voiceprints are useful only as a disqualifier; NIST now advises against forced periodic password changes.
- Ubiquiti EdgeRouter X recommended (per-port interfaces for segmenting IoT), with a caution about slow firmware fixes on Ubiquiti wireless products.

# Other segments

- xkcd picture of the week about autocorrect and a lunch order.
- Chip-and-PIN complaints in the US; Darwin Award for thieves who stole GPS trackers.
- SpinRite listener notes (order of format versus scan, a NumLock oddity).
