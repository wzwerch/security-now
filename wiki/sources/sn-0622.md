# Summary

Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte review the fallout from Black Hat and DEF CON. At DEF CON's first Voting Machine Village, hackers compromised all 30 pieces of election equipment, leading Steve to argue that machines must be independently audited before purchase and that paper trails and standardized barcode interfaces would help. A new SMB resource-exhaustion attack, SMBloris, can crash servers with a tiny client and affects all SMB versions, and Microsoft said it would not fix it. Microsoft expanded its bug bounty up to $250,000. Broadpwn, the Broadcom WiFi flaw, was shown as a wormable, remote attack across roughly a billion phones. The week's misleading headlines about Facebook "AI inventing its own language" were traced to a mundane training quirk. Apple removed VPN apps from the Chinese App Store, iRobot walked back remarks about selling home maps, Chrome announced iframe permission restrictions and an easier certificate viewer, and WikiLeaks published more CIA Vault 7 tools.

# Key points

- Voting Machine Village: every one of about 30 machines and e-poll books tested (Diebold, Sequoia AVC Edge, ES&S iVotronic, AccuVote TSX, WinVote and others) was compromised to some degree; one e-poll book held about 650,000 voters' records. Researchers had rarely been allowed to examine these devices. Steve argues for independent audits before purchase, heterogeneity of systems and a standardized printed barcode ballot summary; Leo points to verified-voting groups and paper audit trails.
- SMBloris (Sean Dillon, RiskSense): the SMB/NetBIOS session service preallocates 128 KB of non-paged kernel memory per connection claim; with many ports one attacker can force gigabytes of allocation and crash even large servers; Microsoft said it affects all SMB versions, treats it as configuration, and sees it as hard to fix. ISPs and NAT routers usually block the SMB ports.
- Microsoft's Windows bug bounty: up to $250,000 (Hyper-V), as little as $500 for Edge bugs, and 10 percent of the top amount for previously reported issues.
- Broadpwn (Nitay Artenstein): a Broadcom BCM43xx WiFi exploit that gives code execution on the main application processor of Android and iOS devices and can be turned into a worm via WiFi probes; Apple and Google had patched in July; unpatched devices are unsafe.
- Facebook AI "language" story: two negotiating agents drifted from English because nothing rewarded using English, and researchers changed the setup; Steve notes the real concern is humans not understanding AI-to-AI communication.
- Apple removed VPN clients from the Chinese App Store; Steve sees a closed iOS ecosystem as a trade-off and suggests alternatives such as a cheap Android device.
- iRobot's CEO said he never planned to sell map data, after the earlier uproar.
- Chrome 63 will restrict powerful features in iframes by default through an allow attribute (geolocation, microphone, camera, speakers, MIDI, encrypted media); Chrome 60 offers a flag (show-cert-link) that puts a certificate viewer link under the padlock.
- Vault 7 release 18: Achilles (merges malware into legitimate Mac disk images and removes itself), SeaPea (Mac rootkit needing root) and Aeris (Linux/Unix implant with TLS-secured beaconing).
- Listener items: backing up to Amazon S3 with a small command-line tool; risk of sharing a phone hotspot with Uber riders; weak account-recovery paths undermine 2FA, and Leo found PayPal's fallback used mother's maiden name and last four Social Security digits so he changed to nonsense answers; malware on an endpoint can read Threema messages before encryption; "printing to PDF" flattens malicious PDF content.
