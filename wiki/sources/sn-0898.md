# Summary

Steve notes Firefox 107 (many high-severity fixes, no zero-days) and Google's $391.5 million settlement with 40 state attorneys general over misleading location-tracking settings, which he links to ad-profiling economics. Red Hat now publishes a GPG-signed SHA256SUM list for its zip distributions, which Steve prefers to hashes posted beside the files, though open source projects lack an easy equivalent. The New York Times found the FBI had planned to deploy NSO's Pegasus despite telling Congress it was only researched, and Greek press reported 7 million euros paid for Intellexa's Predator; Steve and Leo weigh court-supervised lawful use against abuse. 1Password supports passkeys and a community directory lists services that accept them, which Leo tries live with Carnival Cruises. The OMB ordered agencies to inventory quantum-vulnerable cryptography by May 2023 and the NSA wants quantum-resistant encryption by 2035. 33 state attorneys general urged the FTC to regulate commercial surveillance (location, biometric, health-adjacent data, data brokers, data minimisation). After Twitter-turmoil discussion, listener feedback and a SpinRite 6.1 update (detecting SSD, hybrid and shingled drives so users are warned about write-heavy levels), the main topic is Wi-Peep: researchers showed a roughly $20 ESP-based device on a drone or carried by a pedestrian can locate every WiFi device in a building to about a meter, without joining the network.

# Key points

- Firefox 107 shipped many high-severity fixes with no zero-days.
- Google/state AGs settlement ($391.5 million) stems from 2018 AP reporting that Google tracked location even with the setting off; Google points to auto-delete defaults, Maps incognito mode, and forthcoming simpler location-history deletion and clearer account setup.
- Red Hat signs a SHA256SUM file of zip artifacts with a 4,096-bit release key held on a signing server and publishes the detached `.asc` signature; Steve notes this is better than hashes next to the download, but open source maintainers lack identity-verified signing (Let's Encrypt gives only domain validation), so some GitHub-based signing with strong identity checks may be needed.
- FBI and Pegasus: bought a license in 2018 (about $5 million paid to NSO), tested it and a US-number variant called Phantom, and drafted plans to use it in criminal cases until deciding against it in July 2021; Senator Wyden objected to Director Wray's earlier testimony. Greece reportedly paid 7 million euros plus an extra fee for rotating 10 targets per month to Intellexa and then used Predator on politicians and journalists. Leo notes the vendor operates the tool, so a foreign firm would learn the customer's targets.
- 1Password added passkeys; passkeys.directory lists 43 services (e.g., Best Buy, eBay, Kayak, Microsoft, PayPal, Nvidia, Robinhood), some marked MFA. Leo's live demo shows login via QR code and phone Face ID, and the need to create a passkey per device.
- OMB memo orders federal agencies to inventory cryptography exposed to quantum attack by 4 May 2023; Steve says it is premature but supports moving early, noting one NIST candidate was recently broken classically.
- Letter from 33 state AGs to the FTC (response to its Aug 2022 rulemaking notice) asks for rules on location data, biometrics, health-adjacent data, data brokers and data minimisation, citing laws in California, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah and Virginia.
- Listener points: pfSense installs its own OS (FreeBSD) so ZimaBoard default Debian accounts do not matter there, though two NICs make it a poor router; PayPal can be asked to unconfirm a phone number to stop SMS login codes; custom recovery questions cannot be bypassed.
- Twitter discussion: Steve avoids Mastodon for now, plans an email list; recommendation of TwitterIsGoingGreat.com and a former Twitter reliability engineer's essay on automated resilience.
- SpinRite 6.1: earlier AHCI "bug" turned out to be a false positive in his hashing check; GRC server ready for test releases. SpinRite will warn users about writing to SSDs, hybrid drives and SMR drives at levels 3 and 4, so Steve bought sample drives on eBay.
- Wi-Peep (ACM MobiCom 2022): an injected fake beacon uses the 802.11 power-save mechanism to make all clients answer; devices then ack unsolicited packets even from outsiders, enabling round-trip time-of-flight distance measurements (subtracting the SIFS interval, which varies from 8 to 13 microseconds). Many noisy measurements from a moving drone or walker, collected in about two minutes, give a median error near 1.2 m across floors of a WiFi 6 house. It works with any WiFi device, needs no network access or decryption and only the device's MAC address. Implications: tracking guards, hotel occupancy, burglars, following a person's known MAC, and positive uses like hostage rescue. Mitigation requires new WiFi chips or standards that randomise the SIFS delay; Apple's MAC randomisation helps against tracking only partly.
