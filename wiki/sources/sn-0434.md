# Summary

A Q&A-labelled show that mostly stays on news. Microsoft's December Patch Tuesday brought 11 bulletins fixing 24 flaws, including actively exploited zero-days in Windows and Office, and Adobe patched Flash and Shockwave after Word documents carrying malicious Flash files were seen in attacks; Firefox 26 made Java plug-ins click-to-play. The NSA and GCHQ were reported to be piggybacking on advertising cookies such as Google's PREF identifier to track users and select hacking targets, which Steve says is easy wherever traffic is not HTTPS. SpiderLabs found a Pony botnet controller holding about two million stolen credentials, again headed by "123456". FreeBSD 10 will stop trusting hardware random number generators like Intel's on-chip one and feed them through the Yarrow generator. Steve argues the French agency ANSSI's claim that an unauthorised intermediate certificate was a "human error" is implausible, since it was installed in a traffic-inspection appliance and was caught only because Chrome pins Google certificates. Other items cover Apple's iBeacon, Touch ID reliability, SQRL's completed protocol semantics, and SSDs showing very high correction counts under SpinRite, with the advice to buy two and return the worse. The Q&A portion addresses why the show has not covered HealthCare.gov, 256-bit identifier strength and the lack of show notes.

# Key points

- December Patch Tuesday: 11 bulletins, 24 vulnerabilities (12 remote code execution, 8 privilege elevation); three critical items expected to be exploited; priority list given by Microsoft's exploitability ranking.
- Adobe fixed Flash and Shockwave; an in-the-wild attack embedded .swf files in Word attachments.
- Firefox 26: Java plug-ins become click-to-play, password manager handles script-generated fields, background update service lets non-admin users update.
- NSA/GCHQ slide shows tracking via Google's PREF cookie to enable "remote exploitation"; any unencrypted request carries cookies, so mass observers can link browsing like an ad network; login-only HTTPS leaves session cookies exposed, as the Firesheep tool showed.
- Pony botnet repository: about 1.58 million website logins, 320,000 email accounts, 41,000 FTP, 3,000 remote-desktop and 3,000 SSH credentials; top passwords were 123456, 123456789, 1234, password, and some sites accepted a single-digit password.
- FreeBSD 10 backs away from trusting CPU hardware RNGs after suspicion of NSA influence; entropy sources will be mixed through the Yarrow design (two SHA-1 pools). Steve notes hardware generators from Yubico's HSM and DIY diode circuits exist.
- ANSSI intermediate certificate: Google's Chrome detected fake Google certificates issued by an intermediate chaining to the French agency, which said it was used in a commercial device on a private network and was a human error; Steve says appliance design shows it was deliberate; Google cited Certificate Transparency.
- Apple iBeacon uses Bluetooth LE without pairing; Forbes speculates it will lead to a wallet service; Steve is wary of radio-based payment privacy and spoofing.
- Touch ID can degrade ("finger fade"), seemingly worse in the cold; remedies are retraining fingers and enrolling the same finger in several slots.
- SQRL: the protocol semantics are finished and posted; Steve will write code once feedback settles.
- SpinRite on SSDs: use the read-only Level 2; a new Kingston SSD showed nearly 3 million corrections with a 15-fold spread across regions, and SMART flagged it; densities and warranties are dropping. Compaq once pre-qualified drives with SpinRite.
- HealthCare.gov: Steve says he skipped it because it barely worked and details were unavailable; assume the worst.
- Uniqueness of 256-bit identifiers: a random 256-bit secret equals about 39 characters from a 95-symbol set, far stronger than typical usernames and passwords, and collisions are not a practical concern given good randomness.
- Show notes are now published as a PDF at GRC for each episode.
