# Summary

Steve opens with five baseline rules for Internet-connected devices (no default credentials, physical confirmation for dangerous configuration changes, minimal exposed services, automatic signed firmware updates by default, and a stated support lifetime). A researcher's immediate public disclosure of a hard-coded backdoor account and command-injection flaw in discontinued D-Link NAS models (DNS-320L, 325, 327L, 340L; about 92,000 exposed units; CVE-2024-3272 and CVE-2024-3273) led to active exploitation within days, and Steve argues silence would have been better for end-of-life devices that will never be patched. Brian Krebs exposed a network of Privnote look-alike sites that silently swap cryptocurrency addresses in notes. The zero-day brokerage market is described: Crowdfense published price lists up to $7-9 million for full-chain zero-click exploits, prices rising because platforms are harder to break. NASA found about 3 percent of Voyager 1's Flight Data Subsystem memory corrupted and hopes to work around a failed chip. SpinRite 6.1 became the product GRC sells. The main topic is the Minimum Viable Secure Product (MVSP) checklist, now with CISA joining Google, Salesforce, Okta, Slack and others; Steve walks through its business, application design, implementation and operational controls and hopes governments make it mandatory in procurement.

# Key points

- Five device principles: no vendor default credentials (generate strong random ones at first boot), physical button press for risky changes (a CISA Secure by Design idea), only essential services exposed, autonomous signed firmware updates on by default, and a publicly stated support commitment.
- D-Link NAS: backdoor username with blank password in nas_sharing.cgi plus a command injection; models end-of-sale in 2017, support ended 2019; about 92,589 reachable; GreyNoise reported exploitation within days. Lesson: do not expose unsupported devices; move them to isolated internal roles.
- Disclosure ethics: no notice to the vendor and no benefit to anyone, Steve argues, unlike a coordinated process such as Project Zero's 90-day policy.
- Privnote clones (privnote.co, privnota.com and others) were traced by Krebs to Russian-linked operators; they alter any cryptocurrency address placed in a note, and one operator threatened MetaMask with a lawsuit after being added to its phishing blocklist.
- Zero-day market: Crowdfense offers $7-9 million for SMS/MMS zero-click chains, $5-7 million for iOS, $5 million for Android, $3-5 million for WhatsApp/iMessage; Google counted 97 in-the-wild zero-days in 2023; spyware vendors responsible for most of those targeting Google products; Russia's Operation Zero offered up to $20 million. Crowdfense says it follows US sanctions; Intellexa is blocklisted after US sanctions.
- Voyager 1: 3 percent of FDS memory corrupted, probably one failed chip; engineers hope to relocate code; spacecraft is about 15 billion miles away with several instruments switched off.
- SpinRite 6.1 became the official sale version on 7 April 2024 (anniversary day); GRC's Bootable freeware helps Mac users test boot paths; the Mac and SSD refresh stories boosted sales.
- MVSP business controls: published vulnerability disclosure policy with legal safe harbor, documented triage and prompt patching, annual third-party penetration test, breach notification within 72 hours, media sanitisation, SSO at no extra charge.
- MVSP application controls: HTTPS everywhere with HSTS and Secure cookies, security headers (CSP, framing controls, no caching of sensitive API responses), password rules (no character restrictions, at least 64 characters allowed, salted memory-hard hashing, no secret questions, lockout), no default passwords, maintained dependencies with priority for CISA KEV items, logging of authentication and configuration changes kept 30 days at no charge.
- MVSP operational controls: patch material vulnerabilities within 90 days, access reviews and removal of stale accounts, MFA for remote access to production or customer data, inventory of third parties with data access, encrypted data in transit and at rest, backups.
- CISA frames MVSP as a "secure by demand" procurement tool; Steve hopes governments will require it.
