# Summary

The episode opens with the growing Salt Typhoon victim list: Viasat has acknowledged a breach through a compromised device, and Canada's Cyber Centre warned that three devices at a Canadian telecom were hit in February 2025, adding to US carriers, Comcast and Digital Realty. The Markup found four more state health-insurance exchanges (Nevada, Maine, Massachusetts, Rhode Island) sending medication names, doctors and other sensitive answers to Google, LinkedIn and Snapchat through web trackers. Apple announced at WWDC a secure import/export mechanism for passkeys, passwords and verification codes built on a FIDO Alliance schema, Facebook adds passkey login, TikTok's deadline was extended another 90 days, Microsoft will start expiring legacy drivers from Windows Update, and Austria agreed a plan for court-approved monitoring of secure messengers starting in 2027. Steve then shares an o3 answer about Windows 10 Schannel lacking TLS 1.3 and develops the idea that language models are linguistic simulations of intelligent entities, not intelligence. The main topic is Cisco Talos's "Weathering the Storm" analysis: Salt Typhoon relied largely on old, already-patched Cisco flaws (CVE-2018-0171, and the CVSS 10.0 web UI bypass CVE-2023-20198 that exposed about 42,000 devices in 2023), stolen credentials, and on-device packet capture and persistence, and Steve argues that relying on customers to patch and harden edge gear has failed.

# Key points

- Salt Typhoon victim list now includes Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile, Spectrum, Lumen, Charter, Consolidated Communications, Windstream, Comcast, Digital Realty and Viasat; Viasat says no customer impact was found and the issue is remediated. In 2022 Russian actors had wiped Viasat KA-SAT modems with AcidRain.
- Canada's Cyber Centre and the FBI: three network devices at a Canadian telecom were compromised in mid-February 2025 via CVE-2023-20198, with configuration files retrieved and a GRE tunnel added for traffic collection; the actors are expected to keep targeting Canadian organizations for two years. Steve notes carriers' claims of full eviction cannot be substantiated.
- Steve's thesis: because post-sale patching of perimeter equipment demonstrably does not happen, devices must ship without exploitable flaws and without insecure defaults; Leo points out that a Biden-era executive order on vendor liability has been rolled back.
- The Markup: state exchanges used dozens of trackers (California 63, Nevada 49, Maryland 31, Massachusetts 28; Washington, D.C. none). Prescription names and dosages, doctors, and answers about pregnancy or disability reached Google, LinkedIn and Snapchat; some data flowed via a contractor-run comparison tool. States cut trackers after being contacted; trackers inevitably see visitor IP addresses, and class actions and HIPAA exposure follow.
- Apple at WWDC 2025: secure, user-initiated transfer of passkeys, passwords and verification codes between credential managers (no plaintext export files), using a FIDO Alliance data schema also supported by 1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane and others. Leo notes some sites still ask for a one-time code after a passkey login, which defeats the purpose.
- TikTok's forced-sale deadline was extended a third time by 90 days.
- Microsoft will periodically expire old drivers that have newer replacements from Windows Update (six-month window for vendors to object), reducing Bring-Your-Own-Vulnerable-Driver risk.
- Austria's coalition agreed to let police monitor messaging apps after a three-judge approval, expected for 25-30 people per year, with technology tendered and monitoring starting in 2027; Steve and Leo doubt it can work without breaking encryption for everyone.
- Steve's o3 example: asked how to use Windows 10's Schannel for TLS 1.3, o3 correctly answered it cannot (TLS 1.3 arrived with Windows 11 and Server 2022), explaining the back-ported constants that cause confusion. Steve's conclusion: LLMs are linguistic simulations of intelligent entities whose output is indistinguishable from the real thing, so "simulated intelligence" is a better term; Leo says claims like shutdown disobedience just reflect simulated human behavior.
- Cisco Talos (Weathering the Storm): initial access was usually valid stolen credentials; only one confirmed Cisco-vulnerability case (CVE-2018-0171 Smart Install, CVSS 9.8), though CVE-2023-20198 and 20273 (IOS XE web UI) and CVE-2024-20399 (NX-OS) were also tied to the campaign. Actors exfiltrated device configurations (including weakly encrypted passwords and SNMP strings), captured SNMP/TACACS/RADIUS traffic, pivoted between carriers, created GRE tunnels, rogue accounts and ACL changes, enabled Guest Shell and alternate SSH servers, set up packet capture exported by FTP, cleared logs, and used a custom Go utility called JumbledPath for chained remote captures. One intrusion persisted for more than three years.
- Steve recalls episode 945 (Oct 2023): the CVSS 10.0 IOS XE flaw let attackers compromise about 42,000 devices within weeks, and the apparent drop in vulnerable devices came from attackers closing the hole behind them. He faults Cisco's design allowing internet-wide exposure of the management interface and a "Hardening Guide" approach; he says insecure configurations should require deliberate effort.
- Talos advice: configuration auditing, monitor syslog/AAA for gaps, fingerprint devices for new open ports, check for large bash history files, and patch or decommission legacy Smart Install devices.
