# Summary

Steve starts with a self-inflicted problem: October's Patch Tuesday enforcement of the TLS Extended Master Secret requirement broke Windows TLS connections (and thus Chrome, but not Firefox) to servers lacking the extension, and a registry workaround restored access. Chrome 78 fixed a Kaspersky-reported zero-day exploited through a watering-hole on a Korean news portal (Operation WizardOpium) and temporarily turned off code-integrity enforcement because other security products also broke. Other items: the Chromium-based Edge going stable in January, two years of speculative-execution side-channel fallout, QSnatch malware on thousands of QNAP NAS boxes, Armor's tally of managed service providers that spread ransomware, a rental car still controllable through the FordPass app months later, and the US Interior Department grounding Chinese-made drones. The closing topics are Comcast's lobbying deck against browser DNS-over-HTTPS, which Steve rebuts point by point, and the first in-the-wild BlueKeep exploitation, delivering a Monero miner without worm behavior.

# Key points

- Microsoft's October update enforced Extended Master Secret for ephemeral Diffie-Hellman TLS cipher suites (a fix for a TLS spoofing weakness); connections to servers without EMS hang or time out. The workaround is two SCHANNEL registry values disabling client and server EMS enforcement; Steve expects a Microsoft correction.
- Chrome's renderer code-integrity protection broke other products beyond Symantec (PC Matic, Palo Alto Traps, a print auditing tool); Chrome 78 disables it, to return around mid-November.
- Chrome 78.0.3904.87 fixes a zero-day (reported by Kaspersky, same-day patch): a race condition producing use-after-free, exploited since about Chrome 65 via a compromised Korean news site that profiled visitors, delivered RC4-encrypted exploit chunks, bypassed ASLR with heap grooming, and installed a payload with scheduled-task persistence.
- Chromium-based Edge stable is due January 15; Steve wonders why users prefer Chrome.
- Roughly 20% performance has been sacrificed for Spectre/Meltdown-class mitigations with no known real-world exploitation; new Intel designs will take years.
- QSnatch infected thousands of QNAP NAS devices (7,000+ in Germany): persists in firmware, blocks firmware updates and the built-in malware remover, steals NAS credentials, and can load modules; recovery requires a factory reset plus the November 1 firmware, credential changes and an access control list; units not exposed to the Internet were not at risk.
- Armor identified 13 MSPs and cloud providers used to push ransomware to clients in 2019; one dental IT provider closed after an attack. Advice: limit what remote access an outside provider gets.
- A man's FordPass app retained control of a rental SUV for five months; Steve proposes automatic unpairing of phones not near the vehicle for a set time.
- The Interior Department grounded Chinese-made drones (excepting emergency uses) pending review; DJI offers a Government Edition; Steve suggests open firmware.
- Comcast's slide deck against browser DoH claims centralization, national security and antitrust harms; Steve notes Chrome keeps the user's chosen DNS provider if it supports DoH, avoids overriding enterprise private DNS, and that Comcast itself lobbied in 2017 to sell browsing data. Mozilla says it is paid nothing for using Cloudflare and will add providers meeting its privacy terms.
- BlueKeep: honeypots crashed over the weekend; Marcus Hutchins found Metasploit-derived exploit code and a PowerShell-delivered Monero miner; mass exploitation from a target list, not a worm; about 724,000 unpatched systems remain exposed on port 3389.
