# Summary

Steve covers news revealed at Kaspersky's analyst summit. Google softened Project Zero's 90-day disclosure policy by adding a 14-day grace period if a vendor announces a patch date, moving deadlines that fall on weekends or US holidays, and pre-assigning CVE numbers, after reporting that 85 percent of its 154 fixed bugs met the deadline; Steve and Leo find the tone arrogant but welcome the flexibility. The Carbanak group had stolen hundreds of millions of dollars, possibly a billion, from about 100 banks in 30 countries since 2013 by spear phishing, watching staff screens, then making ATMs dispense cash to mules or inflating and quickly draining account balances. Kaspersky's "Equation Group" report describes malware that rewrites hard drive firmware on more than a dozen drive brands, surviving reformatting and reinstalls, matching the NSA IrateMonk slide leaked by Snowden. Steve recommends freezing credit reports at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion for a small fee to protect against identity theft after breaches such as Anthem. The main topic is HTTP/2: why HTTP/1.1 struggles with modern asset-heavy pages and multiple parallel TCP and TLS connections, and how HTTP/2, derived from Google's SPDY, uses a single connection, binary frames with stream IDs, priorities and dependencies, header compression with one shared context per connection, and server push. Draft 17 dated 11 February 2015 is nearly final and browsers and servers are adopting it.

# Key points

- Project Zero changes: 14-day grace period when a vendor commits to a patch date, deadlines moved off weekends and US holidays, and CVE assignment before disclosure. Google says the share of deadline misses fell from 15 percent to about 5 percent for recent issues; Steve notes Microsoft's monthly patch cycle makes 90 days awkward.
- Steve keeps SHA-1 certificates expiring at year's end to avoid Chrome's early warning; a viral video shows a user baffled by Chrome's red mark on HTTPS.
- Carbanak: infection via spear-phishing, two to four months of lateral movement and video surveillance of bank staff, then cash-outs via ATMs or fake balance edits; attacks capped near 10 million dollars per bank; losses absorbed by banks, which had kept the incidents quiet.
- Equation Group: a highly capable actor, judged to be part of the NSA, whose modules reprogram HDD and SSD firmware, give persistence beyond reformatting, invisibility and hidden storage, and use zero-days that predate Stuxnet and Regin; hard drive command sets allow writing but not reading firmware, yet some drives store firmware off-chip on ARM-based controllers or expose debugging interfaces, so reverse engineering or access via vendor NDAs is plausible.
- Credit freeze: lock reports at the three bureaus for a one-time fee (about 10 dollars each in California) and receive a PIN to thaw; this blocks new credit in your name but also legitimate lending.
- SpinRite on SSDs: wear leveling relocates rewritten sectors, but SpinRite still helps because the recovered data is reread or relocated and bad regions are opportunistically mapped out.
- HTTP/1.1 problems: one request at a time per connection, browsers open six to eight parallel TCP connections, each with TCP slow start and a TLS handshake, and every request repeats bulky headers and cookies.
- HTTP/2 design: no backward compatibility; one connection per server; a signaling pseudo cipher suite (ALPN) in the TLS negotiation; frames with a 9-byte header (length, type, flags, 31-bit stream ID) carried over the TCP stream; many simultaneous streams multiplexed and interleaved; client-set stream priorities and dependencies; unchanged HTTP semantics inside the streams; one compression context per connection so repeated headers shrink to pointers; server push of resources the client will likely need. Draft 17 was current; Apache, nginx, Firefox, Chrome, Safari and the Windows 10 preview support it, and Chrome will drop SPDY in 2016.
- Steve notes HTTP/2 allows non-TLS use, but in practice nobody deploys it unencrypted, and clients and servers will need smarter logic over time.
