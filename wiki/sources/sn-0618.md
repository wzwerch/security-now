# Summary

Steve Gibson reviews a week of research of varying usefulness. NIST is starting a lightweight cryptography standardization process for constrained IoT devices, which Steve calls necessary but not sufficient. Daniel Bernstein and co-authors showed RSA could in principle survive quantum factoring only with absurd key sizes (an eight-terabit public key), while real post-quantum schemes already exist. Cisco's claim of detecting malware in encrypted traffic with 99 percent accuracy appears to rest on metadata and DNS context, and the paper reports no false-negative rate. Guido Vranken's fuzzing found new OpenVPN bugs after two audits; Steve argues fuzzing and code audits complement each other. A hyperthreading microcode bug affects Intel Skylake and Kaby Lake CPUs. NaviStone-style scripts capture form fields before submission and track people across sites. Also covered: WannaCry reappearing at a Honda plant and Australian traffic cameras, Linksys's CherryBlossom advisory, Australia joining the push for encryption backdoors, and the NSA's GitHub projects.

# Key points

- The 2015 "In this corner we have Dave" human-error cartoon returns as picture of the week; Steve backs Leo's view that third-party antivirus hooks destabilize the OS and Windows Defender is sufficient; whitelisting is the real fix. Windows 10 S blocks browser access to localhost, which removes SQRL's client-provided session protection.
- NIST Internal Report 8114 on lightweight cryptography: a portfolio of algorithms for small devices, to be recommended only within profiles; trade-offs include smaller S-boxes and more rounds; standards remove the cost excuse for IoT crypto.
- Post-quantum RSA paper (Bernstein et al.): with quantum computers, RSA can be pushed beyond known quantum attacks only using keys on the order of eight terabits; the authors introduce a new batch prime generator and a quantum factoring method faster than Shor's. Not practical; lattice and other post-quantum schemes are the real answer.
- Cisco claims 99 percent detection of malware in encrypted traffic without decryption; Steve points out the underlying research uses TLS handshake, DNS and HTTP context, reports a 0.00 percent false positive rate but says nothing about misses, and advises skepticism and lab testing.
- OpenVPN: Guido Vranken found four bugs (crashes and double frees) by fuzzing after audits; fixed in updated releases; Steve says audits find crypto and design issues fuzzing cannot, so both are needed.
- Intel Skylake and Kaby Lake: short loops using the high-byte registers (AH, BH, CH, DH) with hyperthreading active can cause unpredictable behavior; found via an OCaml compiler; microcode patches are being prepared; Haswell and earlier are unaffected.
- NaviStone: JavaScript on many shopping sites captures typed form data before the Submit button and links it across sites, enabling emails or postcards to people who never submitted anything; Gizmodo found more than 100 sites using it; Leo notes cart abandonment emails are common but this crosses a line; NaviStone backed off after coverage.
- WannaCry resurfaced: Honda's Sayama plant halted production for about two days; 55 Australian traffic cameras were infected via a USB drive.
- Linksys advisory (June 21) on CherryBlossom: update firmware, set a strong admin password, disable guest access, WPS and UPnP.
- Australia's attorney general will push for encryption backdoors at the Five Eyes meeting.
- The NSA runs a public GitHub presence (e.g. Apache Accumulo, tools to find unexpected CA certificates on Windows).
- Listener follow-ups: no tested HP printer enables automatic firmware update by default; correction that Apple 2FA does not use iMessage; formatting no longer verifies drive surfaces, so scanning drives with SpinRite is worthwhile; wait on brand-new VPN services; TeraByte Image for Windows recommended for imaging (Leo mentions the Windows 7-style backup); review OAuth app permissions on Google, Twitter and Facebook; "password" has eight characters.
