# Summary

After a one-week break for Independence Day, Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte cover "the usual suspects" ahead of Black Hat. The W3C added Encrypted Media Extensions (DRM support) to HTML5 without protecting security researchers from DMCA anti-circumvention claims. OpenBSD introduced KARL, which relinks a freshly randomized kernel in the background for each next boot. Apple's bug bounty is judged a failure because gray-market jailbreak exploits sell for around $1.5 million. Chrome will end its conditional trust of WoSign and StartCom, Let's Encrypt will offer wildcard certificates in January 2018, and a Libgcrypt side-channel attack on RSA was overhyped as "cracking" encryption. Broadpwn, a zero-click Broadcom WiFi firmware flaw, is the week's Android scare. Steve praises Microsoft's Controlled Folder Access whitelisting and recommends DNSthingy for DNS-level per-device controls.

# Key points

- W3C approved EME for HTML5; Steve thinks a standard container for per-vendor content decryption modules was inevitable but regrets that no exemption for security researchers was written into the spec.
- OpenBSD KARL (Kernel Address Randomized Link): each boot spawns a background relink of the kernel with randomized object order for the next boot, far finer-grained than ASLR; Linux developers are interested; Windows unlikely.
- Apple's bug bounty (announced at Black Hat 2016 with payouts up to $200,000) is not attracting top exploits since multi-exploit iOS jailbreaks fetch about $1.5 million on the gray market.
- WoSign misissued a certificate for GitHub's root domain after proof of control of a subdomain and failed to disclose or revoke related issues; Chrome 61 will drop the remaining WoSign and StartCom trust (stable around mid-September 2017); Symantec faces a similar process.
- Let's Encrypt will begin issuing wildcard domain-validated certificates in January 2018.
- Libgcrypt (GnuPG) RSA-1024 key recovery via an L3 cache side channel exploits sliding-window exponentiation leaks; efficient for 13 percent of RSA-2048 keys; this is an implementation flaw, not a break of RSA.
- A judge dismissed (with prejudice for privacy and wiretap claims) the suit claiming Facebook tracks logged-out users via Like buttons; Steve explains that cookies are sent to third-party domains regardless of login state.
- China ordered the three big carriers to block VPNs by February 2018; Steve doubts a complete block is feasible but expects loss of convenience.
- DJI is pushing firmware to break drone jailbreaks of its no-fly-zone and altitude limits; DMCA exemptions do not clearly cover drones.
- Broadpwn: Broadcom BCM43xx WiFi firmware heap overflow reachable by a malformed quality-of-service element from any nearby access point without user interaction; patched in the July 5 Android bulletin; iOS impact unknown pending the Black Hat talk; unpatched Android devices cannot be used securely.
- Windows 10 Fall Creators Update adds Controlled Folder Access, letting only allowed apps write to protected folders; Steve sees this as a clever, constrained form of whitelisting against cryptomalware.
- An IoT story: a voice assistant misheard a shouted phrase as a command and called 911 during a domestic violence incident.
- Upcoming Black Hat talk "Skype & Type": typing sounds over Skype can reveal passwords.
- Vault 7 OutlawCountry: a CIA Linux kernel module that redirects a targeted machine's outbound traffic via a hidden Netfilter table.
- Firewalla Kickstarter and DNSthingy (DNS-based per-device controls, ad blocking, time limits, restricting IoT devices to their own service domains).
- Preview of the password reset man-in-the-middle research for next week; a video visualizing 2^256 recommended.
- Why primes matter: multiplying primes is easy and factoring the product is hard, the trapdoor behind RSA; elliptic curves reach equal strength with much smaller keys.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a fake "is your credit card in a hacker database" form.
- SpinRite: a Btrfs user's SSD sped up after a Level 2 scan; a 1988 BYTE review of SpinRite; 39 years of the program.
- Net Neutrality "Internet Freedom Day" protest announced; Security Now T-shirts.
