# Summary

This catch-up Q&A episode starts with follow-ups: Steve responds to listener outrage about his remarks on Apple unlocking phones under warrant (he stands by it as a possible compromise), notes Mozilla's revenue (about 330 million dollars) after leaving a Google-only search deal, and reports that Microsoft revoked the Dell eDellRoot certificates through its certificate trust list. New stories include a cable-modem backdoor in several Arris models based on a daily generated password with a shared default seed (over 600,000 exposed devices found with Shodan), BlackBerry again refusing Pakistan's demand for unfettered server access, and a patent-holding firm suing 66 then 67 companies, claiming elliptic-curve TLS use infringes a 2001 patent. Adobe renamed Flash Professional to Animate CC as HTML5 takes over. Steve explains why SpinRite scans on huge drives take time and promises a faster 6.1. The ten questions cover programming-language rankings, a router-intercept side effect with HSTS, DDoS against ISPs, blocking only third-party scripts, whether built-in disk encryption is enough, a decoy-finger idea for fingerprint unlock, the Windows Scripting Host running double-clicked script files, how many primes exist, an online bank limiting passwords to eight characters, and monitoring a router with NetWorx via SNMP.

# Key points

- Policy follow-up: Steve acknowledges criticism of his stance that warrant-based unlocking by Apple could be reasonable and says it was an observation about political reality, not a preference.
- Mozilla's 2014 revenue was about 330 million dollars, now from regional default search deals (Yahoo in the US, Baidu in China, Yandex in Russia).
- Microsoft updated its certificate trust list to distrust the Dell certificates; automatic on Windows 8 and later, and optionally installable on Vista and 7.
- Arris: a hidden library generates a daily changing password from a seed that is identical across models; a second factor is just the last digits of the serial number. Steve stresses that algorithmic backdoor secrets always leak and that per-device random secrets, or none, are the only safe option. Comcast reportedly changed its seed.
- BlackBerry declined Pakistan's deadline-bound demand for full access to its enterprise servers, as it did with other countries in 2010.
- Patent troll: a firm holding a patent granted in 2001 (expiring 2018) sued dozens of large online retailers, travel, media and finance companies for using elliptic-curve cipher suites; Netflix moved to dismiss; users, not implementers, are liable under patent law, and settling is often cheaper than fighting.
- Adobe Flash Professional becomes Animate CC, shifting to HTML5 Canvas and WebGL; Steve sees another step in Flash's decline, driven by iOS and mobile.
- SpinRite: a case where a Level 2 scan crawled through a failing region and revived a laptop; Steve says 6.1 will scan about a terabyte in two hours.
- TIOBE: Java number one, C second; assembly ranks eleventh, likely embedded devices.
- HSTS side effect: a router's captive-page intercept makes the browser show an HSTS failure and hides the real outage.
- DDoS against ISPs is uncommon because ISPs have many address blocks and diffuse peering.
- uBlock Origin advanced mode can block third-party scripts globally or per site, but may break sites.
- FileVault and BitLocker suffice against thieves; open source is preferable if the adversary is a government.
- Decoy "panic finger" idea for biometric unlock to avoid compelled access.
- Windows Scripting Host runs .js and .vbs files on double-click without warning, which can infect a careless user (Leo verified on Windows 10).
- Primes remain dense at any size, so large random primes for RSA are found by testing random candidates.
- A major bank's new password policy advertised 14 characters but only accepted eight; Steve says eight characters offers about 48 bits of entropy.
- NetWorx can monitor the whole network via the router's SNMP counters; Steve enabled SNMP on pfSense.
