# Summary

Steve starts with Utah becoming the first state to require app stores to verify ages and obtain parental consent, sketches a browser-based age-and-jurisdiction disclosure model, and praises Apple's new Declared Age Range API as close to what he has wanted. A long segment follows Roger Grimes of KnowBe4 interviewing a suspected North Korean fake job candidate, a reminder that the remote-worker scheme is widespread and can turn into extortion. Bybit updates cover how the stolen Ether was laundered (THORChain, mixers) and the finding that the Safe{Wallet} compromise began with a social-engineering attack on a developer who ran a malicious Docker file. Other items: Apple's appeal to the UK Investigatory Powers Tribunal, the Passkeys "crack" that was really a browser FIDO:// URL-handling bug, a UK law firm trying to force a US breach-news site to remove posts, LastPass-sourced thefts still surfacing, and an Akira ransomware case where an unmonitored Linux webcam was used to encrypt servers protected by EDR. The headline "Bluetooth Backdoor" is taken apart: Tarlogic found 29 undocumented vendor HCI commands in the ESP32 firmware, but Steve judges them ordinary host-side controller commands, not a remotely exploitable backdoor.

# Key points

- Utah passed the App Store Accountability Act (first in the US); Meta, Snap and X back app-store enforcement while Apple prefers narrower measures and the Chamber of Progress cites First Amendment concerns. The Supreme Court is due to weigh in on age verification for adult sites.
- Steve's framework: a standardized W3C/browser mechanism where a site signals it wants age and jurisdiction, and the user chooses to disclose an integer age once. The hard unsolved part is a trustworthy source of the age that cannot be spoofed.
- Apple's "Helping Protect Kids Online" paper: simpler Child Account setup with safe defaults, parents can correct a child's age, and a Declared Age Range API shares only an age range with developers if parents allow it. Apple argues store-level ID checks are not data minimization. Steve likes it but would rather have parents enter a date of birth; Leo suspects Apple deliberately avoids knowing more.
- Picture of the Week: Minnesota's unemployment-insurance login demanding passwords of exactly six characters with no special characters.
- KnowBe4 / Roger Grimes: North Korea runs large teams of fake remote developers (often based in China, Russia or Malaysia), uses synthetic or stolen identities, paid local intermediaries and "laptop farmers", and funds weapons programs. Telltale signs from the interview: delayed answers (likely searching), wrong technical answers, accent mismatched with claimed origin, and cultural-knowledge gaps. When discovered, such workers may pivot to data theft or ransomware.
- Bybit: about 77 percent of the 1.46 billion USD remained traceable, about 20 percent went dark via mixers; most was swapped to Bitcoin through THORChain across roughly 7,000 wallets. Eleven parties helped freeze funds. Steve corrected his earlier slip: the 10 percent bounty is 150 million, not 150,000.
- Safe{Wallet} post-mortem: a developer was socially engineered into running a malicious Docker file, malware took his AWS credentials, and attackers injected code into Safe's front end targeting Bybit's multisig. Safe then added raw transaction display and removed some hardware-wallet support. Blind signing on hardware wallets is the underlying weakness.
- Apple appealed to the Investigatory Powers Tribunal against the UK technical capability notice; the Home Office will not confirm the order exists. Trump and DNI Gabbard criticised the UK demand. Leo thinks Apple leaked it deliberately and that pulling ADP is a de facto warrant canary.
- Passkeys: not cracked. A researcher found an account-takeover path that depended on mobile Chrome, Edge, Safari and Firefox letting pages navigate to FIDO:// links; fixed between October 2024 and February 2025.
- UK healthcare provider HCRG (ex-Virgin Care) obtained a High Court injunction and sent its law firm after DataBreaches.net over reporting on a Medusa ransomware attack; the US site and its registrar refused to comply.
- The FBI recovered 23 million USD in XRP stolen from Ripple's Chris Larsen, loot traced to the 2022 LastPass vault theft; weak PBKDF2 iteration counts on old vaults made cracking easy.
- Akira ransomware case: after EDR blocked their payload on Windows servers, the attackers scanned the network, found an unmonitored Linux-based webcam with known vulnerabilities, ran the Linux encryptor from it and encrypted shares over SMB. Mitigation: segment IoT devices (for example a separate VLAN) and monitor their traffic.
- ESP32 "backdoor": Tarlogic (RootedCON, Madrid) built a hardware-independent USB Bluetooth driver and, using Ghidra, found 29 undocumented HCI commands (opcode 3F), tracked as CVE-2025-27840. BleepingComputer retitled its story from "backdoor" to "undocumented commands". Steve (after translating the slides with ChatGPT) sees no remote attack path: commands such as read/write memory or set MAC are host-side controller functions, and anyone able to issue them already controls the device. He calls it a mouse hole.

# Other segments

- TWiT's 20th anniversary approaching (13 April) and a call for listener videos; Steve's mailing-list verification system.
- Leo references Neal Agarwal's password-rules game and recalls early export-restricted 40-bit crypto.
- Steve repeats the TNO / Pre-Internet Encryption advice: encrypt data yourself before it reaches a cloud.
