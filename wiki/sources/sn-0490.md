# Summary

News first. A listener reports that CryptoWall running under a non-admin account cannot touch Windows shadow copies, so files can be restored after cleanup; AppLocker can also be configured with the local policy editor on Ultimate or Enterprise editions without Active Directory; SPDY support is being replaced by HTTP/2; and the FCC signals Title II for net neutrality. Brian Krebs reported that the Lizard Squad's attack botnet is largely made of compromised home routers with default credentials, which scan for and infect other routers. The Notepad++ site was defaced after the Paris attacks, Windows 7 moved to extended support through January 2020, and Google published a Windows privilege-escalation bug after its 90-day deadline passed two days before Microsoft's Patch Tuesday, prompting a Microsoft complaint. Steve also warns that roughly 60 percent of Android devices run a pre-4.4 WebView that Google said it will not patch, while Metasploit added 11 exploits for it. US Central Command's Twitter and YouTube accounts were briefly hijacked, and British prime minister David Cameron said communications that the state cannot read should not be allowed, which Steve and Leo debate. The main topic is the Enigma cipher machine: its commercial origin with Arthur Scherbius, the Polish mathematicians who reverse-engineered it, the keyboard, plugboard, three rotors and reflector design, the daily codebook plus per-message key, its roughly 10^23 settings and the design weakness that no letter ever encrypts to itself, which, with guessed plaintext such as weather reports, let Turing's bombe machines reject wrong settings.

# Key points

- CryptoWall recovery tip: with a non-admin account, shadow copies (System Restore versions) remain intact; clean the malware, then use an admin account and a shadow-copy viewer to restore earlier versions.
- AppLocker can be managed through the local policy editor on Windows 7 Ultimate and Enterprise; Windows Family Safety offers a simpler whitelist-style interface elsewhere.
- IE11 had flaky SPDY support and will move to HTTP/2, which brings multiplexing, binary framing, compression and server push over one connection.
- FCC chair Tom Wheeler hinted at Title II common-carrier rules for broadband as the basis for net neutrality.
- Lizard Squad's botnet uses consumer routers with default logins and unpatched backdoors that scan for and infect more routers. Advice: change default credentials, turn off remote management, and keep firmware updated.
- Notepad++ website defaced after a "Je suis Charlie" release; Windows 7 mainstream support ended 13 January 2015 with security updates until January 2020.
- Google's Project Zero publicly released details of a local Windows privilege-escalation flaw when its fixed 90-day deadline lapsed; Microsoft objected in a blog post, noting its monthly patch calendar makes the effective deadline stricter.
- Android WebView before 4.4, used by about 61 percent of Android devices, got 11 Metasploit modules; Google said it would not fix pre-4.4 WebView issues itself and only accepts patches. Steve predicts a long-running problem with a billion unpatched phones.
- CENTCOM's Twitter and YouTube accounts were vandalized for about 30 minutes by self-styled "CyberCaliphate"; the Pentagon said its networks were not compromised.
- David Cameron said the UK should not allow communications it cannot read even with a warrant; Steve fears a ban on end-to-end encryption such as WhatsApp or iMessage; Leo thinks it is unenforceable, noting open-source tools like TextSecure.
- Enigma history: Germany's First World War cipher was broken without their knowledge; Arthur Scherbius patented the Enigma as a commercial product; Polish mathematicians reverse-engineered it from intercepts, built clones and an early "bombe," and shared everything with the British and French shortly before the war; Germany changed its procedure on 1 May 1940, which broke the Polish method.
- Enigma design: 26-key keyboard (no digits or punctuation, numbers sent via a Y prefix), a plugboard swapping letter pairs, three rotors chosen from five, a reflector, and lamps. Pressing a key steps the rightmost rotor like an odometer, producing a polyalphabetic cipher that changes with each letter. Settings counted: 60 rotor orders, 676 ring settings, 17,576 start positions and about 150 trillion plugboard wirings, for around 10^23 combinations; Germany limited messages to about 250 characters.
- Operation: a codebook gave daily rotor order, ring settings, plugboard pairs and start positions; each operator also chose three random letters, sent them twice as a message key, and the receiver, with identical settings, decrypted symmetrically thanks to the reflector.
- Weaknesses: the reflector meant no letter could encode to itself, and the repeated message key was exploited by the Poles; captured codebooks gave windfalls. Steve says Turing's bombes searched for settings consistent with guessed plaintext (such as "weather report") and rejected impossible ones, and that the film has many historical inaccuracies, including a false blackmail subplot and a fake midnight deadline.

# Other segments

- Steve and Leo's reaction to "The Imitation Game": a great film but loose with history, with a Guardian article cataloging errors.
- Listener SpinRite testimonial: a drive with bad sectors was cloned successfully to an SSD after SpinRite.
