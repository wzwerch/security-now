# Summary

Steve first covers, at a purely technical level, the 17-18 September 2024 detonation of thousands of Hezbollah pagers and hundreds of handheld radios, using a CryptoMuseum write-up: a tampered supply chain via intermediary or shell companies, hidden explosive, and a remote trigger. He then discusses a Ford patent application for in-car advertising based on listening to occupant conversation (noting that patents often do not reflect product plans), and the Cybernews finding that MC2 Data left a 2.2 TB database with 106 million US records unprotected. Google announced Chrome-native Passkeys sync with a Google Password Manager PIN. A researcher using the handle evilsocket teased an unauthenticated Linux remote code execution (rated 9.9) with a disputed disclosure, due in about two weeks. Listener feedback covers credit freezes (Canada, an Experian account mismatch), Drobo versus Synology, GRC's email reputation problems, VPNs on open Wi-Fi, CPE credit proof and Microsoft Defender XDR. The main topic is Kaspersky's forced exit from the US after the Commerce Department ban: its software was silently replaced by UltraAV, and Steve uses it to reflect on technology fragmenting along geopolitical lines.

# Key points

- Pager and radio attack: about 5,000 Gold Apollo AR-924 pagers (supplied via a Hungarian licensee, BAC Consulting) reached Hezbollah, around 4,000 exploded on 17 September 2024 after a specially crafted message (at least 12 killed, about 2,750 injured); the next day roughly 450 counterfeit ICOM IC-V82 radios also exploded; a Bulgarian company was investigated.
- Steve's reading: a textbook physical supply-chain attack requiring custom firmware, a hidden charge without X-ray markers and a long preparation time; devices were likely modified before Hezbollah ordered them. He keeps politics out and stresses collateral harm; Leo notes this is a rare, decade-scale operation, not an everyday risk.
- Ford patent application (published 29 August 2024) describes using in-cabin audio and location history to select ads; Ford says patents do not indicate product plans. Steve explains patent departments emit ideas continuously, while still seeing a trend toward ad-saturated vehicles and data used by insurers.
- MC2 Data (PrivateRecords.net, PeopleSearcher and similar sites) exposed 2.2 TB, 106,316,633 records plus 2.3 million subscriber records (names, addresses, birth dates, phones, relatives, property and legal records, partial payment data); no Social Security numbers seen, but it could be merged with the National Public Data leak.
- Chrome now saves and syncs Passkeys in Google Password Manager on Windows, macOS, Linux and Android (ChromeOS in beta), protected by a new Google Password Manager PIN so Google cannot read them; Steve calls it a huge step, adds that it locks users into Google's ecosystem, and notes the PIN resembles a feature his SQRL had.
- Linux claim: Simone Margaritelli (evilsocket) says an unauthenticated RCE affecting GNU/Linux and BSD systems was reported three weeks earlier, vendors such as Canonical and Red Hat confirmed severity 9.9, no fix or CVEs yet, and he is abandoning responsible disclosure because developers dispute the impact. Steve urges waiting for facts before panic.
- Credit freezes: in Canada freezes depend on provincial law (reportedly only Quebec offers them); a listener found Experian linked a new account to another person's credit file because only part of the first name and last four SSN digits were matched. Steve wants freezes on by default.
- Drobo went out of business; Steve still uses a Drobo 5N behind NAT with Syncthing syncing his wife's remote neurofeedback client laptops, but prefers Synology (running Syncthing natively) and advises replacing Drobo if possible.
- GRC email: bounce rate above 10% at 2008-era addresses, Apple and others routing mail to junk; Google and Postmark require spam complaint rates under 0.1%; Steve recommends nuevoMailer as a sending backend; listeners are asked to mark messages "not spam".
- Public Wi-Fi: a VPN protects traffic on open or captive-portal hotspots; since nearly all web traffic is now HTTPS (versus the era of Firesheep, episode 272), open Wi-Fi is much less risky without a VPN, though DNS and destination IPs remain visible.
- A listener notes Microsoft Defender XDR (in M365 E5) is comparable to CrowdStrike; Leo agrees to show episode number and date near the end of the show for CPE-credit screenshots.
- Kaspersky exit: after the Commerce Department ban on sales and updates from 29 September 2024, Kaspersky pushed an update on 19 September that removed its software from US PCs and installed UltraAV (Pango Group, which also runs Hotspot Shield, UltraVPN, Betternet and the Comparitech review site) without clear warning; some users feared malware, and subscriber data was evidently transferred.
- Steve reviews Kaspersky's record of discovering Stuxnet-era threats, Flame, Red October, Mask, Regin, Equation Group and Duqu 2.0, notes no evidence of wrongdoing, yet accepts the national-security logic; warns of internet fragmentation (Russia's disconnect tests, expected US ban on Chinese and Russian connected-car software) and discusses with Leo the need for zero-trust thinking about hardware and software supply chains.
