# Summary

The episode opens with a viral claim that US Minuteman launch codes were all zeroes for two decades; Steve explains this was about the Permissive Action Link interlock ordered after 1962 and that commanders set it to zeroes because they feared being locked out, while Leo notes launch crews describe separate codes and two-key procedures. Next he puts the previous "badBIOS" hype in perspective after Fraunhofer researchers showed a covert acoustic mesh network between laptops (about 20 bits per second over roughly 65 feet) that only lets already-infected machines talk, and covers a 772-pound vehicle-stopping microwave device, Amazon's drone delivery publicity and NASA's warp-bubble interferometer work. The main topic is BULLRUN, the NSA's code name for its decryption effort. Following Matthew Green's blog post, Steve lists plausible attack classes in order of realism: stealing or compelling server keys or abusing the many trusted certificate authorities; subverted hardware encryption chips leaking session-key bits through nonces; side-channel attacks, especially in shared cloud hardware; weak random number generators including a NIST-standardised generator that RSA's BSAFE used by default; and fragile points in perfect-forward-secrecy deployments such as session tickets and bad Diffie-Hellman parameters. The "tinfoil" list covers cracking 1024-bit RSA (estimated at around a million dollars of hardware per key per year), RC4 weaknesses, undiscovered side channels, quantum computers and a covert NSA-run certificate authority. Steve concludes that leaked documents call the NSA's capabilities "extremely fragile", that industry-wide hardening is hurting them, and that trust-no-one designs are the answer.

# Key points

- Nuclear PAL story: Permissive Action Links were mandated by a 1962 memorandum to prevent unauthorised detonation; the all-zeroes claim dates from 2004 reporting and reflects the tension between preventing false launches and ensuring a real order works.
- Acoustic mesh networking is feasible at very low bitrates but cannot infect a clean machine; Steve says it only offers persistent covert communication among machines already compromised.
- E2V's mobile high-power microwave device can stall modern cars at 50 metres by coupling into wiring harnesses.
- BULLRUN, from leaked briefing slides, prompted headlines that the NSA broke Internet encryption; Green asks how this could be achieved at mass-surveillance scale rather than by targeted malware.
- Key theft is the simplest route: gag-ordered subpoenas, cooperation of providers, and the huge number of CAs trusted by browsers, each able to mint certificates.
- Hardware SSL accelerators are an attractive target; a chip could hide session-key material in "random" nonces and shrink a 128-bit key to a feasible brute-force size, and once discovered every deployed unit is compromised.
- Side channels (cache timing, resource usage) matter most when TLS servers share virtualised hardware; Steve notes Bernstein's elliptic-curve design avoids secret-dependent memory access and branching, the reason he chose it for SQRL.
- Weak randomness is fatal for client premaster secrets, Diffie-Hellman exponents and long-term key generation; duplicated keys found by the EFF SSL Observatory and a suspect NIST random generator default in RSA's BSAFE illustrate the risk.
- Forward-secrecy caveats: session resumption tickets are hard to manage across server farms, and unsafe elliptic-curve or DH parameters undermine everything.
- 1024-bit RSA: Shamir and Tromer's 2003 estimate of 10 million dollars per machine fell to about one million in 2013; nobody has publicly factored a 1024-bit key, and 2048 bits is now standard.
- RC4 carried about half of HTTPS traffic; known attacks need millions of related or identical ciphertexts, so it is weakening rather than broken.
- Because the NSA's own papers call current abilities fragile, reactions such as encrypting inter-data-centre links and adopting forward secrecy have cost them access; they must now rely on legal compulsion and certificate authorities.
