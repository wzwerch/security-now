# Summary

First episode of year 13. Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte discuss the politics around the Marcus Hutchins case: six charges and up to 40 years, reports that British authorities knew of the FBI investigation, and public arguments over whether code in the Kronos banking malware matches a hooking technique Hutchins had blogged about (Steve says it is standard practice) or whether the WannaCry kill switch story was staged. A hacker published the decryption key for Apple's Secure Enclave Processor firmware, opening that code to scrutiny. Other items: TOS;DR for rating terms of service, phished Chrome extension developers whose extensions were hijacked, a design weakness of the car CAN bus, ElcomSoft's corrected benchmark showing 1Password as hardest to brute force, two Foxit PDF zero-days, Cloudflare's lava-lamp entropy wall, the iOS 11 five-press Touch ID lockout, a clean Libsodium audit, and a candid security disclaimer from Turtl.

# Key points

- Hutchins: faces six counts; GCHQ reportedly knew of the FBI probe before his US trip; Kronos code similarity claims rest on a standard inline-hook and "trampoline" technique and a common compare-exchange instruction, which Steve sees as weak evidence; commentator Dave Aitel speculated the kill switch story was staged, which Steve treats as unproven. Leo recalls the Morris worm precedent.
- Apple Secure Enclave: the firmware decryption key was published (via GitHub), allowing analysis of SEPOS, a surprisingly complex OS; Steve notes algorithms being known is not fatal but bugs may now be found; Apple's bug bounty lags gray-market prices. Keys must briefly be present on-device for firmware to run, so they can be extracted by a clever researcher.
- TOSDR.org grades services' terms (A to E) and offers a browser extension for Chrome, Firefox, Safari and Opera.
- Proofpoint: attackers phished extension authors' Google accounts and pushed malicious updates to extensions including Copyfish, Web Developer, Chrometana, Infinity New Tab, Web Paint, Social Fixer and the TouchVPN and Betternet VPN extensions; extensions carry more trust than web scripts.
- CAN bus (Bosch, 1983): a new attack deliberately causes error frames so a targeted component is forced into the Bus Off state, disabling systems like airbags or ABS; the protocol cannot be fixed easily because it assumed cooperating nodes.
- ElcomSoft corrected its benchmark: 1Password at default settings is now the slowest to brute force (about 6,200 or 3,000 guesses per second depending on mode), slower even than Office 2016; the earlier chart used old data. Offline cracking only matters against weak master passwords.
- Foxit PDF reader zero-days were mitigated by sandbox defaults; users should update when patched.
- Cloudflare photographs a wall of lava lamps a thousand times per second and hashes the images as an entropy source.
- iOS 11 beta: pressing the sleep/wake button five times disables Touch ID until the passcode is entered; rumors of face recognition on the next iPhone.
- Libsodium audit by Matthew Green (for Private Internet Access) found no critical flaws; Libsodium underpins SQRL.
- A mistake in AWS bucket permissions exposed data; configuration errors are the user's responsibility.
- Turtl's "when is Turtl not secure" page lists coercion, malware, bad passwords and its own mistakes as limits.
- Errata: the road-sign classifier's 91 percent accuracy was on a separate test set; S3 has four-nines availability and eleven-nines durability.
- HTTP 418 "I'm a Teapot" comes from April Fools RFC 2324 (HTCPCP).
- SpinRite listener story: a degraded RAID-Z1 pool returned to a usable state after a Level 2 scan on a failing drive.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: propped-open door despite "RFID-implanted" staff security.
- HP calculator banter, bitcoin mining economics (Steve's 50 early coins) and top-level domain prices.
- Rho Agenda 99-cent promotion; Game of Thrones and Breaking Bad chat.
