# Summary

The Shadow Brokers dump, claimed to come from the NSA-linked Equation Group, is the lead story: a sample of scripts and tools for Cisco, Juniper, Fortinet and TopSec gear, dated no later than 2013, offered in a bitcoin auction with a broken-English announcement Steve finds suspicious; a Cisco ASA tool relied on SNMP and Cisco quickly issued a short-term block. Exploit broker Exodus Intelligence offers up to half a million dollars, undercutting Apple's new $200,000 bounty. A flaw in the random number generator of GnuPG and Libgcrypt, present since the 1990s and fixed in GnuPG 2.1.15, lets an attacker who sees a few thousand output bits predict the next 160, but researchers advise against hurriedly revoking keys. The EFF criticises Windows 10 on both forced upgrades and mandatory telemetry, Chrome's warnings about SHA-1 certificates are called overkill, and a Johns Hopkins team led by Matthew Green shows weaknesses in Apple's closed iMessage protocol (centralised key directory, no forward secrecy, signature rather than authenticated encryption), which Steve uses to argue protocols should be public. In the main topics, Steve discovers that the Qualcomm switch chip inside many cheap consumer routers has advanced VLAN and ACL features that firmware does not expose, and he explains operating-system history and the idea of a microkernel. He ends with a puzzle on why cryptography uses prime numbers.

# Key points

- Shadow Brokers: 256 MB sample of batch and Python tooling for routers/firewalls (Cisco, Juniper, FortiGate, TopSec), asking an unrealistic price; attribution unclear and the files could have come from a lost field kit rather than a hack; a tool targeting Cisco ASA via SNMP stopped working on newer versions only because of a version check, and removing it crashed current firmware, so Cisco had to react.
- Advice: do not expose SNMP to untrusted networks.
- Exodus Intelligence sells exploit subscriptions (about $200,000 per year) and bids up to $500,000 for iOS, Chrome, Edge and Flash bugs, showing the market competition for Apple's bounty.
- GnuPG/Libgcrypt RNG flaw: mixing weakness lets output be predicted after 4,640 bits; fixed in 2.1.15; RSA keys not believed weakened; Steve describes how his own SQRL entropy harvester pools system noise.
- EFF on Windows 10: forced upgrade tactics and unavoidable telemetry for non-enterprise users, justified by Microsoft as needed for Windows Update.
- Chrome marks sites with SHA-1 certificates as weak; no practical break exists, and no CA issues them after the 2016 cutoff, so they will expire naturally.
- iMessage paper "Dancing on the Lip of the Volcano": chosen-ciphertext attack on compressed payloads allows retrospective decryption; Apple's single key directory is a man-in-the-middle point, new-device notification is fragile, no forward secrecy, ad hoc crypto using signatures instead of authenticated encryption; fixed in iOS 9.3 and OS X 10.11.4. Apple retains about 30 days of messages for offline devices. Steve favours Signal and Threema for explicit key handling.
- Open protocols matter more than open source; SQRL's protocol is public.
- Router hardware: cheap consumer routers often contain a Qualcomm switch chip with per-port subnets and hardware ACLs not exposed in firmware; DD-WRT command-line access can reach some of it; Ubiquiti EdgeRouter X is Steve's recommendation over MikroTik.
- Operating system and microkernel history: from batch jobs and timesharing to supervisors; a kernel's irreducible duties are loading, scheduling and memory management; micro-kernels tend to bloat, and Windows' GDI move into the kernel and image-parsing bugs are examples; Linux is monolithic, macOS descends from Mach.
- Puzzle for next week: why must cryptographic keys use prime numbers.
