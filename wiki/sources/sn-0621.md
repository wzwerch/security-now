# Summary

Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte open with a company that implanted RFID chips in employees and claimed the chips were hack-proof, then cover the arrest of an 18-year-old in Budapest who reported that a new transit ticketing site trusted a user-supplied price. Other stories include Verizon throttling video in violation of Title II, a House hearing on Net Neutrality, a McAfee page telling visitors to disable Firefox tracking protection, iRobot's plan to sell home maps, the announced end of Flash in 2020, Firefox 55 Quantum performance gains, and FCC spectrum for vehicle radar. Errata debunk the story of an Echo calling 911. The main segment, "Crypto Tension", explains an IETF controversy: enterprises want passive decryption of TLS 1.3 traffic, which static Diffie-Hellman would allow only by giving up perfect forward secrecy; Steve sides with the view that they should keep using TLS 1.2 internally.

# Key points

- Three Square Market (Wisconsin) offered employees implanted RFID chips; the CEO's claims of encryption and unhackability were wrong, since such tags return a fixed, cloneable ID.
- Budapest transport authority BKK had a teen arrested after he showed that editing the price field in the page's form issued a cheap ticket without server-side validation; further flaws surfaced (plaintext password storage and emailing, predictable URLs exposing other users' ID data, adminadmin admin password, no HTTP to HTTPS redirect, copyable tickets). Steve argues laws must protect good-faith researchers.
- Verizon throttled Netflix/YouTube to about 10 Mb on LTE, discovered by users and bypassed by VPNs; Verizon called it a test. A congressional hearing on Net Neutrality was set for September 7 with top tech and ISP executives invited; Steve and Leo prefer clear legislation over regulation ping-pong.
- A McAfee page showed a banner telling visitors to turn off Firefox tracking protection through about:config to load social widgets; Steve and Leo find it irresponsible.
- iRobot's CEO said Roomba home maps may be shared with smart home companies; Leo thinks it is benign if opt-in, Steve worries about incentives and warrants; iRobot later says it would seek approval.
- Adobe will end Flash in 2020; Chrome usage of Flash sites fell from over 80 percent to 17 percent in three years; Microsoft published a phased plan to remove Flash from Edge and IE by end of 2020.
- Firefox 55 with the Quantum Flow work opened a 1,691-tab test in about 15 seconds using under half a gigabyte, compared with four minutes and 2 GB for version 54. Firefox share is about 12 percent versus Chrome near 60 percent.
- FCC allocated the 76-81 GHz band for vehicle radar, enabling finer resolution.
- Errata: Wired found that no Echo called 911 in the New Mexico case (voice assistants cannot dial the phone network); Steve clarified that his talk of ISPs minting certificates was a technical example only; Apple's fix for the Broadcom WiFi bug arrived in iOS 10.3.3.
- Site password limits (length, symbols) are a concern but not proof; what matters is hashing, salting and slow hashing on the back end.
- A browser extension exists to defeat paste-blocking on password fields.
- SpinRite recovered all but 13 of 60,000 family photos from a failed external drive; Steve outlines free 6.x updates and a later rewrite.
- TLS 1.3 controversy: the working group removed non-forward-secret key exchange; enterprise operators want to decrypt captured internal traffic for debugging; a draft by Matthew Green describes a static Diffie-Hellman option that deliberately weakens forward secrecy; Stephen Checkoway's summary lists enterprise, forward-secret and pragmatic viewpoints. Steve notes that static keys would be visible to browsers, which could refuse to advertise TLS 1.3, and that TLS 1.2 already supports internal monitoring.
