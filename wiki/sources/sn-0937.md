# Summary

The episode starts with a developer-humor "what we say versus what we mean" table as Picture of the Week. Steve then explains that the WinRAR 6.23 fix he flagged the previous week closed CVE-2023-38831, which Group-IB found being exploited since April 2023 through booby-trapped archives posted on cryptocurrency and stock trading forums. Listener feedback covers whether local-network devices should be exempt from Chrome's HTTPS push, self-signed certificates, using your own domain with a hosted email provider, whether Topics favors large advertisers, how uBlock Origin and Acceptable Ads fit in, how wide Voyager 2's antenna beam is, and whether desktop users should worry about Windows Secure Time Seeding. Steve then closes the TLS thread by explaining that the handshake's Unix-time field was only ever a convenience for building a unique nonce and a 2013 draft proposed deprecating it. A SpinRite tester's old USB stick, and a cheap 2 TB drive Steve bought, expose the epidemic of fake-capacity storage, which he says SpinRite 6.1 catches and SpinRite 7 will test fully. The main topic answers listeners who say plain HTTP is risky: man-in-the-middle attacks need a privileged network position, but Steve concludes HTTP-only sites should remain a choice, noting certificate authorities issue plenty of certificates for phishing sites.

# Key points

- CVE-2023-38831 in WinRAR (fixed in 6.23 on 2 August 2023) let a crafted RAR or ZIP archive execute a hidden script when a user opened an apparently harmless file; Group-IB saw it distributing DarkMe, GuLoader and Remcos RAT via at least eight trading forums, with at least 130 confirmed victims. Steve urges updating, skepticism toward strangers' files, and uploading a suspicious file's URL to VirusTotal before downloading.
- WinRAR has no auto-update, whereas Notepad++ updates very often; Leo prefers package managers such as winget for keeping software current.
- Private address space (RFC 1918: 192.168/16, 172.16/12, 10/8) is not uniformly trustworthy, so browsers cannot simply exempt it; a self-signed certificate that a user explicitly trusts gives encryption plus limited authentication for local appliances such as routers and NAS devices. Steve's pfSense self-signed certificate runs about five years, unlike CA certificates now limited to 13 months.
- Bringing your own domain to a hosted email provider (Fastmail, Zoho and others) keeps control of your addresses and lets you switch providers, the third option beyond running your own server or using the provider's domain.
- Topics filtering by prior observation favors advertisers with wide reach, but Steve sees it as consolidation of larger advertisers rather than clear monopoly; uBlock Origin with Acceptable Ads still shows tame ads.
- Voyager 2's 3.7 m dish at S-band has a beam of roughly 1.5 degrees, so 2 degrees off-axis was only attenuated, not a hopeless miss.
- Windows Secure Time Seeding (UtilizeSslTimeData) applies to Windows 10 and later and Server 2016 and later, separate from "Set time automatically", which should stay on. Desktop CMOS clock errors are a separate problem from dead RTC batteries and ripple-counter read glitches.
- TLS Random fields hold a 32-bit time plus 32 random bits only to guarantee nonce uniqueness; the spec says clocks need not be correct and a 2013 IETF draft ("Deprecating gmt_unix_time in TLS") called the practice neither necessary nor safe, so Microsoft's assumption that the field carries real time is wrong.
- A tester's 256 MB flash stick silently stopped storing data at its halfway point, and SpinRite's confidence test failed it; Steve's cheap $26.88 "2 TB" drive failed the same test, and fake SSD enclosures filled with ballast are in circulation. Only a full pseudorandom fill-and-verify proves real capacity, which SpinRite 7 will add.
- Man-in-the-middle attacks are harder than eavesdropping (Firesheep showed passive session hijacking on open Wi-Fi); active interception needs a privileged position such as a corporate TLS-inspecting middlebox with an installed root certificate, or a hostile ISP that injects content into HTTP pages.
- Chrome's automatic HTTPS upgrade also thwarts downgrade attacks that rewrite https:// links to http://, but certificates for phishing sites (14,766 Let's Encrypt certificates for PayPal lookalikes in one report) show the padlock is not a guarantee.
- Leo objects to Google ranking HTTPS sites higher as using market power; Steve agrees on the ethics.
