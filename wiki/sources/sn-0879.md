# Summary

Steve opens with a mechanical "OR gate" picture of the week, then covers OpenSSL 3.0.4, which shipped a heap-corruption bug in RSA private-key operations on x86-64 machines with AVX-512 IFMA; maintainers hesitated to call it a vulnerability, but others pointed out it was reachable in a TLS handshake, and 3.0.5 fixed it (CVE-2022-2274). NIST announced the first four of eight post-quantum algorithms after a six-year open competition: CRYSTALS-Kyber for key establishment and CRYSTALS-Dilithium, FALCON and SPHINCS+ for signatures, with SPHINCS+ valued as a hash-based backup to the lattice schemes. Yubico donated 30,000 FIDO keys to Ukraine, coordinated by the Ukrainian firm Hideez. Apple previewed Lockdown Mode, an optional hardening mode against mercenary spyware that Steve considers sensible. Microsoft briefly re-enabled Office VBA macros from the internet and then reversed itself again after an uproar. Motherboard published code of the FBI-run ANOM messaging app, which silently copied messages to a hidden "ghost" contact. After listener feedback on VirusTotal, IoT network separation and VPN use, the main story is Rolling-Pwn: Honda's keyless entry accepts replayed old codes because its rolling counter can be resynchronized backwards.

# Key points

- OpenSSL 3.0.4 (released June 21, 2022) bug in RSA private-key operations on AVX-512-IFMA capable x86-64 CPUs, CVE-2022-2274; 1.1.1, BoringSSL and LibreSSL unaffected; upgrade to 3.0.5. Debate over whether a memory-corruption bug that is "just a serious bug" counts as a vulnerability.
- NIST post-quantum selection: Kyber (small keys, fast) for encryption; Dilithium primary signature, FALCON for smaller signatures, SPHINCS+ (hash-based, larger and slower) as a different-math fallback. Four more algorithms still under consideration; final standard expected in about two years; NIST advises experimenting but not hard-coding yet. Steve says the open process makes a repeat of the Dual_EC-style episode unlikely.
- "Harvest now, decrypt later" is the reason to move early to post-quantum cryptography; the new algorithms are not slower, only larger.
- Yubico gave 30,000 FIDO YubiKeys to Ukraine; about 16,000 deployed to government and critical-sector staff via Hideez; one department of roughly 500 moved to FIDO in under a week. Original FIDO (U2F) needs server-side support, while FIDO2 uses WebAuthn.
- Apple Lockdown Mode (iOS 16, iPadOS 16, macOS Ventura): blocks most message attachment types and link previews, disables JIT JavaScript compilation, blocks unsolicited FaceTime and service invitations, blocks wired data connections while locked, and bars configuration profiles and MDM enrollment. Apple also funds a $10M grant for mercenary-spyware research. Steve and Leo plan to try it.
- Office macros: Microsoft's February 2022 default block of internet-sourced VBA macros was rolled back "temporarily" in July after business feedback, then restored the day before the show after backlash. Steve and Leo explain that businesses balk at retraining; Group Policy can still block them.
- History of macro malware from the 1995 Concept virus through 1999 Melissa and template-infecting macros, as retold by Sophos.
- ANOM / Operation Trojan Shield: the FBI-run encrypted phone company used an open-source XMPP stack plus a hidden contact named "bot" that received copies of all messages with location data; more than 1,000 arrests and over 300 syndicates affected. Steve notes this matches his long-held view that provider-managed keys allow an invisible extra "end", as GCHQ officials had also suggested in 2018.
- Listener Q&A: VirusTotal for checking suspicious executables; keeping a Chromecast on the main network while isolating dubious IoT gear on a guest network; Steve's router is an Asus RT-AC68U, with a caution from Michael Horowitz that guest SSIDs may share a subnet; Horowitz's Defensive Computing checklist (grc.sc/879).
- VPNs: Steve does not use one at home but would when traveling on "other people's networks" (OPNs); TLS has made this less critical, but rogue certificates and hotel Wi-Fi remain concerns.
- Rolling-Pwn (CVE-2022-27254 and related CVE-2021-46145 references; researchers Kevin2600 and Wesley Li of Star-V Lab): Honda's counter resynchronizes on consecutive lock/unlock commands, so previously captured codes work again. Passive capture with off-the-shelf software-defined radio lets an attacker unlock and start (but not drive) vehicles from 2012 to 2022. Contrast with a Tesla-style forward-only counter, which needed an active jam-and-capture attack.
- Honda: no vulnerability reporting channel, no reply to the researchers, and no plan to update older models. Steve notes the key rolling-code patents expired by 2016 and calls continued use of the flawed scheme a lack of care; the strongest fix is a bidirectional challenge-response handshake, which is costlier.

# Other segments
