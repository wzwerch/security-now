# Summary

A news-heavy episode leads into Steve's main topic, a research paper on iOS apps that call private APIs. In the news, a transient Chrome Web Store rejection of uBlock Origin was resolved as a review glitch, and Google announced that Symantec must log all certificates it issues to Certificate Transparency after Symantec's own count of improperly issued test certificates grew from a few to thousands. Hacking Team, hit by a large leak months earlier, wrote to customers saying it is building new capabilities, and Steve argues that legal malware implants beat weakened encryption. Tor Messenger entered beta, the US State Department declined to classify cyber products as munitions while the UK pushed an Investigatory Powers Bill, a browser-history probe called Sniffly abused HSTS cache timing, and ECMAScript 6 was ratified. Threema passed an independent audit. In the main segment Steve explains that iOS was designed assuming trusted first-party apps, that Objective-C calls methods by name strings through a dictionary, and that Apple therefore can only scan for forbidden names, which developers evade by building names at runtime; he considers the problem unfixable without moving to a statically bound language such as Swift.

# Key points

- uBlock Origin on the Chrome Web Store: Google emailed the developer about hidden code and later said the rejection was a review-system snag; the extension was never removed. Steve and Leo see stress from automated vetting at scale rather than malice.
- Symantec: it first reported a few mistakenly issued test certificates (including for Google domains), fired employees, then admitted dozens more and thousands for unregistered domains. Google's Ryan Sleevi announced that from June 1, 2016 Symantec-issued certificates must support Certificate Transparency or may trigger browser warnings. Steve calls it a conduct-based penalty and highlights the weakness of trusting every CA never to err.
- Hacking Team: after the Phineas Fisher leak (400 GB, including exploits and customer lists naming US agencies), its CEO told customers that law enforcement will "go dark" without such tools. Steve sees government malware under court warrant as preferable to backdoored encryption.
- Tor Messenger beta combines Tor with the OTR protocol; Leo recounts a false Facebook alarm triggered by his own Tor login appearing to come from Egypt.
- Policy split: the EFF reported the State Department advisory group recommended against listing cyber products as munitions; the UK's proposed Investigatory Powers Bill would bar unbreakable encryption and require a year of browsing-history retention, which Steve says VPNs would defeat. Steve notes the historic 40-bit export limit as the origin of weak crypto still found in software.
- Sniffly: a page uses Content Security Policy to block HTTPS image loads and times the failure to learn whether the browser has HSTS entries for sites, inferring visited sites; limited to HSTS-supporting browsers and defeated by HTTPS Everywhere. Steve is not alarmed.
- ECMAScript 6 additions: block-scoped let, constants, default parameters, modules, classes; transpilers backfill support, and Edge showed the best compatibility.
- Threema audit by an independent Swiss lab confirmed end-to-end encryption and minimal data retention; Steve likes its user-verified key model.
- Main topic: iOS originally assumed trusted apps; when third-party apps arrived, Apple hid dangerous functions by removing them from headers but they had to remain callable by the trusted wrapper libraries in the same process. Objective-C dispatches by looking up a method-name string, so Apple's string scanning fails if names are obfuscated; dynamic analysis (a 2,000-app study found a non-trivial number using private APIs) cannot be complete because apps can fetch names or tokens from servers. Swift or C-style static binding would allow real vetting.
- SpinRite: after repairing a drive, check the SMART screen for red bars during the run to decide whether to replace the disk.
