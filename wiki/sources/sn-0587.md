# Summary

Steve follows up on the LessPass author, who responded graciously and promised full-alphabet passwords and clearer code, then turns to the week's two big stories. Samy Kamkar's PoisonTap turns a five-dollar Raspberry Pi Zero into a USB network adapter that a locked computer trusts, letting it hijack traffic, harvest cookies and plant persistent web backdoors. Kryptowire found that cheap Android phones sold through major US retailers carried Adups over-the-air update firmware that sent text messages, contacts, call logs and device identifiers to a server in Shanghai and could run privileged commands; a separate firm found Ragentek-based update software with an unencrypted channel and unregistered fallback domains that it sinkholed, exposing millions of devices. Further items: an iPhone lock-screen bypass using Siri and VoiceOver, a media-file link that hangs iPhones, a webcam infected within 98 seconds of going online, Bruce Schneier's testimony to Congress on the IoT, the UK's sweeping Investigatory Powers legislation passing, and the Apocalypse ransomware crew asking researcher Fabian Wosar to help fix their broken encryption. The episode ends with the real reason behind the BlackNurse name, the SQRL slide deck, and NASA's published EM Drive thrust measurements.

# Key points

- Skype quality: Steve found calls were being relayed through Microsoft servers rather than connecting peer to peer, which explains choppy audio; relaying also lets Microsoft monitor calls.
- LessPass follow-up: the author acknowledged the entropy mistakes (templates borrowed from another scheme), committed to using the full character set and longer defaults, and to migrating users carefully. Steve's lesson: well-meant amateur crypto can attract thousands of users before anyone reviews it.
- PoisonTap: builds on a September credential-snatching USB-Ethernet trick; a device claiming to be a network adapter gets trusted even on a locked machine, and rogue DHCP lets it route traffic, siphon cookies for the top million sites via hidden frames, expose the internal router and seed cached backdoors. Defences: HTTPS everywhere with HSTS, secure-flag cookies, disabling or blocking ports, encrypted deep sleep; screen locking does not help.
- Adups firmware: collected message bodies, contacts, call history, IMSI/IMEI and app usage, matched messages against keywords, could install apps and reprogram devices remotely, and evaded antivirus because it shipped with the OS. Affected models included a $50 Amazon-subsidised phone, which Amazon pulled. Kryptowire compared it with the earlier Carrier IQ controversy.
- Ragentek update software: commands could be injected by a man in the middle, and because two fallback domains were never registered, researchers could register them and observe about 2.8 million devices across roughly 55 models; they now control the domains to prevent abuse. Advice: pay more for security-conscious devices.
- iPhone lock-screen bypass: chaining Siri, VoiceOver and the message-reply screen exposes contacts and the photo library; interim advice is to disable Siri on the lock screen. A separate crafted video link freezes iPhones until a hard reset and could hint at a parsing flaw.
- Rob Graham put a cheap WiFi camera online behind rate limiting and watched a Mirai-style worm try default credentials within 98 seconds.
- Schneier's four points to the House Energy and Commerce Committee: attack is easier than defence, vulnerabilities live in interconnections, the Internet scales attacks, and economics do not trickle down to cheap, unpatchable, long-lived devices; he argued for government involvement while Leo doubts a new regulator is the answer.
- UK Investigatory Powers legislation ("snoopers' charter") passed: ISPs must keep top-level web histories for up to a year, companies can be compelled to decrypt, and agencies get legal hacking powers.
- The Apocalypse ransomware crew asked Emsisoft's Fabian Wosar to find a bug that makes their Windows CryptoAPI use fail and overwrite files with junk; Steve and Leo see it as an ethical trap.
- Windows 10 Insider builds nag users who try to change the default browser away from Edge.
- BlackNurse naming: the Danish SOC team said the name was a joke referring to two colleagues (a former blacksmith and a nurse).
- The SQRL presentation slides (about 40 slides) were published on GRC.
- NASA Eagleworks paper on the EM Drive reported small thrust in vacuum (about 1.2 mN/kW), which Leo treats skeptically.
