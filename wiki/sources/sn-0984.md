# Summary

The episode centres on the 19 July 2024 CrowdStrike outage, in which a faulty Falcon content update (Channel File 291) crashed around 8.5 million Windows machines into boot loops, grounding flights and disrupting hospitals, banks, broadcasters and retailers. Before it, Steve covers news: the FBI used Cellebrite's newest unreleased software to unlock the Samsung phone of Donald Trump's would-be assassin, while leaked Cellebrite matrices show it could not unlock iPhones on iOS 17.4 or later; Cisco disclosed CVE-2024-20419, a CVSS 10.0 password-change flaw in Smart Software Manager On-Prem; and Entrust announced it will resell certificates issued by SSL.com after 31 October. Google's Privacy Sandbox lead announced that Chrome will keep third-party cookies and instead offer a user choice, which Steve sees as a victory for the tracking industry. Listener mail includes first-hand outage accounts and lessons from Steve's first weeks of GRC email delivery. Steve then explains the technical mechanics: Falcon is a boot-start kernel driver that hooks Windows APIs, and a bad pointer (a tiny offset, 0x9C, read from unmapped memory) in kernel context causes an unrecoverable crash. Because CrowdStrike would not yet explain how the file escaped testing, he lays out hypotheses and expects reverse engineers to supply details.

# Key points

- Cellebrite: the Pittsburgh FBI office's licence could not open the new Samsung phone, but Cellebrite's federal team supplied unreleased software and the phone opened in about 40 minutes; leaked April 2024 support matrices show most Android phones are crackable (except some powered-off Pixels) while iPhones on iOS 17.4+ were listed as "in research"; The Verge notes such vendors overstate capabilities.
- Cisco CVE-2024-20419 (CVSS 10.0): improper password-change process in Cisco Smart Software Manager On-Prem lets an unauthenticated remote attacker change any user's password, including admin; no workaround, patch required.
- Entrust: after Chrome's distrust of certificates issued after 31 October, Entrust will keep selling and managing TLS certificates, issued by partner CA SSL.com (itself about 0.05% of sites versus Entrust about 0.1%); Entrust urges customers to renew before 31 October (valid up to 398 days) and says it intends to return to the Chrome root store.
- Google's Anthony Chavez announced a "new path" for Privacy Sandbox: third-party cookies will not be deprecated, Chrome will offer an informed-choice prompt, Sandbox APIs stay available and IP Protection will come to Incognito. Steve's GRC cookie-forensics page shows third-party cookies persisting for 17.8% of visitors overall and under 2% for Safari.
- GRC email lessons: a JPEG thumbnail triggered antivirus false positives, "polyfill.io" in the text hit a URL blocklist, a zero-size hidden div was flagged, and Hover's mail hosting rejected the sender for low IP reputation; about 7,000 subscribers received this week's mail.
- Name trivia: Snowflake is named after the "snowflake schema" in data warehousing. A CDK developer reports CDK reset 15-year-old interface credentials during restoration. A listener notes SRI pinning means manual updates when library bugs are fixed. A listener points out IP-blocking tools can be abused for denial of service via spoofed UDP, though not TCP services like SSH.
- Outage facts: the update went out 19 July 04:09 UTC and was withdrawn by 05:27 UTC; sensors version 7.11 and later that were online in the window were exposed; Linux and macOS were unaffected. Recovery needed manual Safe Mode file deletion per machine (BitLocker recovery keys often required) or a Microsoft USB recovery tool; some machines recovered after repeated reboots pulled the fix. CrowdStrike (about 29,000 customers) apologised and said it was not a cyberattack; attackers began registering CrowdStrike-themed domains.
- Technical explanation: the Falcon driver is a Microsoft-signed boot-start driver that hooks Windows APIs; Channel File 291 updated detection of malicious named-pipe use by command-and-control frameworks. The crash dump shows a function receiving a near-null pointer (value 0x9C) and loading 32 bits from non-existent memory; in kernel mode, Windows cannot recover as it can from an application fault. Steve discounts the "file full of nulls" rumour, speculates that no pre-release testing path existed for such content updates or that a distribution glitch might have occurred, and questions whether channel files are signed. Leo notes Apple has largely removed third-party kernel extensions, whereas a 2009 EU agreement constrains Microsoft; commentators also expect Congressional scrutiny.
- Listener reports: a hospital restoring hundreds of servers, a corporate team restoring 500 systems by 10 am, an Australian broadcaster unable to run its studio, and a rumour about Southwest Airlines using Windows 3.1 that Leo says was debunked (Southwest simply did not use CrowdStrike).
