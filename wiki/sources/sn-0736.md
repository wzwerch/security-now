# Summary

Steve starts with a reminder that hardware supply chain attacks are feasible: a researcher plans to show a roughly $2 microcontroller soldered into a Cisco ASA 5505 firewall that triggers the password recovery feature at boot to create a backdoor admin account. He then covers the October 4 open letter from US, UK and Australian officials urging Facebook not to adopt end-to-end encryption without lawful-access provisions, Facebook's refusal to build backdoors, and Snowden's op-ed; Steve and Leo agree the issue is power and who holds keys. Lighter items include the 39-year-old Unix password of Ken Thompson finally cracked, a stalker locating a pop star from reflections in her eyes, and a Pew survey on weak public security knowledge. Good news includes native OpenPGP in Thunderbird 78, Windows Defender Tamper Protection enabled by default, and Google Cloud architects mentioning SQRL. The main topic is checkm8, a use-after-free in the boot ROM USB code of Apple A5 through A11 devices that cannot be patched, which is mainly a problem for Apple's ability to keep its platform opaque.

# Key points

- Hardware implant proof of concept (Monta Elkins, to be shown at CS3STHLM): an ATtiny85 chip from a cheap Arduino board added to a Cisco ASA 5505 using about $150 of tools; it abuses the serial-console password recovery path at boot. Steve notes targeted interception of shipments is realistic for nation-states and that Bloomberg's earlier Supermicro claim was never confirmed; mitigations include buying through a third party or building appliances from generic hardware and open-source software.
- Open letter to Zuckerberg (US, UK, Australia) demands lawful access in readable form and delaying E2EE; Facebook cites the CLOUD Act and opposes backdoors; Leo cites Snowden's argument that weakened encryption exposes users to many adversaries.
- Steve notes that if someone else manages keys, you are not managing your keys; Signal uses phone numbers, WhatsApp may not warn on key changes, Threema is closed source.
- Historical BSD 3 password file: descrypt hashes of Unix pioneers were cracked over five years with Hashcat; Ken Thompson's was a chess-notation move, cracked in four days on a consumer GPU at about 930 million guesses per second. The other recovered passwords were weak.
- A Japanese stalker used reflections in a celebrity's eyes in selfies, plus map data, to find her home; Bellingcat's Eliot Higgins notes high-resolution photos increase geolocation potential. Steve advises printing redacted PDFs to a new PDF.
- Pew survey: most US adults answered fewer than half the digital knowledge questions; only 28% identified two-factor authentication; Steve argues users should not need this knowledge and systems should become reliable black boxes.
- Thunderbird will integrate OpenPGP natively starting with version 78 (summer 2020), replacing Enigmail's external dependencies.
- Windows 10 1903 Tamper Protection is now on by default (Steve found it off on his machine); prevents programmatic changes to Defender settings.
- Google's "Modern Password Security" documents describe SQRL as an emerging alternative to passwords.
- checkm8 (by axi0mX): a boot ROM use-after-free in USB handling, fixed silently in the A12 and later and in iOS 12 betas, affects A5-A11 devices (iPhone 4S through iPhone X, many iPads, Apple Watch, Apple TV); not remote, requires physical access and tethered execution, does not break the Secure Enclave or PIN, and does not persist after reboot. Implications: researchers can dump and diff Apple's firmware for every update, malware authors could do the same, and a device taken out of the owner's control (border crossing, hotel) could be implanted then rebooted, so a full restart is advised.
