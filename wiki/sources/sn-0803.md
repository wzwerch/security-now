# Summary

Chrome 88 and Edge 88 improved their built-in password managers (weak-password check, central editing, Edge generator); Steve still prefers a third-party manager for portability and notes that anything that auto-fills must hold decryptable secrets. Adobe Flash's built-in expiry on January 12, 2021 halted a Chinese regional railroad until a pirated build without the time bomb was used, and the South African Revenue Service shipped its own browser to keep Flash-based tax forms working. Ransomware gangs (SunCrypt, Ragnar Locker, now Avaddon) add DDoS attacks to pressure victims who can restore from backups. Microsoft's deep dive on SolarWinds second-stage activation reveals extreme per-victim customization, the use of Image File Execution Options to separate the Cobalt Strike loader from the SolarWinds process, and removal of the backdoor from builds by June 2020. Malwarebytes disclosed it was hit by the same actor via a dormant Office 365 application. Project Zero's Natalie Silvanovich found calling-state-machine logic bugs in several messaging apps after the 2019 FaceTime flaw, which Steve uses to argue that users cannot perceive security flaws, so market incentives fail and standards labs or regulation may be needed. The show closes with the Johns Hopkins (Matthew Green) comparison of iOS and Android security.

# Key points

- Chrome 88 (January 19) adds a weak-password check and bulk password editing; Edge 88 adds a password generator. Locally visible passwords remain a risk on shared machines; Steve's advice is to prefer a portable third-party manager.
- Flash end of life: a time-bomb in the last Flash builds stopped it on January 12, 2021; a Chinese railroad lost access to timetables for over 16 hours, and South Africa's tax agency (SARS) distributed a special browser for unmigrated forms.
- Ransomware pressure tactic: DDoS against the victim until they negotiate (SunCrypt, Ragnar Locker, Avaddon), after better backups reduced the need to pay.
- Microsoft's Solorigate analysis: backdoor compiled February 2020, deployed late March, removed from SolarWinds builds in June 2020; per-machine unique loaders, file names, WMI filters and C2; attackers disabled audit logging and added temporary firewall rules around noisy recon; timestomping and wiping; handover to the Cobalt Strike loader via an IFEO debugger registry value, removed after use.
- Steve's takeaway: the FireEye detection was luck; organisations need internal network monitoring and intrusion detection.
- Malwarebytes: attacker abused a dormant email-protection application with privileged access to its Office 365 and Azure tenant to read limited internal email; no source-code or build compromise found. Lesson: remove unused integrations; service-principal credentials are a target.
- Project Zero (Natalie Silvanovich): logic bugs in calling state machines let callers force audio or video from the callee in Signal Android (fixed September 2019), JioChat and Mocha (2020), Facebook Messenger (November 2020) and Google Duo (December 2020), following Group FaceTime in 2019.
- Perverse incentives: consumers and reviewers see features, not security; Steve compares to Underwriters Laboratories, Consumer Reports and seatbelt regulation as possible fixes for IoT and app security.
- Johns Hopkins study (securephones.io, about 120 pages): iOS has strong controls but leaves much data in "available after first unlock" protection class and iCloud weaknesses (GrayKey passcode guessing seen in 2018); Android has fragmented updates, no equivalent of Complete Protection, opt-in end-to-end backup, and broad data synced to Google. Practical advice: power the phone off if you may lose control of it.
- Apple patched three iOS zero-days (two WebKit, one kernel) around the recording date (mentioned by Leo).
