# Summary

The news half starts with an actively exploited zero-day in Microsoft's RTF parser, reachable through Word documents and Outlook mail, for which Microsoft issued a one-click Fixit and pointed to the Enhanced Mitigation Experience Toolkit (EMET) as a defense; Steve explains why Windows cannot simply force ASLR on everything. He then dismantles two overblown headlines: a paywalled journal paper claimed to show WPA2 was "cracked", which on reading turned out to be an ordinary dictionary attack on weak passphrases with absurd arithmetic about full keyspaces, and a Symantec post about malware (Backdoor.Ploutus) that makes XP-based ATMs dispense cash when triggered by SMS, which he says is a physical-access attack unrelated to XP support ending. Good news: Google made HTTPS mandatory for all Gmail access and encrypted internal data-center traffic. Steve is uneasy about a Snowden-derived story that the NSA broke into Huawei's headquarters, since it does not touch Fourth Amendment concerns. The iOS Security series concludes with AirDrop (hash-based contact matching and TLS with iCloud-issued identity certificates), Siri's server-side data, the problem that iMessage relies on Apple's key directory so Apple could insert itself as a man in the middle, and the use of the NSA-originated P-256 curve for iCloud Keychain syncing. Jailbreaking is postponed. Steve also reports that SQRL's translations are now loaded from external language files, with nearly 300 volunteer translators.

# Key points

- RTF zero-day: a malformed RTF file or email could execute code in Word and in Outlook's renderer, used in targeted attacks; Office 2013 has partial mitigations; Microsoft's Fixit blocks RTF handling; EMET's mandatory ASLR and anti-ROP settings block the observed exploit. Not running as administrator also limits it.
- EMET (version 4.1, version 5 preview): free mitigation tool that is hard to deploy because it breaks compatible software (a Microsoft list includes 7-Zip, Adobe products, iTunes, Dropbox, Chrome); Steve ties this to why mitigations are hard to retrofit.
- "WPA2 cracked" paper (Brunel, Macedonia and Lancaster authors): just an off-the-shelf dictionary attack on weak passphrases, with a huge, irrelevant keyspace figure; WPA2 with a strong random passphrase and its PBKDF2 slowdown remains sound.
- Symantec's ATM story: Ploutus malware lets criminals who have physical access and an external keyboard command the machine, including by SMS; the "95 percent of ATMs run XP" link to the April 8 end of support is hype.
- Gmail now forces HTTPS for every user, and all mail moving between Google data centers is encrypted; email between providers remains generally unencrypted.
- Crypto101 (a free introductory cryptography course) was recommended by listeners.
- NSA operation against Huawei's Shenzhen servers (New York Times, from Snowden documents): Steve thinks this strays from Snowden's stated Fourth Amendment rationale; Leo notes other revelations also do.
- Firefox 28 shipped, Firefox 29 beta brings a redesigned interface with customization.
- AirDrop: Bluetooth Low Energy announcement carrying a hashed identity, an ad hoc peer-to-peer WiFi link, TLS with identity certificates issued through iCloud, contacts-only default; Steve rates it very well designed.
- Siri sends contacts, music library names and location only as needed and discards session data after ten minutes.
- iMessage: per-device RSA and ECDSA keys with encrypted messages, but Apple's directory service supplies the public keys, so Apple could add an extra key and read messages; Apple's claim that it cannot read iMessages therefore overstates the case. Metadata such as timestamps is not encrypted. Threema and TextSecure are suggested for real end-to-end privacy.
- iCloud Keychain: a circle of trusted devices and a signed syncing circle, but protected using the P-256 elliptic curve, which Steve says the community distrusts because its parameters came from NSA seeds; he is bothered that Apple used it nowhere else.
- Leo notes mid-show news of Facebook's purchase of Oculus Rift for about 2 billion dollars.

# Other segments

- SQRL: 297 translation volunteers; Steve describes an assembly-language lookup routine he wrote for the string table and the language file now loaded outside the executable.
- SpinRite: a Sibelius music-notation user recovered recent work after the machine died.
- Steve discusses academic journal paywalls after paying about 30 euros for the WPA2 paper.
