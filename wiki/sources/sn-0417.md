# Summary

Marking the end of the show's eighth year, Steve covers August Patch Tuesday (23 vulnerabilities, with a cumulative Internet Explorer fix, a font flaw affecting XP and Server 2003, and Exchange issues in an Oracle component), then the shutdown of Lavabit, whose founder Ladar Levison closed the service rather than become complicit in something he could not describe, followed by Silent Circle closing its Silent Mail product. Steve stresses that neither was truly secure email, since mail arrives unencrypted and the server briefly sees the user's key at login, and sums it up as "we can no longer safely delegate our security". He recounts the FISA court opinion of 2011 that found collection unconstitutional, which the Justice Department was trying to keep secret. A critical weakness in Android's random number generator, which produced repeated values, let attackers recover Bitcoin wallet private keys generated on Android. Security experts believe attackers are saving Windows XP exploits until support ends. Steve also revisits GRC's cookie forensics page, notes IE leaks third-party cookies via favicon, embed and object requests, and reports SpinRite 6.1 test progress. In the Q&A he answers why GRC's mail server does not support STARTTLS, whether CryptoLink might be revived as free open source, and defends Mailvelope against claims about private key storage.

# Key points

- Patch Tuesday of August 2013: 23 flaws, three critical. The IE patch fixed a bug first shown at Pwn2Own in March, and Microsoft itself warned exploit code was likely soon because patches can be reverse-engineered.
- Lavabit shut down without warning after about 10 years; the owner said he could not reveal what he was asked to do. Steve's earlier blog noted it only encrypted mail at rest, and that on login the service necessarily handled the user's decryption key.
- Silent Circle preemptively closed Silent Mail, destroying its servers, but kept its end-to-end encrypted voice, text and chat products, which cannot be compromised by a provider. Bruce Schneier noted a privately owned firm can close in a way a public company with shareholders cannot.
- Steve's maxim: "We can no longer safely delegate our security because our delegates may be compelled to secretly violate our trust." Even encrypted email leaves envelope metadata exposed; Bitmessage (peer-to-peer broadcast, blockchain-inspired) avoids this.
- Mother Jones reported on the 86-page 2011 FISA court opinion finding some Section 702 collection unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment; Senator Ron Wyden had pushed for disclosure. Leo is critical of the secrecy.
- Android bug: the secure random generator could return identical values, and Bitcoin's ECDSA signatures leak the private key if a nonce is ever reused, so wallets made by apps such as Bitcoin Wallet and others on Android were exposed; the researcher had found vulnerable addresses in the blockchain and recovered funds with the owner's consent. Fix: update Android and regenerate keys; avoid keeping large Bitcoin holdings on a mobile device.
- Windows XP: 236 days remained before end of support, with about 43 percent of PCs still on it; attackers may be hoarding exploits for after the cut-off.
- GRC's cookie forensics page (shortened as checkcookie) shows that Internet Explorer 10 still lets third-party cookies through favicon, embed and object requests even when blocking is on.
- A viral battery bounce test (good alkaline batteries bounce less) is mentioned as unverified fun.
- Steve cannot be compelled to give up CryptoLink keys if it is free and open source; he will think of finishing it after SpinRite.
- GRC's mail server (hMailServer) lacks SMTP STARTTLS; GRC staff connect over SSL, and server-to-server encryption only works if the other end supports it.
- Mailvelope controversy: Hak5 criticized its private key storage, but Leo and Steve agree PGP/GPG tools always rely on a passphrase protecting the private key, and the real risk is physical access to an unlocked computer.
- Appellate courts have been leaning toward holding that compelling a password violates the Fifth Amendment, so a memorized secret is protected; Steve suggests a TrueCrypt passphrase partly memorized.

# Other segments

- Eighth anniversary reflection; the "State of Surveillance" episode became the most downloaded in the series.
- SpinRite 6.1: SpinTest 10 released, a discovered disabled-interrupt problem on a few machines solved, and older Macs can boot the test code from optical disk or USB.
- TV and film chatter: Elysium, Olympus Has Fallen, Breaking Bad, Dexter.
- Questions left over are deferred; the PGP protocol starts next week.
