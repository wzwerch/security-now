# Summary

Recorded on April Fool's Day (with a promise that every story is real), the episode begins with Malaysia's prime minister refusing a 10 million USD ransom after an attack on Kuala Lumpur International Airport forced staff to post flights on a whiteboard. Troy Hunt, creator of Have I Been Pwned, was phished by a Mailchimp lookalike (mailchimp-sso.com) while jet-lagged; the page relayed his one-time password in real time and exported his roughly 16,000-address list, which he disclosed within 43 minutes. Steve praises Cloudflare for closing port 80 entirely on its API endpoints (HTTPS only) and moving away from static IPs, then covers an academic study showing that re-implementing malware in uncommon languages or with different compilers sharply lowers static antivirus detection. Cloudflare data shows 41 percent of successful human logins on its free-plan sites use previously leaked passwords, and a listener's Microsoft sign-in log shows constant foreign guessing with no geofencing for consumer accounts. After a 23andMe update, Steve recommends the RansomLook.io victim tracker, shares a user story for his InControl tool, and ends with the EU OS proof of concept: a Fedora-based Linux with KDE Plasma and common management tooling aimed at public-sector bodies, plus a reflection on the sustainability of volunteer open-source maintenance.

# Key points

- Kuala Lumpur airport ransomware (March 2025): the Prime Minister declined the 10 million USD demand within seconds; operations fell back to manual whiteboard displays.
- Troy Hunt phish: a fake "sending privileges restricted" Mailchimp notice led to a lookalike domain; 1Password did not autofill, but he proceeded anyway. TOTP two-factor failed because the attacker relayed the code instantly; Mailchimp offers no phishing-resistant factor. The export held about 16,000 records including 7,500 unsubscribed addresses, IPs and approximate location. Cloudflare blocked the phishing site about two hours later. Passkeys are inherently phishing-resistant because the server's challenge is signed per site.
- Steve recalls Microsoft once accepting one-time codes for a long window, enabling brute-force crowdsourcing.
- Cloudflare is closing all plain HTTP ports on api.cloudflare.com (connections will simply fail rather than return 403), will drop non-SNI clients (about 0.55 percent of API connections) and let the API change IPs dynamically; an opt-in HTTP-off option for customer sites is planned for late 2025.
- Research "Coding Malware in Fancy Programming Languages for Fun and Profit": malware is mostly C/C++ built with Microsoft's compiler; rewriting in less common languages (Rust, Go, Haskell, even Lisp) or merely switching compilers breaks signature-based static detection and raises reverse-engineering effort. About 280,000 new samples appear per day; APT29, Akira, BlackByte and Hive have already changed languages. Cross-compilation for IoT and AI-assisted code translation lower the cost. Steve speculates about Forth as a more extreme option.
- Cloudflare password-reuse study (Sept to Nov 2024): 41 percent of successful human logins on its free plan used leaked passwords; 95 percent of login attempts with leaked passwords come from bots doing credential stuffing; Cloudflare fronts about 30 million properties (about 20 percent of the web).
- A listener's Microsoft account activity page showed about five failed foreign sign-ins a day; geofencing of logins is available only for business accounts. Leo reports a newly exposed SSH server being probed within two hours; Steve calls it Internet Background Radiation.
- 23andMe: court approved sale of customer DNA data, so Steve repeats his advice to delete accounts (shortcut grc.sc/byebye).
- RansomLook.io lists new ransomware victims and group profiles and ransom notes; 22 new victims were posted for a single day (shortcut grc.sc/1019). The Arkana group had just hit the ISP WoW.
- InControl: a UK flight-sim user relies on it to stay on Windows 11 23H2 because 24H2 removes Windows Mixed Reality headset support.
- EU OS (Robert Riemann, European Data Protection Supervisor): a community proof of concept, built on Fedora with KDE Plasma, for public bodies with a few hundred users, emphasising secure, sovereign and sleek, no phoning home, and a shared way to manage users, software and devices (GitLab). Precedents cited: Munich LiMux, French Gendarmerie GendBuntu (about 82,000 seats), the Swiss Federal Court, Astra Linux, Kylin and Nova Linux, and a German state migration. Steve expects adoption from the bottom up as Windows 10 support ends and Windows 11 hardware requirements bite, and worries about volunteer maintainers ("the xkcd Dependency problem"). Leo speculates AI could eventually maintain infrastructure projects.
