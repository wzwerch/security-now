# Summary

Iyaz Akhtar substitutes for Leo as co-host. Steve covers May's Patch Tuesday, which fixed 33 flaws including the IE8 zero-day from the previous week, along with Flash, Reader, Acrobat and Firefox 21 updates, and relays Brian Krebs' plea that Microsoft stop pushing Silverlight on its security pages. He reports a seven-week wait for Apple to extract iPhone data for police, another Syrian Internet outage, and a H-Online experiment showing that Microsoft servers fetched private HTTPS links sent through Skype chat, which he reads as Microsoft monitoring Skype chats, so Skype should no longer be considered private. He describes The New Yorker's StrongBox, a Tor-based anonymous dead drop built on Aaron Swartz's DeadDrop code, and Oracle's new Java version-numbering scheme needed because of the volume of updates. The Department of Homeland Security seized Mt. Gox's funds held at Dwolla as an unlicensed money transmitter, and bitcoin dipped only briefly. The main topic is an EFF article on how Facebook lets advertisers target users using offline data from brokers Acxiom, Datalogix, Epsilon and cookie-based BlueKai, using hashed email matching and cookie matching, with a Byzantine opt-out. Steve is less alarmed than usual because of hashing barriers and Facebook's published standards but notes Facebook, which created the hashes, can reverse them and ignores Do Not Track.

# Key points

- May 2013 Patch Tuesday: 33 vulnerabilities fixed, the IE8 zero-day in a separate IE8 update; Flash fixes 13 flaws; Firefox 21 fixes a maintenance-service privilege escalation and three critical bugs.
- Krebs's open note asking Microsoft not to urge Silverlight installs on security update pages, in line with reducing attack surface.
- Report that police face a seven-week wait for Apple to crack iPhone encryption; Steve guesses brute-forcing on hardware and notes Apple holds iCloud keys.
- Syria dropped off the Internet again (about 8.5 hours); Google's speak-to-tweet service was relevant earlier.
- H-Online found that links with embedded credentials sent in Skype chats were fetched from a Microsoft IP in Redmond; Microsoft pointed to terms allowing spam scanning. Steve concludes Skype chat is surveilled and suggests examining Trust-No-One secure chat alternatives.
- The New Yorker launched StrongBox: Tor access, a random ID for follow-up, files decrypted on an air-gapped machine, three servers with one monitoring the others; based on the open DeadDrop project; it appeared as the Associated Press complained about Justice Department seizure of reporters' phone records.
- Oracle's Java numbering now jumps by 20 for feature updates and uses increments of five for critical patch updates.
- DHS alleged Mt. Gox was an unlicensed money transmitter and seized its Dwolla account; bitcoin dipped to about 105 dollars and recovered to about 115.
- EFF/Facebook explanation: data brokers (Acxiom, Datalogix, Epsilon, BlueKai) assemble offline profiles; for the first three, the broker hashes email addresses of a target audience and Facebook matches them to hashes of its own users, discarding non-matches; Facebook can still learn the audience from the ad it shows and reports aggregate performance.
- BlueKai works via tracking cookies and a pixel-and-redirect "cookie matching" on Facebook, so sites visited while logged out influence ads on Facebook.
- Opting out requires separate opt-outs at each broker, which merely suppresses data; the EFF recommends Ghostery, DoNotTrackMe or Adblock Plus with EasyPrivacy, and asks Facebook to honor Do Not Track and push for a single opt-out hub.
- Facebook published minimum standards for broker partners: inline transparency, control over ad display, disclosures and data-access tools.
- Government agencies work with data brokers (ChoicePoint, Acxiom) and mine social networks; employers do background checks too.
