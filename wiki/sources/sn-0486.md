# Summary

The Chrome security team proposes, for rollout during 2015, to mark non-HTTPS origins as affirmatively non-secure, and Steve hopes browsers will also distinguish domain-validated certificates from organization-validated ones as the EFF's free automated certificates arrive. A previously unreported February 2014 attack on Las Vegas Sands casinos is revealed: a Visual Basic wiper script erased servers and drives, causing an estimated 40 million dollars of damage, and was traced to Iran after Sheldon Adelson's remarks; the foothold was a brute-forced VPN at a remote casino. Steve and Leo then argue over whether the press should publish stolen Sony emails; Steve sees public-interest value in making the damage tangible to other executives, Leo calls it publishing stolen goods for clicks, and Brian Krebs reports a legal threat from Sony's lawyers. Verizon and Cellcrypt's Voice Cypher, an encrypted calling app with a lawful-access option, gets mocked. In the Q&A Steve explains his SSL Labs grade falling to C because he keeps old protocols for compatibility, discusses whether VPN exit location matters against NSA collection, recommends application whitelisting such as Windows AppLocker, clarifies that "nothing can be done" about Sony meant it is impractical to make a huge network fully secure, and explains credit-bureau-based identity questions and how 12 TB could leave a large network unnoticed.

# Key points

- Chrome security team proposal: gradually label HTTP as non-secure in the browser UI, with a transition plan in 2015. Steve says "non-secure" is accurate, and wants DV certificates visually distinguished from OV and EV ones.
- Sands casino attack (February 2014, disclosed late 2014): destructive malware wiped systems and cost about 40 million dollars; attackers entered via a brute-forced VPN at a remote property and moved to Las Vegas; good logging let investigators reconstruct it afterward. Analysis noted such attacks on small pieces of infrastructure fall below the threshold for a government response.
- Disclosure ethics: Steve argues, citing the Pentagon Papers and Snowden leaks, that publishing Sony's internals served a public interest by pushing companies to fund security; Leo says only the categories of stolen data should be reported, not private email contents. Sony's lawyer David Boies demanded that Krebs and others stop publishing and destroy the stolen data.
- Verizon Voice Cypher (built by Cellcrypt) is marketed as end-to-end encrypted voice but gives law enforcement access, costs about 45 dollars a month, and uses the slogan "Security when it matters most," which Steve finds ironic.
- A listener's SpinRite story: a RAID 5 array with two failed drives was brought back when one drive was revived on a laptop with SpinRite Level 2.
- SSL Labs grading: GRC dropped from A+ to C because it keeps RC4, older protocols and SHA-1 certificates for compatibility with old Windows XP clients; Steve plans to drop RC4 and SSLv3, which should raise it to B, and shares a hand-ordered cipher suite list for Windows servers (limited by a roughly 2,047 character registry string).
- A VPN protects against ISP metadata collection (IP addresses, DNS queries) but not against NSA taps on cables; Steve expects legislation mandating backdoors and says that is why he suspended CryptoLink.
- SmushBox (a USB SMS gateway) lost its special carrier deal, so owners must set up their own mobile plan.
- AppLocker, Windows 7/8's application whitelisting, restricts execution to approved programs and can run in an audit mode first; Steve plans to deploy it. Leo notes macOS Gatekeeper does something similar by default (App Store or identified developers). Whitelisting does not stop users from trusting a vulnerable approved program, and the Sony hack showed malware signed with a stolen Sony certificate.
- Steve's more nuanced Sony answer: lock down machines with whitelisting, give users an isolated virtual machine with Internet-only access for anything else, and silo networks so one foothold does not expose everything.
- FedEx-style identity checks ask multiple-choice questions drawn from credit bureau data, which lets a service authenticate a stranger without prior shared secrets.
- 12 TB leaving Sony is plausible without alarms because a large media company moves vast volumes of data; torrents were even seeded from Sony's own servers. Steve hopes a technical post-mortem will emerge. Leo mentions a February breach Sony reportedly chose not to disclose.
- Banks are likely better defended than Sony but are breached too (a large JPMorgan Chase breach in October affected 83 million accounts), and Steve notes personal and corporate accounts differ in fraud coverage.
