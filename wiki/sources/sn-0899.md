# Summary

In a potpourri episode, Steve starts with the international takedown of iSpoof, a caller ID spoofing service with about 59,000 users and over a million spoofed calls a month that was linked to roughly 115 million euros in fraud losses; 142 suspects were detained. Kasada describes "freebie bots" that scan retail sites for mispriced goods (often decimal-point errors) and buy them at scale before the error is fixed. PIXM Security details a multi-stage scam against crypto exchange users: lookalike login pages, a fake support chat, and a TeamViewer session used to approve the attacker's device and drain wallets. The Karakurt extortion gang advertised for recruits, Microsoft linked a Chinese attack on an Indian power grid operator to the unmaintained Boa web server embedded in IoT devices, and the US and UK restricted Chinese networking and surveillance equipment. Short items cover MIT's national cyber defence ranking, 1,652 malicious Docker Hub images, Chrome's eighth zero-day of 2022, CISA on Mastodon, and an anonymous advertiser's account of Twitter's deterioration. Listener feedback explains why quantum computers will not speed up password cracking, why some passkey sign-ins via a third-party provider (OwnID, as with Carnival Cruises) lose passkeys' phishing resistance, and how Steve signs code with a hardware module. SpinRite 6.1 alpha releases began with strong early results.

# Key points

- iSpoof: launched December 2020; Dutch police wiretapped the hosting, mapped users, and Europol with the FBI, Secret Service and UK Metropolitan Police seized the site; administrators earned about 3.7 million euros. Steve notes it ran almost two years and blames weak inter-carrier signalling (SS7).
- Freebie bots (Kasada): over 250 retailers targeted; one community bought nearly 100,000 items worth $3.4 million for $882, typically after price errors with up to 99% discounts; Steve calls it unethical, Leo says it is not his problem as a buyer.
- PIXM report: lookalike Coinbase, MetaMask, Crypto.com and KuCoin pages hosted as azurewebsites.net subdomains relay credentials and 2FA codes in real time; a fake chat support agent ("Veronica", tawk.to) pushes the victim to install TeamViewer, makes them re-enter their password in chat, and approves the attacker's device via the emailed confirmation link; funds are moved while the victim is kept waiting.
- Karakurt (linked to former Conti members; extortion without encryption, demands $25,000 to $13 million in Bitcoin, one-week deadlines, harassment of victims' contacts) posted a "great recruitment" ad for pen testers, malware developers, insiders, and social engineers.
- US government cybersecurity apprenticeship sprint hired about 7,000 apprentices.
- Boa: a tiny web server released in 1995 and last updated in 2005, no TLS support, still widely embedded in IoT and industrial devices; Microsoft identified it as the entry point for a China-linked intrusion at an Indian electricity grid operator (Recorded Future reporting). Steve found many past null-pointer fixes in its errata.
- On 25 November 2022 the US (Steve says the FTC; the transcript is likely garbled for the FCC) banned import and sale of networking and video equipment from Dahua, Hikvision, Huawei and ZTE, and the UK told departments to stop deploying Chinese cameras at sensitive sites. Steve is uneasy about bans but says trust cannot be proven for closed-source black boxes and favours open source.
- MIT ranking of national cyber defence: Australia, Netherlands, South Korea, US, Canada lead.
- Sysdig found 1,652 malicious images on Docker Hub, over a third with cryptominers, others with hidden backdoor tokens, proxy malware, or dynamic DNS tools.
- Chrome fixed its eighth in-the-wild zero-day of 2022 (CVE-2022-4135, GPU heap overflow); CISA opened an official Mastodon account on infosec.exchange after an impostor appeared.
- A B2B advertiser reports weaker Twitter ad performance, brand safety problems, a vanished account team and buggy tools after the ownership change, and threat intelligence firms say takedown requests may no longer be answered.
- Quantum computing threatens only asymmetric cryptography based on factoring (and similar problems); it does not meaningfully speed up password or hash brute forcing, and symmetric ciphers such as AES need no change. Steve compares it to nuclear fusion: promising but hard, so there is no rush.
- Passkey caveat: passkeys.directory lists many sites that use OwnID, whose QR-code flow sends the phone to the provider's domain, so a phishing site can relay the flow and defeat the domain binding that makes WebAuthn phishing-resistant; proper implementations call WebAuthn from the browser itself. The listing site also has opaque review via Google Forms.
- Steve explains HSM-based EV code signing (DigiCert key on a Gemalto/SafeNet USB token); per-customer SpinRite downloads will trigger Windows Defender reputation warnings because each executable is unique.
- Open registration is hard: GRC's forums once had 6,500 users, mostly bogus accounts from Afghanistan, Turkey, Indonesia and Russia, and 20,000 more attempts were blocked; GRC's GitLab is gated by information available in its text-only NNTP newsgroups (news.grc.com).
- SpinRite 6.1 alpha: first release went out on the Friday after Thanksgiving; testers report fast scans (1 TB level 4 in under 10 hours versus 150 before), revived USB sticks and recovered drives; the fourth alpha was out by Sunday; Steve's next task is evaluating the commercial RTOS (decision due by year end).
