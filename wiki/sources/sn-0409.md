# Summary

Steve reports an overwhelming reaction to his upstream-fiber-tap theory of PRISM and says a Narus marketing brochure that shows a prism graphic, plus reports in the Washington Post and CNET, are consistent with it; he notes Narus built the traffic analysis gear described in the AT&T San Francisco case and is now a Boeing subsidiary. Oracle released a Java critical patch fixing 40 flaws, 37 remotely exploitable. A Foreign Policy article describes sales spikes for Silent Circle, Whisper Systems, Cryptocat and Tor after the revelations, and Steve highlights the Threema messenger and the role of traffic analysis, which makes metadata about who talks to whom valuable even when content is encrypted. He returned the BlackBerry Q10, discusses reports that Canada needs PDP-11 programmers, and announces details of a free SpinRite 6.1 that will run on Intel Macs and bypass the BIOS, with development in the open on a GRC newsgroup. Listener questions cover how DNS settings cascade from ISP to modem to router to computer via DHCP, zettabytes, whether certificate authorities or leaked private keys would let the NSA break SSL, combining VPNs and Tor, how to protect oneself, SMTP over TLS, the way Windows fetches root certificates on demand, whether iMessage is really end-to-end protected, and the open-source TextSecure and RedPhone apps from Moxie Marlinspike.

# Key points

- Steve says the PRISM tap theory has support from Narus brochure imagery, though he concedes a half-silvered mirror splitter is what a fiber tap really is; the passive tap model implies SSL traffic is still unreadable unless keys leak.
- Oracle's critical patch update for Java fixed 40 flaws (37 remotely exploitable without credentials).
- Business is booming for secure communications: Silent Circle sales up 400 percent, Whisper Systems new users up 3,000 percent, Cryptocat added about 5,000 users, and Tor US users up 17 percent. The Silent Circle CEO says the NSA relies on traffic analysis rather than brute force, which maps entire social networks.
- Threema is highlighted as a well-designed messenger that grades trust levels, scans QR codes in person and pads message lengths.
- Canada's nuclear robotics run on PDP-11 assembly code, and the operator seeks programmers to keep it going until 2050.
- John McAfee's bizarre uninstall video is mentioned.
- SpinRite roadmap: the Mac-capable release is the same product, 6.1, free to all owners; Steve is writing his own low-level drivers to bypass the BIOS, enumerating the PCI bus for AHCI and IDE controllers, with 6.1 supporting Macs, large drives and UEFI and a later 6.2 for USB; development discussion is in the grc.spinrite.dev newsgroup. He ended CryptoLink partly because of government pressure for backdoors in commercial encryption.
- DNS settings cascade: the ISP supplies them via DHCP to the modem, which passes them to the router, which gives them to computers; overriding at any level affects everything downstream. Changing them in the router is simplest.
- Zettabyte scale: the Utah NSA data center is said to hold five zettabytes, five billion terabytes.
- SSL privacy is separate from authentication: a passive eavesdropper cannot decrypt a session even with certificates; the CA never holds the site's private key, but if the private key is stolen, an attacker could impersonate the site until revoked. Steve concludes pre-Internet encryption that you control is the safest course ("Trust No One").
- Combining VPN and Tor can be blocked by some providers; Steve has no good answer to protecting oneself from the NSA, and Leo notes cell phone location data are retained as business records.
- Leo notes violating a site's terms of service is treated as a federal crime under the Justice Department's reading of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, making stored data a basis for building cases.
- SMTP over TLS (STARTTLS, RFC 2487) exists but cannot be required, so most mail still crosses providers unencrypted; Steve's own mail server does not offer it.
- Windows Vista and later fetch missing root certificates on demand from Microsoft, which reveals which CAs a machine encounters.
- iMessage is proprietary and complex; Apple says it cannot decrypt messages, but new devices receive history, which Steve explains might work if keys derive from the user's authentication. He prefers open protocols such as Cryptocat and Silent Circle.
- TextSecure and RedPhone (Android, open source, Moxie Marlinspike) offer end-to-end encrypted messages and calls; metadata still leaks through cellular networks.
