# Summary

Steve reviews Venafi and Proofpoint survey results showing that paying a ransom rarely ends the matter: about a third of payers could not recover data, many were hit with repeated or multi-stage extortion, and some data still surfaced on the dark web, so his advice is to keep tested backups, refuse to pay, and notify law enforcement. Symantec's report on Daxin, a Windows kernel-driver backdoor linked to China and dating back to 2013 (with Zala as an ancestor), is described as extremely stealthy, hijacking existing TCP connections and relaying traffic across multiple infected hosts. A Log4j retrospective shows scanning peaked for about three weeks but never fell to zero, and an Iranian group exploited it against VMware Horizon. Steve covers cyber aspects of Russia's invasion of Ukraine (more DDoS, Russian sites returning HTTP 418 as geofencing, Russia's own DNS) and a disputed NBC report of US options for disruptive attacks. He notes that Windows Weekly hosts also called the Windows 11 hardware requirements arbitrary, answers a listener question on defending against BGP hijacks via certificate thumbprints, and reports on SpinRite pre-release 9. The main story is the Tel Aviv University paper "Trust Dies in Darkness": Samsung's Keymaster trusted application in TrustZone let callers supply AES-GCM initialization vectors, enabling key extraction on roughly 100 million Galaxy devices, and Steve argues security-critical designs must not stay proprietary and secret.

# Key points

- Venafi survey: 18 percent of payers still had data exposed, 35 percent paid but could not recover data, and 83 percent of successful attacks used double or triple extortion; Proofpoint: 70 percent of respondents hit in 2021, 60 percent of them negotiated, many paid more than once.
- Reasons ransomware promises are unreliable: short-lived RaaS brands, independent affiliates who hold exfiltrated data, data persisting in criminal markets.
- Daxin (Symantec, linked to China, seen as recently as November 2021, earliest sample 2013): kernel driver that hijacks legitimate TCP connections on pattern triggers, brings its own TCP/IP stack, does a custom key exchange, and chains encrypted hops across infected machines in an onion-like path; victims included governments, telecoms, transport and manufacturing; related to the older Zala backdoor (active since about 2009).
- Log4j/Log4Shell: opportunistic scanning spiked for about three weeks after 10 December 2021 because exploitation requires per-application work; persistent low-level scanning continues and state actors (Iran, per SentinelOne) use it.
- Ukraine war: renewed DDoS on Ukrainian targets and on Russian sites like RT; mil.ru and others answered with HTTP 418 "I'm a teapot" (RFC 2324 joke status) as a geofence against non-Russian traffic; Russia has built an internal DNS to survive disconnection.
- NBC reported that US cyber options against Russia (disrupting Internet, power, trains) were presented to the President; the White House denied it; Steve notes revealing such capabilities is costly and all sides fear cyber retaliation.
- Windows 11 requirements: Steve recalls SN 835 on TPM 1.2 versus 2.0 finding no technical reason for the TPM 2.0 requirement and welcomes Windows Weekly saying it was arbitrary; Windows 10 support ends 14 October 2025.
- BGP self-defense Q&A: individual banks do not have AS numbers; the hijack worked by getting a fresh certificate from an ACME CA, so checking that a site's certificate thumbprint is unchanged would reveal it, at the cost of false alarms on legitimate rekeys.
- SpinRite pre-release 9 was largely a success; a Supermicro BIOS issue and a mini-benchmark bug were fixed.
- Samsung Keymaster (Tel Aviv University, USENIX Security 2022): TrustZone Keymaster TA on Galaxy S8 to S21 wrapped keys with AES-GCM but let the caller set the IV; IV reuse allows hardware-protected key extraction, and a downgrade attack reached newer devices; consequences include a FIDO2 WebAuthn login bypass and compromise of Google's Secure Key Import.
- Disclosure and fixes: CVE-2021-25444 (IV reuse, reported May 2021, patched August 2021 by removing the custom IV option) and CVE-2021-25490 (downgrade, reported July 2021, patched October 2021); older unpatched devices remain exposed; exploitation needs a malicious app and repeated operations, so it is mainly a targeted-attack concern.
- Lesson: authenticated encryption order (encrypt then authenticate), nonce uniqueness, and the principle that implementations as well as algorithms must be public and audited.
