# Summary

The week before, the TrueCrypt website redirected to a bare page warning that the software "may contain unfixed security issues" and recommending BitLocker, together with version 7.2, which can only mount existing volumes. Steve spent a week analysing it, wrote blog posts, and published an archive of the 7.1a release. He concludes the developers made this deliberately: after ten years, with no activity for 27 months and Windows 8, UEFI and GPT support looming, they decided they were done and wanted people off the code rather than to see it forked. A developer reportedly said they were happy with the audit and had had no government contact, and the audit (by the Open Crypto Audit project) would continue. Steve keeps using 7.1a; Leo is uneasy about trusting software that warns against itself and whose authors are anonymous. Before that, Steve discusses how the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act is used to threaten vulnerability researchers, a court order giving the FBI a two-week window to interfere with the GameOver Zeus botnet (and why small businesses are especially exposed to its bank fraud), the Snowden interview with Brian Williams, and Apple's WWDC announcements (app extensions, third-party keyboards, Touch ID for apps, HomeKit, and the Swift language, which traps integer overflow).

# Key points

- CFAA is broad enough that companies threaten researchers who disclose unfixed flaws after vendor inaction; Congress wants to widen it. Leo cites Aaron Swartz, Randal Schwartz and Adrian Lamo as examples of overzealous use. Steve suggests anonymity for disclosure.
- Brian Williams interviewed Edward Snowden, who said he raised concerns internally; Snowden said he would like to return home.
- A US court gave the FBI a limited two-week authority to redirect GameOver Zeus botnet traffic; estimates were 0.5 to 1 million infected PCs, about a quarter in the US, with over 100 million dollars lost.
- Zeus targets online banking; small businesses lack the legal protections consumers have, so Steve again urges disabling wire transfers and firewalling accounts.
- iOS 8 will allow curated extensions, third-party keyboards, Touch ID for third-party apps, per-app battery use and HomeKit; Steve likes starting closed and opening carefully.
- Swift removes silent integer overflow and switch fall-through, which would prevent bugs like Apple's earlier "goto fail"; Steve says he will stay with assembly.
- TrueCrypt 7.2 removed the ability to create volumes and added restartable decryption: significant deliberate work, so Steve believes the developers themselves published it.
- Theories considered: site hack (ruled out by SourceForge), warrant canary (developer denied government contact beyond one support-contract inquiry), fear of audit findings (Steve doubts).
- The first phase of the TrueCrypt audit covered only the bootloader and Windows kernel driver and criticised code quality (comments, deprecated functions); Steve still finds the code beautiful.
- TrueCrypt is cross-platform, usable on Windows, Mac and Linux with the same volumes, which no built-in alternative (BitLocker, FileVault) offers; Steve distrusts BitLocker over government requests.
- The audit group said it may support a fork under a free license, and continues the cryptanalysis of 7.1; the name will likely have to change.
- TrueCrypt's modern mode is XTS; CBC and LRW remain for compatibility and could be dropped in a successor.
- A SpinRite listener story and Steve's explanation of why reading a drive with `cat` is not the same as SpinRite's Level 2 scan.

# Other segments

- SQRL: decisions on where the client looks for identity files (documents folder, program folder, current directory) to support portable use.
- TV series "Halt and Catch Fire" comments (a Compaq-like BIOS cloning story) and Harry's razor remarks.
- Podtrac counting of downloads explained; the show passed 100,000 listeners.
