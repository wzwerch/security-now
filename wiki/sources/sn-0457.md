# Summary

This Q&A episode opens with a guest, Brett Glass, a veteran columnist and operator of what he describes as the first wireless ISP (in Laramie, Wyoming, around 1,000 accounts and about 1.25 Gbit/s of backhaul). He argues that "Net Neutrality" is poorly defined, that ISPs have not historically censored content, that heavy-handed regulation (Title II common-carrier rules) would hurt small ISPs, and that a two-sided market, where heavy streaming services share costs with ISPs, is fairer than caps. Leo strongly disagrees, arguing edge providers should not pay for access to ISP customers, and that large ISPs such as Comcast have misbehaved; Steve leans toward usage-based billing like electricity and notes the value of caching video inside ISPs. In the news, Steve explains a registry hack that makes Windows XP look like the POS-embedded edition so it keeps receiving updates until 2019, while Microsoft warns it is untested. eBay admitted losing its customer database (names, hashed passwords, addresses, phone numbers, birth dates) and reduced the maximum password length to 20 characters. Some Australian Apple devices were locked remotely by someone calling himself "Oleg Pliss", and an Apple software-update certificate lapsed. Listener questions cover Steve's monotasking, archive downloads, his private ".steve" DNS top-level domain, SSL termination at Rackspace load balancers, firmware in wireless chips, whether to add a timestamp to SQRL keys (no, randomness is always better), hostile kernel modules versus RdRand, and the 2FA voicemail attack as a password-guessing oracle.

# Key points

- Brett Glass: net neutrality regulation is aimed at feared, not documented, ISP abuses; past incidents (such as Madison River blocking VoIP) were short-lived under consumer pressure; Title II would impose old telephone rules.
- Two-sided market idea: customers pay a flat fee while content providers like Netflix pay the ISP for the extra load. Leo and Steve debate this; Leo cites Sandvine deep packet inspection and interconnect congestion as evidence of bad faith.
- Netflix Open Connect is a pre-loaded server, not a cache; Glass says Netflix refuses to let small ISPs cache content, which he finds wasteful.
- A registry key (PosReady) tricks Windows Update on XP into treating it as embedded XP and delivering updates until 2019; Microsoft says the updates are not tested for XP and the trick may be closed.
- eBay's breach exposed customers' personal data and passwords stored in an undisclosed "encrypted" form; users were told to change passwords. Steve notes password reuse as the real danger.
- eBay cut its allowed password length from 64 to 20 characters although the form still permits 64; Steve says 20 random characters is still strong.
- Australian iOS and Mac devices were locked with a passcode set remotely by "Oleg Pliss", with a ransom request to a PayPal address that did not exist; likely reused iCloud credentials. Devices with a passcode could be restored from backup.
- Apple's software-update server certificate expired and was reissued as a two-year certificate.
- SQRL secure storage format was redesigned as a compact binary format that can be printed as text or QR code for paper backup.
- SpinRite helped a university team recover data from an unbootable laptop after 50 bad sectors were fixed; Steve says SpinRite on drives with problems is fine but check SMART readings and back up more often.
- Archive bandwidth: Steve explains his datacenter 95/5 billing and why he does not offer a bulk torrent of 457 episodes; a community script downloads episodes and Leo offers to seed a BitTorrent archive.
- SSL terminated at a load balancer means traffic is unencrypted inside the host's network (as at Rackspace); acceptable if you already trust the host.
- Wireless chips and basebands run their own closed firmware (a Broadcom chip with an ARM core was the example); Steve says this is the architecture of modern devices and other worries are bigger.
- Adding a 32-bit timestamp to a random key would reduce, not improve, collision resistance; pure randomness is always better.
- An attacker with root can defeat any software-only entropy collection (hardware breakpoints, kernel modules), which is why hardware authenticators and the SQRL rescue code matter.
- If two-factor prompts appear only after a correct password, they confirm password guesses; always prompting would enable a denial-of-service flood of messages.
