# Summary

Leo announces that from January 2014 the show moves to Tuesdays at 1 pm Pacific. Steve starts with Firefox 25, part of Mozilla's 42-day release cycle, which exposes full cipher-suite details to add-ons such as Calomel SSL Validation and keeps session cookies across a browser restart. His "bad idea of the week" is LinkedIn Intro, which would proxy a user's email through LinkedIn's servers and rewrite messages. Follow-up on CryptoLocker: a listener's employer paid the ransom of about 300 dollars and got all files back, which shows the criminals do deliver decryption; the malware also encrypts mapped network drives, so versioned backups are essential. Steve then covers two Snowden items: UK Prime Minister Cameron threatening newspapers over leaks, and a leaked NSA slide showing a tap on Google's unencrypted data-centre links behind the front-end servers that terminate SSL (MUSCULAR, run jointly with GCHQ). He praises Threema as properly designed point-to-point encrypted messaging using Curve25519, notes that BlackBerry Messenger is not strong security, and announces SQRL now means Secure Quick Reliable Login, with a new Identity Lock protocol. The Q&A covers verifying a VPN with packet capture, whether disk encryption protects against ransomware, free antivirus and firewall choices, Match.com emailing another user's credentials to a wrong address, TrueCrypt's status and CryptoLocker prevention via group policy.

# Key points

- Firefox 25: release train every 42 days; Web Audio API completed; add-ons can read the full negotiated cipher suite (used to grade perfect forward secrecy); session cookies now survive a restart; guest browsing and mixed-content blocking arrive on Android.
- LinkedIn Intro: users hand over email credentials so LinkedIn can store, modify and forward their mail. Steve calls it a terrible idea given LinkedIn's earlier loss of millions of password hashes, and notes it can even undermine attorney-client privilege.
- CryptoLocker recovery reports: paying the ransom produced working decryption in the cases reported; the risk is that law enforcement may take down key servers, and payments fuel the business. Prevention depends on backups with enough version depth to predate the infection.
- CryptoLocker enumerates all drive letters, so mapped corporate shares get encrypted too.
- Whole-disk encryption such as TrueCrypt gives no protection against ransomware running on a mounted, unlocked system; the data is simply encrypted twice. Running email in a virtual machine isolated from the real files is a much stronger defence.
- CryptoPrevent and a Spiceworks group-policy kit block the places CryptoLocker installs from; Steve calls this a useful but soft fix that the authors could route around, and hosted the toolkit on GRC after inspecting it. BleepingComputer's CryptoLocker page is his recommended reference.
- NSA MUSCULAR slide: traffic between Google data centres was in the clear after the front end removed SSL and the agency, with GCHQ, tapped it. Steve says it makes technical sense because many sites terminate SSL at accelerators.
- David Cameron warned newspapers that the government may act unless they show more "social responsibility" over Snowden material.
- Threema: client-generated Curve25519 key pairs, private key never leaves the device, server stores messages for at most 14 days; colour-coded dots show how well a contact's key was verified (in-person QR scan gives the highest level). Steve calls it a trust-no-one design.
- BBM reached 20 million downloads in a week, but Steve warns it is not strongly secure.
- SQRL is renamed Secure Quick Reliable Login to stop people thinking it is only about QR codes. Identity Lock lets a client silently supply per-site proof that a hacked client cannot abuse, and an offline unlock key lets the user reclaim a stolen identity.
- VPN verification: GRC cannot show whether a tunnel is encrypted; use a packet capture tool such as Wireshark (or Android capture apps) on the physical interface and compare traffic with the tunnel up and down.
- Known- and chosen-plaintext attacks: modern ciphers like AES do not leak the key from plaintext-ciphertext pairs, whereas a plain XOR stream cipher does, which is why key streams must never be reused.
- Antivirus advice: Steve and Leo see Microsoft Security Essentials and the Windows firewall plus a NAT router as adequate; behaviour matters more than product; Steve nearly fell for a fake PayPal email and notes his old mail client hides link targets.
- Match.com sent a listener another member's screen name, password and personal details after she mistyped her address, showing plaintext password storage and no address verification.
- TrueCrypt is finished rather than dead; a community audit fundraiser is under way, though UEFI full-disk support is missing.
