# Summary

Steve hosted this episode with Father Robert Ballecer standing in for Leo. The FBI asked a court to postpone its hearing against Apple after an outside party showed it a possible way to unlock the San Bernardino phone, and both hosts doubted that cloning storage would defeat a key held in the Secure Enclave. They also noted Microsoft's German datacenter arrangement with a local trustee as a legal-jurisdiction workaround. Matthew Green and four students published an attack on iMessage that could decrypt attachments because the protocol does not authenticate ciphertext properly; Apple mitigated it in iOS 9.3 but the design flaw remains. Tel Aviv researchers extracted elliptic-curve signing keys from phones using a hand-wound coil and a sound card. An Angler-based malvertising campaign hit many major sites through several ad networks, and Lenovo's start page was briefly serving the same exploit kit. Pwn2Own 2016 netted about 460,000 dollars of prizes, with Windows 10 and OS X each having several bugs, Edge improved over IE, Chrome strongest, and no-one bothering with Firefox. A new Stagefright-related exploit called Metaphor defeated address randomisation on some Android versions. The main topic was DROWN, which uses SSLv2 handshakes as an oracle to decrypt recorded TLS sessions even when the target browser uses modern TLS.

# Key points

- FBI told the court on 21 March that an outside party had demonstrated a possible unlock method; a status report was due by 5 April. Steve noted the Burr-Feinstein bill might require decryption capability after a future date.
- Padre describes Microsoft's German cloud run by a trustee so the data sits under German jurisdiction, as a legal rather than cryptographic response to government demands.
- iMessage (before iOS 9.3 and OS X 10.11.4): attachments could be decrypted slowly over many probes through a padding-oracle-style weakness because the design signs but does not properly authenticate the ciphertext; Apple's mitigation is to detect repeated probing, which Steve calls a kludge; Green suggests moving to a vetted protocol such as Signal's. Users should update.
- Side-channel on phones: electromagnetic or power measurement via a coil on a sound card extracted ECDSA keys from OpenSSL and a Bitcoin library on iOS and partially from Android implementations; EM shielding history (Tempest) noted.
- Malvertising wave in early March via Angler: sites included major news outlets; the code was heavily obfuscated, avoided security tools, and used a lapsed domain bought a day before. Advice: remove unused plugins like Flash, Java and Silverlight, use uBlock Origin rather than NoScript, and consider browser isolation in a VM or container.
- Lenovo's start page was found delivering Angler; Padre laments pre-installed crapware.
- Pwn2Own: 21 vulnerabilities, Windows 10 had 6 and OS X 5, Safari 3; Edge is much tougher than IE; the VM-escape prize went unclaimed.
- Metaphor exploit against Stagefright: an information leak reveals library layout so the ASLR randomisation no longer protects Android 2.2 to 4.0 and 5.0 to 5.1 devices (about 300 million phones); Google said devices with a patch level of October 2015 or later are protected. It needs device-specific payloads.
- Steve's new PC: an NVMe boot SSD kept mostly empty plus mirrored 3 TB hard drives, nightly imaging with TeraByte's Image for Windows.
- SlySoft/AnyDVD successor RedFox moved to an online model.
- Solid-state lithium battery research from Toyota and academics: wide temperature range, fast charging, early stage.
- SpinRite on SSDs: use Level 2 (read mostly), since writing fatigues cells and the same error-correction codes make unreadable bits recoverable.
- DROWN: an attacker records about a thousand TLS handshakes, then makes tens of thousands of SSLv2 export-grade handshakes to a server sharing the key, using a Bleichenbacher-style oracle to decrypt a session (not the private key). Slow version: roughly eight hours on cloud hardware for about 440 dollars; fast version works in real time on a laptop against servers with OpenSSL older than a year (26 percent of HTTPS servers). Key reuse across email, VPN or other servers extends exposure (33 percent of servers). Fix: disable SSLv2 everywhere, update OpenSSL, avoid sharing certificates with legacy servers. Not exploited in the wild at that time. Steve notes it is a legacy of 1990s export-weakened crypto.

# Other segments

- SQRL status: client being updated so identities can hold four previous keys; iOS and Android clients under way and a demo server in use.
- Steve and Padre's chat about copper-mesh shielded rooms and shows such as Billions.
