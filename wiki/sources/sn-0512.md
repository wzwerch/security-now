# Summary

Steve first explains the LastPass network breach disclosure: the company saw anomalous traffic, assumed prudently that email addresses, password reminders, per-user salts and authentication hashes may have been taken, but said the encrypted vaults were not exposed. Steve describes the design (local key derivation with many iterations, then 100,000 additional server-side iterations with a per-user salt), argues the risk is limited to targeted attacks against weak master passwords or reused passwords, and advises changing the master password, raising the iteration count to a random five-digit number, and enabling two-factor login, though he notes two-factor does not address password reuse. He then covers the far larger second breach at the US Office of Personnel Management, which exposed security-clearance background forms and up to 14 million personnel records, and dismisses a British press claim that Russia and China decrypted Snowden's archive. Smaller items cover Mozilla and Chrome considering dropping built-in FTP, a BGP mistake by Telekom Malaysia that diverted Level 3 traffic, Wikipedia going HTTPS-only, and a Carnegie Mellon and Facebook study of SSD failures. The main topic is Firefox tracking protection, with debate between Steve and Leo about the ethics of blocking ads and trackers, perverse incentives, and page-weight evidence.

# Key points

- LastPass: breach could have exposed email addresses, reminders, per-user salts and authentication hashes, not vault data; the company reported scaling problems from users changing passwords and plans to use more cloud scale and network segmentation. Steve stays a customer and cites the PBKDF2 design, the per-user salt (defeating rainbow tables), and the email-confirmation step for new devices.
- Advice: change the master password (long, random, written down safely), do not reuse it elsewhere, use a nonstandard iteration count, enable two-factor; Steve notes SHA-256 is the Bitcoin hash so fast hardware exists. He contrasts this with SQRL, which stores no central secrets.
- OPM second breach: background-investigation forms (SF-86) with highly sensitive personal data, plus a separate personnel data theft affecting up to 14 million current and former employees; officials suspected China; a former counterintelligence official said it identifies almost everyone with a clearance. Steve and Leo contrast this with government demands for encryption access.
- The British report that China and Russia decrypted Snowden's documents was criticised by Glenn Greenwald as false, and Steve finds no reason to believe Snowden carried the files to Russia.
- Mozilla bug and Chromium discussion on removing built-in FTP support; only a tiny share of users open FTP links.
- BGP incident: on 12 June, Telekom Malaysia announced about 179,000 routing prefixes belonging to Level 3, pulling traffic and overloading it for about two hours with packet loss near 100 percent for some paths. Steve notes BGP was sketched on napkins and was meant to be temporary.
- Wikimedia announced HTTPS plus HSTS for all sites; Steve checked it on SSL Labs and found forward-secret suites, OCSP stapling, and a 180-day HSTS period.
- Twitter planned to expand direct messages to 10,000 characters; the Syfy show Dark Matter looked poor to Steve.
- SSD study (Carnegie Mellon and Facebook): SSD failures correlate with temperature, power use and write volume; many drives report uncorrectable read errors, and once they appear they tend to recur; sparse writes wear drives faster; Steve links this to his plan for SpinRite 6.1 to 6.3 and a rewritten version 7.
- Tracking protection in Firefox 35 (behind about:config): blocks about 1,500 tracker domains rather than ads; Steve measured a news site dropping from 162 requests and 4.5 MB to 139 requests and 3.6 MB; a Mozilla paper reports a 67.5 percent reduction in cookies and 44 percent better performance across 200 news sites, and that one in twenty machines show ad-injection malware.
- A former Mozilla privacy engineer argued advertising externalises costs and that the browser should act as the user's agent.
- Debate: Leo argues blocking ads while consuming content is unethical and that sites should be avoided instead; Steve says he accepts ads but objects to intrusive ones, hidden profiling scripts and the malware risk, and that site greed, not blocking, drives ad bloat; both agree an in-browser warning about heavy tracking would be useful.

# Other segments

- Banter on Star Trek and the Silicon Valley season finale; cartoon of "Human Error" as the weakest link.
- Steve asks listeners for one-minute tip ideas for The New Screen Savers.
- SQRL nearing completion, with bug fixing before returning to SpinRite 6.1.
