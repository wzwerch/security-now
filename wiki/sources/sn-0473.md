# Summary

Steve starts with a personal note: his ISP Cogent told him his Cisco router was answering SNMP queries and being used as a reflector in a UDP amplification attack, so he turned that off. The news covers reports that Comcast agents told customers to stop using Tor (which Comcast denied), Tim Cook's claim on a TV interview that Apple cannot read iMessages (Steve says Apple does manage the keys and could, even if it does not), a LinkedIn feature that let researchers confirm guessed email addresses of famous people through fake address-book imports, and a severe same-origin-policy bypass in the default Android browser on pre-KitKat devices that is trivially exploitable and already in Metasploit. LastPass announced iOS 8 support with Touch ID and Safari integration, and SQRL's Windows client UI was handed to testers. The main topic is Google's decision to start warning in Chrome in November 2014 about any SHA-1 certificate valid into 2016 or 2017, even though about 92 percent of certificates were SHA-1 and Microsoft had already announced a 2017 sunset. Steve explains certificate signing, the history of MD5 collisions, and why practical attacks on SHA-1 were not expected before roughly 2021. He argues that the short notice, the user-facing "insecure" warning for sites that are not actually broken, the CloudFlare and DigiCert objections about XP SP2 compatibility, and Google's own use of short-lived SHA-1 certificates make the move heavy-handed and likely to teach users to ignore warnings.

# Key points

- UDP reflection: an open SNMP service on Steve's router was abused for amplification; ISPs notify the owner when abuse reports arrive.
- Comcast and Tor: reports of support agents calling Tor illegal and asking which sites were visited; a Comcast vice president denied any such policy. Steve judges it was likely clueless support staff, not policy.
- Tim Cook said Apple has no keys to iMessages; Steve says that is only technically defensible since Apple supplies the recipient keys to senders and so could read messages if it chose.
- LinkedIn's "find your contacts" feature let two researchers feed many guessed email addresses through a fake address book to learn real ones for celebrities, then use them for social engineering; Steve calls it a design side effect, not a severe breach.
- Android AOSP browser before KitKat has a same-origin-policy bypass triggered by a leading Unicode null in a script URL, letting a malicious page read other open pages and session cookies. It is already in Metasploit; advice: use Chrome or Firefox instead of the stock browser on old Android.
- LastPass announced iOS 8 support with Touch ID and a Safari extension.
- SQRL Windows client UI is working and under test, with Wine display issues being chased; the online protocol is next.
- A listener's story of SpinRite repairing a Synology array disk that fell off a moving truck.
- How certificates work: the CA signs a hash of the certificate; breaking the hash lets an attacker alter a certificate while keeping a valid signature.
- MD5 history: the 2008 team used a cluster of PlayStation 3 consoles to forge an intermediate CA certificate against RapidSSL, thirteen years after the first weaknesses; the world then dropped MD5.
- Microsoft announced in November 2013 that SHA-1 would be sunset in 2017; this drew little opposition because of the long lead time.
- Google's August 2014 "Intent to Deprecate" announcement: from Chrome 39 (November), certificates expiring during 2016 get a degraded indicator and certificates valid into 2017 get stronger warnings and mixed-content-style prompts, despite 92 percent of the web using SHA-1.
- Objections from CloudFlare (customers' visitors on XP SP2 cannot validate SHA-256) and DigiCert (a March 2016 threshold would still force the transition without breaking existing plans).
- Steve points out a double standard because Google itself mints short-lived SHA-1 certificates that never trip its own rule, and says there is no known practical SHA-1 break; the estimate for non-government feasibility is around 2021.
- Steve believes governments need not crack hashes because they can obtain certificates; the real risk is warning fatigue when users are taught to ignore Chrome's alerts. He plans to see whether DigiCert can issue a SHA-1 certificate ending before 2016 for GRC.
- Leo notes the change was in Canary at the time and could still be softened.
