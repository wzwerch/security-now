# Summary

At the end of the show's twelfth year, Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte follow up on Marcus Hutchins, who pleaded not guilty and was released with travel limits. Steve recounts how NIST's 2003 password-rotation advice came from Bill Burr, who now regrets it, and why the 2017 rewrite drops expiration and forced special characters. He debunks two clickbait stories: the "DNA hack" depended on a deliberately broken program and worked a minority of the time, and the "stop sign graffiti hacks cars" research attacked only a small home-built classifier. Microsoft became the last major vendor to distrust new WoSign and StartCom certificates. Steve argues for global treaties on privacy and encryption policy, looks at the UK's data protection bill, praises TunnelBear's published audit in contrast to Hotspot Shield, and puts ElcomSoft's GPU cracker for password-manager vaults in perspective.

# Key points

- Hutchins: pleaded not guilty; the government now calls the alleged crimes "historic" and relaxed some bail conditions (he must stay in the US and may not touch the WannaCry sinkhole); trial could slip if designated complex.
- NIST SP 800-63 (2003) was written by Bill Burr under time pressure without real password data; he now says periodic changes and complexity rules were largely wrong. The 2017 revision drops expiration and character-class requirements. Steve notes password-manager users are unaffected.
- University of Washington "DNA malware" paper: researchers deliberately weakened an open-source DNA compression tool with an undersized buffer; the encoded payload ran only about 37 percent of the time because of sequencing errors and strand direction. Useful as a reminder that bioinformatics tools need hardening, not evidence of real attacks.
- "Stickers fool self-driving cars": the paper "Robust Physical-World Attacks on Machine Learning Models" fooled a small classifier trained on a US road sign dataset (about 91 percent accurate); the authors posted a FAQ saying no real car was attacked.
- Microsoft will stop trusting new WoSign and StartCom certificates in Windows 10 using a NotBefore date of September 26, 2017; Steve asks how Windows 7 and 8.1 will be protected, since Chrome and Firefox already act on their own.
- UK Data Protection Bill statement of intent (aligned with the EU GDPR taking effect May 2018): easier consent withdrawal and data erasure, personal data to include IP addresses, cookies and DNA, and a new offense for reidentifying anonymized data. Critics note it is unenforceable against ad-tech business models and could hinder researchers. Steve concludes global Internet policy needs treaties.
- Kids Pass (UK): any user could read others' data by changing an ID in the URL and passwords were emailed in plaintext; the company blocked the reporting user on Twitter until public pressure grew.
- TunnelBear published two Cure53 white-box audits (2016 and 2017), including embarrassing 2016 findings; Steve holds it up as the right way to run a VPN, contrasted with Hotspot Shield.
- ElcomSoft added GPU brute-forcing of master passwords for 1Password, KeePass, LastPass and Dashlane; LastPass tested at about 44,800 guesses per second on one GPU, 1Password about 95,000, Dashlane about 129,000, versus 7,300 for Office 2016. A long random master password defeats it; Steve contrasts SQRL's memory-hard EnScrypt at one guess every five seconds.
- S3 follow-up: Steve stores 88 GB for about $2 a month. Mozilla Send works in Safari after all.
- HTTP 418 "I'm a Teapot" status code being removed from Node prompted listener outcry.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: security-question form filled with GUIDs; Steve notes GRC's Password Haystacks page gets about 4,100 hits a day.
- Colossus: The Forbin Project available on Archive.org; Rho Agenda author's note.
- SpinRite as a way to extend a failing drive's life; SQRL install, update and removal features are essentially finished.
- Tweet of the week: set your password to "incorrect".
