# Summary

CloudSEK and Hudson Rock analyzed a 195 TB trove stolen during a 40-minute window in March, when TeamPCP's malicious LiteLLM 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 releases (enabled by the earlier Trivy compromise) harvested secrets from about 434,000 CI/CD runs at some 2,500 organizations, including Nvidia, AWS, Samsung, Cisco and Salesforce. Kevin Beaumont found that a large tech company claiming to have rotated its credentials had not. Steve's takeaway: an AI proxy needs every user's secrets, so organizations should make full credential rotation a one-command, easy task, perhaps built with AI.

France's Constitutional Council struck down the under-15 social media ban for failing to define age-proof conditions; Macron wants a rewrite before spring 2027. A Security found a Zoom screen-sharing annotation flaw allowing silent device takeover with fewer than 20 prompts to a public AI model. CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks shares hit highs after Black Hat. The main topic explains how next-token predictors became chatbots (instruction tuning, RLHF, DPO), why refusal training is brittle ("abliteration") and how AE Studio and Anthropic's GRAM confines dual-use knowledge in removable modules.

# Key points

- LiteLLM is an open-source gateway to 100+ LLM providers, so it holds all of a team's API keys. The malicious versions used a `.pth` startup hook to run on interpreter start, harvested environment variables, `.kube/config` and `.aws/credentials`, moved laterally in Kubernetes and installed a systemd backdoor. Hudson Rock attributed 118,829 CI runner dumps to 2,488 corporate domains; many generic pipeline secrets cannot be tied to an owner.
- CloudSEK: Trivy's developers rotated but did not fully revoke an automation token for 20 days, giving TeamPCP its window. Steve notes that staying current is what exposed users; his action item is easy, organization-wide credential rotation.
- France: the Council found the law disproportionately infringed freedom of expression and lacked privacy safeguards. Australia's under-16 ban has had mixed results; China, the UAE and Turkey have similar measures.
- Zoom: the A Security team (Omer Gull, Yossi Torati) found the flaw in the annotation protocol in early June; Zoom fixed server and clients on all platforms. Steve expects a rough patch but no Y2K-style apocalypse, because attackers follow money.
- BTIG analysts say AI agents have become the predominant attack threat and AI security deployment is in "early innings".
- How chatbots came to be: GPT-3 could only complete "The capital of France is"; instruction tuning and RLHF on tens of thousands of examples reshaped whole models (the "superficial alignment hypothesis"). Stanford's 2023 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) replaced RLHF and spawned IPO, KTO, ORPO and SimPO. Post-training also shapes tone, reduces sycophancy and adds refusals based on "uplift".
- "Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction" (ETH Zurich, Maryland, Anthropic, MIT, 2024): subtracting one activation direction removes refusals in 13 open models with little other effect. A Hugging Face post, "Uncensor any LLM with abliteration", spread the technique; abliterated open models are widely available.
- GRAM (Gradient-Routed Auxiliary Modules): extra neurons per layer grouped into modules per dual-use category; when training on, say, virology text, only that module learns. Deleting the module removes the capability about as well as never training on the data, resists re-learning attacks, and four modules yield 16 configurations from one training run. The gap grew with model size (50 million to 5 billion parameters); not yet used in Claude.
- Steve argues commercial providers may gate access to dual-use knowledge; unrestricted open models (such as Venice.ai) remain available to anyone who objects.
