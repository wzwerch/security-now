# Summary

Steve opens with a "keep calm and carry on" theme: much of the press coverage is overwrought, and users rationally weigh security advice. It was a slow news week. Leo thanks DigiCert for offering TWiT a free EV certificate, which prompts a short discussion of why even passive sites benefit from HTTPS (injection attacks against trusted sites). A DDoS knocked the Sony PlayStation network offline, claimed by Lizard Squad, who also tweeted a bomb threat that grounded a Sony Online executive's flight. Steve and Leo criticise the new White House cybersecurity coordinator, who said deep technical knowledge is a distraction and that he would focus on economics and psychology. The main story is the first confirmed major breach traced to Heartbleed: Chinese attackers repeatedly probed a still-vulnerable Juniper VPN server at hospital chain Community Health Systems, captured an administrator's VPN credentials and then stole about 4.5 million patient records. Steve notes that many hundreds of thousands of servers were still unpatched months later. The rest of the show is listener feedback on ground-potential differences when running copper between buildings, chip cards, zip-code prompts at gas pumps, StartSSL's free certificates, offline versus online password attacks, password-change policies, how SpinRite Level 2 helps and why it cannot see inside hardware RAID. The show ends by setting up next week's topic, Matthew Green's critique of PGP.

# Key points

- Steve's overall stance: headlines are often hyped, people ignore advice for rational reasons, and security should be proportionate rather than paralysing.
- DigiCert offered TWiT a free EV certificate; Steve argues sites with a reputation should offer HTTPS even for passive content because plain connections can be tampered with in transit.
- Lizard Squad attacked the Sony PlayStation network (and earlier game networks such as Blizzard and Riot) and used a fake bomb-threat tweet against a Sony Online executive's flight; Leo and Steve condemn it as criminal harassment, not activism.
- New US cybersecurity coordinator Michael Daniel said being too deep in technical weeds is a distraction and he would focus on economics and psychology; the technical community reacted with dismay, though Steve concedes the job is policy-level.
- Community Health Systems breach: roughly 4.5 million patient records (names, addresses, probably Social Security numbers) exfiltrated to China after attackers used Heartbleed against an Internet-facing Juniper VPN server to harvest an administrator's VPN credentials.
- Lesson of the CHS case: Heartbleed was a real, patiently exploitable memory-leak flaw; forgotten "machines in the closet" such as VPN appliances stay vulnerable, and an IDS could easily flag repeated Heartbleed probing.
- Ethernet runs between separate buildings should use fiber: copper connects the grounds of two electrical systems, and ground-potential differences can fry ports and switches. Steve explains differential signalling and why it tolerates small but not large ground differences.
- A listener's test of telling Wells Fargo "I am recording too" produced an organised response (hold, then repeated notice), showing the company anticipates it.
- American Express is issuing chip cards (still with a magnetic stripe). Steve explains that a chip can answer a one-time challenge without revealing its secret, which defeats replay of stolen static card data; the US was lagging behind other countries.
- Zip-code prompts at gas pumps act as a second factor, but if the pump itself is compromised it can capture all factors, so verification should happen as far from the user as possible.
- Steve corrects himself: free StartSSL certificates are trusted by browsers, validated by an email or on-site proof of domain control, limited to non-commercial use, one year validity and a single domain name plus www.
- Steve's password discussions assume offline attacks on a stolen password database, not online guessing, which is easily rate-limited.
- Periodic password changes have a real benefit: they cut off earlier silent compromises and shared access (for instance relatives who knew an old password).
- SpinRite Level 2 helps because it disables drive retries and caching, making the drive more sensitive to growing defects it can then repair itself; higher levels also write inverted data patterns to exercise every spot.
- SpinRite cannot see individual drives behind a hardware RAID controller; drives must be attached directly to the motherboard, whereas motherboard "thin RAID" drives are visible.
- Matthew Green's blog post questioning whether PGP needs a modern redesign (for example elliptic-curve keys) is announced as next week's topic; Leo says he moved to S/MIME.

- Short aside on shaving and razors.
