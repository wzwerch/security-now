# Summary

Chrome 86 brings Safety Check password checkup to mobile, touch-to-fill with biometrics on iOS, Enhanced Safe Browsing on mobile, warnings for insecure form submissions, blocking of executable and archive downloads from secure pages, staged removal of FTP (disabled for 1% of stable users now, 50% in 87, gone in 88), and a Native File System API that Steve flags as risky. Ransomware coverage includes Carnival (reached via unpatched Citrix ADC and Palo Alto devices), Accenture's research on initial-access sellers who feed ransomware groups, and Tyler Technologies, which paid after a RansomExx attack. Steve covers French arrests of cafe managers for not logging open-WiFi users and notes iOS 14 MAC randomization makes such logs useless. A five-person team earned about 288,500 dollars from Apple for 55 web-service vulnerabilities. Zerologon is now used by serious actors, including the group Microsoft tracks as CHIMBORAZO (TA505), and the FBI and CISA saw it used against state and local government networks. Steve then covers Global Privacy Control, a legally-backed successor to Do Not Track, and a Five Eyes call (now with Japan and India) for lawful access to encryption. The main topic explains well-known URIs under /.well-known/, including change-password and GPC.

# Key points

- Chrome 86: Safety Check on mobile; iOS touch-to-fill; mobile Enhanced Safe Browsing; warns when a secure page submits to an insecure URL; blocks executables and archives downloaded from secure pages; supports the well-known change-password URL; FTP phase-out starts; Native File System API is on by default behind a permission prompt.
- Carnival confirmed an August ransomware breach exposing customer, employee and crew data; Bad Packets found unpatched Citrix ADCs and Palo Alto Networks firewalls as plausible entry points.
- Accenture tracks 25+ network-access sellers who sell footholds for roughly 300 to 10,000 dollars on invite-only dark web forums to ransomware crews (Maze, LockBit, Avaddon, Exorcist, NetWalker, Sodinokibi and others), showing market specialization.
- Tyler Technologies (public-sector software) was hit by RansomExx, expected a month to recover, and paid the ransom; several school districts were also hit.
- Five Grenoble cafe and bar managers were arrested under a 2006 French law for not logging open WiFi users; penalties run up to a year in prison and large fines.
- Sam Curry's team spent about three months on Apple's web services, reporting 55 flaws (11 critical); Apple paid over 288,500 dollars for 28 so far and fixed issues within one to two business days. Steve argues big firms should run big, advertised bounties.
- Zerologon: Microsoft reported fake-update campaigns tied to TA505 infrastructure; FBI and CISA saw exploitation against state, local, tribal and territorial networks, some touching election support systems, with no evidence election data integrity was compromised. Initial access came via known flaws in Juniper, Pulse Secure, Citrix and Palo Alto products.
- Global Privacy Control sends a Sec-GPC header with value 1 only when the user has deliberately opted out of sale or sharing; backed by Georgetown Law, browsers like Brave and DuckDuckGo, publishers, and California officials aiming at CCPA enforceability; sites can publish /.well-known/gpc.
- Five Eyes plus Japan and India renewed calls for access to encrypted content. Steve distinguishes managed platforms (iMessage, FaceTime, Zoom, where the vendor holds keys) from unmanaged end-to-end tools like Threema or self-run VPNs, which cannot be opened without weakening encryption.
- Well-known URIs: robots.txt was the first; RFC 5785 (2010) was replaced by RFC 8615 (2019), reserving /.well-known/ in an IANA registry. The change-password entry should answer with a temporary redirect (302, 303 or 307) to the site's real password page, so password managers and browsers can jump straight to it.
