# Summary

The headline story is KeyTrap, a design flaw in DNSSEC found by German researchers: a single crafted DNS response with many colliding key tags and many signatures forces a validating resolver to perform hundreds of thousands of signature checks, pinning the CPU (BIND9 for up to 16 hours). It was disclosed confidentially to a 31-member industry group on 2 November 2023, patched by all major resolver vendors (Akamai first, Microsoft in February Patch Tuesday) by capping key collisions and validation failures, and made public in February 2024; the underlying design philosophy still needs revising. Before that, Steve covers Wyze, whose camera service briefly showed about 13,000 users' event thumbnails to other users after an AWS outage and a caching-library fault, and the February 2024 Patch Tuesday (73 flaws, two exploited zero-days in SmartScreen and Internet Shortcut handling, 9.8-rated Exchange and Outlook bugs). Listener topics include password length and character restrictions, Canada's Flipper Zero import ban and how better car-key protocols would work, why the early Internet lacked security, an information leak in Facebook's old-password message, TOTP versus passwords, a German ISP that left SNMP open with default credentials and silently changed customer DNS, and the security of email-link logins.

# Key points

- KeyTrap (DNSSEC): resolvers must try all colliding keys and all signatures ("eager validation", per Postel's law). Four attacks (SigJam, LockCram, KeySigTrap, plus a hash-based variant) reach a quadratic blow-up: 582 colliding keys times 340 signatures is about 198,000 validations. Roughly a two-million-fold CPU spike; BIND9 was worst (16-hour stall) due to an inefficient key re-parse loop, Unbound suffered from default re-queries. About 31 percent of web clients used validating resolvers (APNIC, December 2023).
- Mitigation: cap colliding keys (4), cryptographic failures (16) and total validations (8); this stops service loss but not the CPU spike, so the DNSSEC standard needs rethinking. Not known to have been exploited. Steve notes the 2008 Kaminsky issue was lesser in comparison.
- Wyze: AWS outage then cache/ID mapping error exposed thumbnails to other users; 13,000 affected, 1,504 viewed them; Wyze added extra verification. Steve will not stream interior camera video to a cloud; Leo mentions HomeKit-secure alternatives.
- Patch Tuesday (13 Feb 2024): 73 flaws (5 critical); exploited zero-days in Windows SmartScreen and Internet Shortcut files; Exchange privilege escalation and Outlook RCE rated 9.8 (NTLM hash relay risk); 15 RCEs in OLE DB provider for SQL Server.
- Password policy restrictions (length caps, banned symbols) are legacy from plaintext-storing mainframe days; Steve's gold standard is client-side hashing with clear requirement checklists.
- Canada bans import/sale/use of devices like Flipper Zero for car theft. Steve explains rolling-code relay attacks and a challenge-response key-fob design that would defeat them but needs two-way radios, so cannot be retrofitted.
- Internet security from the start: public-key crypto only arrived in 1976 and was export-controlled until the 1990s, so it was not feasible in the original design.
- Facebook's "you entered an old password" message leaks that the guess was a previously valid password, which helps attackers who test reused passwords; stating only when it was last changed is safer.
- Email-link login also eliminates password sharing; its strength equals email security, which is the linchpin anyway because of "forgot password" flows. Roughly half of mail is TLS-encrypted in transit (Gmail 98-99 percent), not at rest.
- TOTP is not just a second password: it proves knowledge of a secret without revealing it, expires after 30 seconds and is single-use.
- ISP router case (listener from Guernsey): an ISP left SNMP open on the WAN with default credentials so a script could enable remote web management; customers' routers had their DNS servers redirected to unknown servers in the Netherlands. Fix: restore or change DNS (Quad9), disable SNMP, reset passwords, replace ISP routers. Steve warns SNMP is dangerous when exposed.
- Tips: pfSense has Tailscale integration with exit-node support; DuckDuckGo Email Protection integrates with Bitwarden for unique addresses.
