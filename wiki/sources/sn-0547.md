# Summary

The show begins with Steve explaining that GRC.com was being knocked offline by a denial-of-service attack. Level 3, his provider, cut his connection because the flood threatened other customers; Steve identified it as a DNS reflection attack in which spoofed queries make DNS servers send unrequested replies to GRC, peaking at roughly 13 gigabits per second. He explained why such attacks are cheap, why ISP egress filtering would stop spoofing but never gained traction, and why mitigation services are costly and fit poorly with GRC's raw-packet services. The week's other big story was a stack buffer overflow in the glibc getaddrinfo resolver code, present since glibc 2.9 in 2008, found independently by Google and Red Hat engineers and exploitable through crafted oversized DNS replies by a network attacker or malicious DNS server. He followed up on Apple's Error 53 with listener feedback from a repair professional and corrected the Windows 10 telemetry story. He also covered Bruce Schneier's survey showing hundreds of foreign encryption products, James Clapper's remarks about the Internet of Things as a surveillance resource, five-dimensional quartz data storage, and a new electromagnetic side-channel attack that extracted elliptic-curve decryption keys from a laptop through a wall.

# Key points

- GRC DDoS: attacks began on a Saturday, intermittent at first; a DNS reflection flood of about 13 Gb/s led Level 3 to null-route the connection. Steve notes his service has a 10 Mb committed rate, that anyone with a Linux host and spoofing capability can launch this, and that no attribution is possible.
- Defence at the source: networks that block outbound packets with spoofed source addresses (egress or reverse-path filtering) would stop reflection attacks, but adoption is poor. Typical mitigation services proxy HTTP, which conflicts with GRC's packet-level tools such as ShieldsUP!.
- glibc getaddrinfo flaw: parallel A and AAAA lookups can overflow a stack buffer; replies over about 2 KB are needed; potentially remote code execution on Linux systems, appliances and IoT devices using glibc, with Google confirming a working exploit and a patch from the maintainers. Mac (BSD libc) not affected.
- Interim mitigation idea: drop unusually large UDP DNS responses upstream.
- Error 53 feedback: a South African repair technician argues the secure enclave could simply be wiped instead of bricking the phone; the iPhone 5s did not brick, the iPhone 6 does; iFixit's view is that it was likely unintended.
- Windows 10 telemetry claim retracted: the test blocked connections at the router so Windows kept retrying, and the busiest endpoint was a Teredo server; Steve apologised for not catching this and again recommended Microsoft's official method to disable the Windows 10 upgrade offer.
- Schneier survey: 619 entities sell encryption products, about two-thirds outside the U.S., 865 products from 55 countries, undermining the effectiveness of any U.S. backdoor mandate.
- U.S. intelligence chief James Clapper told the Senate that IoT devices might be used for identification, surveillance, tracking and recruitment, a further reason to isolate IoT networks.
- University of Southampton 5D optical storage in quartz: about 360 TB per disk, extremely long lifetime.
- Side-channel attack on GnuPG's libgcrypt 1.6.3 ECDH implementation: a measurement of electromagnetic emissions from an adjacent room recovered keys within seconds to minutes; libgcrypt 1.6.5 changed the point multiplication so that operations do not depend on secret bits. Earlier work used cable shields or acoustics. Steve says crypto code should have no secret-dependent execution paths.
- SpinRite: file-system metadata sectors that cannot be read make files appear to vanish; SpinRite can recover; all owners will get 6.1 free and SpinRite 7 is planned to understand file systems.
