# Summary

Steve opens with a Homeland Security memo, forwarded to Bruce Schneier, telling employees not to click a Washington Post link to a leaked NSA slide because their workstations would supposedly become classified. The main news item is a CNET report that the US government has been demanding the master SSL keys of Internet companies; Microsoft and Google say they have not handed theirs over, and Steve explains that this fits his theory that traffic is being tapped and stored for later decryption. He explains why perfect forward secrecy (ephemeral Diffie-Hellman) defeats that approach, but notes both ends must support it and that a man-in-the-middle could strip those ciphers from the offer (a downgrade). Other news: Apple's developer site was still offline a week after an intrusion, researchers announced a remote SIM card attack tied to old DES-based over-the-air updates ahead of Black Hat, UK Prime Minister Cameron announced default-on ISP pornography filters, Google was reportedly testing encryption of Drive files, and a Bit9 study found most organizations still run vulnerable Java 6. The second half, titled for the "inflection point" after the Snowden leaks, reviews renewed FISA court metadata orders, lawsuits, a New Jersey ruling requiring warrants for phone tracking, and Schneier's argument that the secret FISA court has broken the public-law link in surveillance. Steve proposes data escrow with warrant-based release as a compromise and promises coverage of PGP and trust-no-one encryption.

# Key points

- A DHS advisory told staff not to click a Washington Post link to a leaked slide, claiming it would raise their computer's classification level; Schneier called it idiotic since the bad guys already have a public document.
- CNET reported the FBI/NSA have sought web firms' SSL master keys; having the keys would let agents decrypt wiretapped or FISA-collected traffic, including previously stored traffic. Microsoft and Google denied handing over keys; others declined to comment. A source said smaller companies were being pressured most.
- Perfect forward secrecy with ephemeral Diffie-Hellman means a stolen server key cannot decrypt past sessions, and Google already offered it to modern browsers. It needs both client and server support, and a downgrade attack can remove those ciphers from the client's list.
- Apple's developer website had been down for about a week after an attempted data theft; Apple said sensitive data was encrypted but names, addresses or emails might have been exposed, and it was rebuilding the whole system.
- Karsten Nohl's Security Research Labs found a way to remotely break SIM cards that still use 56-bit DES for over-the-air updates, affecting hundreds of millions of cards; details were due at Black Hat after disclosure to carriers. Steve doubts plain brute force is the whole story and explains 3DES.
- UK Prime Minister Cameron announced that ISPs would apply "family-friendly" adult-content filters by default to new customers, and ask existing ones to choose; Steve points out that fuzzy definitions of what is blocked and the Internet's design of routing around filtering make this fragile, and that over-blocking has hit things like breast cancer research.
- One UK ISP's filter system reportedly harvested every URL its customers visited.
- Google was reportedly testing encryption of Google Drive files; Steve notes that if the provider holds no keys it cannot decrypt for authorities, and that such client-side encryption is fully feasible.
- A Bit9 study found 82 percent of organizations running Java 6 and fewer than 1 percent on the current Java 7 update; old versions stay installed because Java's updater does not remove them.
- iPGMail, a two-dollar iOS app for PGP email, was mentioned (not vetted) as a pointer to a planned PGP deep dive.
- Government renewed the bulk telephone metadata order from the FISA court; the government relies on the third-party doctrine, while the ACLU sued on First and Fourth Amendment grounds.
- The New Jersey Supreme Court unanimously held that police need a warrant to obtain cell phone location data; Montana passed a similar law, and a House amendment to curb Section 215 collection was later defeated 217 to 205.
- Schneier's analysis: the FISA court's secret rulings (for example broad readings of "relevant") mean the law itself is now secret, breaking the public-law foundation of surveillance oversight.
- Steve's proposal: hold the collected data in escrow with an independent party and release only specific records on a warrant, rather than letting the agency have limitless secret access.

# Other segments

- Leo's TWiT studio second anniversary and plans for a Premiere-based editing setup.
- NFC Ring Kickstarter: a passive ring with a public and a private NFC tag, described as a neat idea.
- Sci-fi: free Kindle book (first Honor Harrington book), Alastair Reynolds' House of Suns, and Peter F. Hamilton's Dreaming Void trilogy.
