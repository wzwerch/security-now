# Summary

Steve Gibson and Leo Laporte begin with Steve's newly shaved moustache and then cover the Marcus Hutchins legal defense fund, which was refunded in full after most donations turned out to be made with stolen cards. A Vault 7 document set called ExpressLane describes a CIA trojan disguised as a software update for a biometric collection system shared with partners; Leo cautions about trusting WikiLeaks. Zerodium raised its prices, including $500,000 for secure-messaging app exploits. Researcher Patrick Wardle highlighted the continuing use of Apple's deprecated AuthorizationExecuteWithPrivileges API. Positive Technologies found a "High-Assurance Platform" flag that turns off Intel's Management Engine after boot. A journalist's SIM was hijacked at T-Mobile. Steve recommends alternatives after CrashPlan's consumer shutdown and ends with the research paper "Shattering Trust" on malicious replacement phone touchscreens.

# Key points

- Hutchins fund: attorney Tor Ekeland found roughly $150,000 in fraudulent donations and only about $4,900 legitimate, so everything was refunded; Hutchins is staying in the US awaiting an October trial. Brian Krebs warns of Hurricane Harvey scam charities. Steve notes high-profile sites with weak abuse protections get overwhelmed (as with the FCC comment system) and wants safe building blocks such as libsodium and standard password handling.
- ExpressLane (Vault 7): CIA technical staff would install a fake upgrade with a Windows-style splash screen to collect biometric data held by partner agencies (named as NSA, DHS and FBI), via USB stick; the biometric system came from CrossMatch. Authenticity of WikiLeaks material is uncertain.
- Zerodium's new schedule: up to $500,000 for exploits in secure messaging apps (WhatsApp, Signal, iMessage and others) and default mobile email, $300,000 for Windows 10 remote exploits of SMB and RDP, $150,000 for baseband and Apache/IIS web server bugs, and 1.5 million for an iOS remote jailbreak; Steve calls the market an unstable consequence of crypto-versus-government tension.
- macOS: AuthorizationExecuteWithPrivileges, deprecated in 2013, is still used by many installers (Slack, Chrome and others) and does not verify what it runs, letting local malware ride on the user's password prompt; the secure replacement (SMJobBless) is complicated and needs a paid developer certificate. Wardle also reported Apple's installer loading unsigned libraries.
- Intel Management Engine: Positive Technologies decompressed the Huffman-coded firmware and found a "HAP" bit tied to the NSA's High-Assurance Platform program that halts the ME after boot; Intel said it was not an officially supported configuration; applying it needs hardware flashing.
- SIM swap: TechCrunch writer John Biggs lost control of his number at T-Mobile, which let attackers reset accounts and message friends asking for bitcoin; moral is to use TOTP apps rather than SMS and to protect carrier accounts.
- Authenticator Plus (and Authy) offered as cross-device TOTP apps; Steve still prefers offline printed QR codes.
- CrashPlan for Home ends in October 2018; Carbonite is the turnkey alternative, while Steve prefers CloudBerry with a one-time license and his own choice of storage provider.
- Errata: the correct term for compare-and-swap is atomic, not monotonic; a listener tallied about 908 hours of Security Now content (Leo says 968).
- SpinRite on USB thumb drives is sensible; SpinRite runs in a tiny VM; SpinRite 7 would drop DOS.
- Voice authentication should use challenge-response with unique phrases, not a fixed passphrase.
- "Shattering Trust" paper: attackers can build a malicious replacement phone touchscreen (shown with Synaptics and Atmel controllers) that injects touches and exploits driver overflows to compromise a stock Android phone; Google rated its driver issue critical. Replacement components should sit outside the trust boundary, like Khan's approach in Star Trek II.
