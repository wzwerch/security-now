# Summary

Steve scraps his planned Google QUIC topic because the week's news is too bleak, and titles the show after Sisyphus. He first covers a small Firefox 27.0.1 fix, the Kickstarter breach (usernames, emails, addresses, phone numbers and hashed passwords taken, no card data; older passwords salted and iterated SHA-1, newer ones bcrypt), and Google's acqui-hire of the three-person SlickLogin audio-authentication startup. The big story is CloudFlare's report of a roughly 400 Gb/s attack built on NTP monlist reflection, using about 4,500 servers on about 1,300 networks, larger than the 2013 Spamhaus attack that needed 31,000 open DNS resolvers; Steve explains source-address spoofing, egress filtering and the one-line fix. Then come three consumer-device failures: the "Moon" worm spreading across Linksys E-series routers through a remote-management web service flaw, Belkin WeMo home-automation devices that leak their firmware signing key, skip SSL certificate validation and expose a remote path into home networks, and ASUS routers whose USB storage and AiCloud service were exposed to the internet after months of ignored reports. Further items cover Google blocking password-protected ZIP files that use the password "infected", a CryptoLocker variant demanding five bitcoin, the Bitcoin transaction-malleability weakness that hurt Mt. Gox, and research showing many mobile banking apps and merchant libraries accept self-signed certificates. Steve's mood is that the industry is losing ground because vendors rush internet-connected "things" to market with security as an afterthought.

# Key points

- Firefox 27.0.1 was a rare minor release fixing small regressions in 27, which also added SPDY 3.1; Google's UDP-based QUIC protocol is postponed.
- Kickstarter breach: law enforcement alerted the company; exposed data included names, emails, addresses, phone numbers and salted, hashed passwords; Steve praises its use of per-account salts, iterated SHA-1 and later bcrypt, but still changed his password.
- Google bought SlickLogin, which sent inaudible sound from a computer to a phone for login; Steve doubts the approach and notes that SQRL needs only two parties, unlike schemes where a third party sits in the middle.
- Record DDoS: CloudFlare mitigated an attack near 400 Gb/s from NTP monlist reflection, with an amplification factor of several hundred; each server sent about 87 Mb/s at the victim, and intermediate routers several hops away were also overloaded.
- Mitigations: disable the monlist command in the NTP configuration, remove raw-socket abuse, and have ISPs apply egress filtering that drops packets with spoofed source addresses, which would stop all reflection attacks. US-CERT lists other abusable UDP services such as DNS, SNMP, SSDP and CharGEN.
- Linksys "Moon" worm (discovered by wireless ISP operator Brett Glass, analyzed by SANS): exploits URL parsing in the router's web service on port 8080, bypasses authentication, downloads a program that scans for more routers, floods outbound traffic, lives only in RAM, so a reboot clears it but reinfection is possible. Linksys said remote management was off by default; Steve's advice is to disable remote administration, and Leo recommends third-party firmware such as DD-WRT.
- Belkin WeMo (IOActive research, CERT advisory, Belkin unresponsive): signed firmware where the signing key and password are in the firmware itself, no SSL certificate validation, a STUN/TURN-based "darknet" allowing device-to-device control with a guessable secret, and XML injection in the cloud API. Impact includes remote control of devices, malicious firmware pushes, occupancy monitoring and a foothold on the home network. IOActive advised unplugging devices.
- Advice for Internet-of-Things gadgets: isolate them on a guest network or a separate router; small low-cost devices skip proper certificate checks because of limited storage and processing.
- ASUS routers: default anonymous FTP over the WAN, AiCloud credentials readable without login, a researcher's reports from the prior year ignored; people found a WARNING_YOU_ARE_VULNERABLE text file on their drives left by a vigilante. Mitigation: turn off AiCloud, FTP, Samba and UPnP and change default credentials.
- Iran-linked intrusion into the Navy Marine Corps network, reportedly present since November, mentioned by Leo as an advanced persistent threat example.
- Google started rejecting password-protected ZIPs encrypted with the conventional malware-sharing password "infected", and remembered the file name even after re-encryption, to the surprise of researcher Brian Baskin.
- CryptoLocker demand rose to five bitcoin (about 3,000 dollars at the time), probably too high for many individuals.
- Bitcoin transaction malleability: an attacker can alter a transaction's identifier so a duplicate appears; Mt. Gox's auditing mistake let this be abused to obtain refunds. Users who wait about ten minutes for confirmation are safe; the community wants the protocol clarified.
- Netcraft found dozens of rogue self-signed certificates impersonating big sites; Stanford and UT Austin researchers found broken certificate validation in Amazon EC2 and PayPal merchant libraries, shopping carts and AdMob; about 40 percent of tested iOS banking apps and 41 percent of Android apps failed to validate certificates, enabling man-in-the-middle interception.

# Other segments

- SpinRite: a listener tweeted that SpinRite 6 brought his files back.
- SQRL: the user-interface pages are now public (about 20 pages), designed for non-technical users.
- Movie chatter: Ender's Game, RoboCop, the film The Moon and the upcoming Transcendence; Steve's frustration that his Security Now notes links route through Google Docs.
