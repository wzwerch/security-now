# Summary

Steve covers a very large February Patch Tuesday (55 fixes, including 41 for Internet Explorer), two Adobe Flash zero-day updates in a week, and the US announcement of a Cyber Threat Intelligence Integration Center to pool information among NSA, DHS, FBI and CIA. He details the Anthem breach: first malicious access on 10 December, detection on 27 January, an unencrypted database of about 80 million records with names, Social Security numbers, birth dates and some incomes, a custom backdoor and a compromised administrator account; phishing followed. Chrome 40 adds HTTP/2, with SPDY to be removed in 2016. A 60 Minutes demo showed a car's brakes being disabled remotely, and Senator Markey's survey found weak automaker security. Samsung's smart-TV privacy policy warning about spoken words sparks a debate about voice assistants, with Leo favoring disclosure plus opt-out. GnuPG's developer Werner Koch received funding from Facebook, Stripe and the Linux Foundation after a ProPublica story. Q&A covers Tor's unfixable traffic-analysis weakness, how to verify emailed links (you cannot reliably, so do not click), a modem's private management address reachable through a router, CloudFlare's "Flexible SSL," a safeguard that stops attackers injecting HSTS headers over HTTP, disabling Flash in Chrome, and client certificates.

# Key points

- Patch Tuesday: nine bundles with 55 fixes, three critical, including a bulletin addressing one public and 40 privately reported Internet Explorer flaws, a TrueType kernel font bug and a TIFF flaw; some systems needed multiple reboots.
- Adobe shipped two out-of-cycle Flash zero-day fixes within about a week; Chrome and IE update Flash themselves.
- The new Cyber Threat Intelligence Integration Center is modeled on the National Counterterrorism Center and is expected to work out of public sight, unlike the DHS CERT.
- Anthem: attackers were inside from December; Anthem's own monitoring found them and Mandiant helped; data was not encrypted at rest (the company said encryption would not have helped since an admin account was used, which Steve calls inadequate); the stolen data enables identity theft because Social Security numbers cannot be changed easily.
- Chrome 40 adds HTTP/2, which grew out of SPDY; Chrome will drop SPDY in 2016, and servers must add HTTP/2 support.
- Car hacking: a 60 Minutes segment showed remote takeover of displays, wipers and brakes; Steve is alarmed that a computer can override braking; Senator Markey's report said many carmakers lack security measures.
- Samsung smart TVs may send voice commands, with device identifiers, to a third-party speech service; Leo argues this is normal for Siri, Cortana and the new Amazon Echo (Alexa) and wants disclosure plus opt-out and trigger phrases.
- GnuPG creator Werner Koch was near quitting; after a ProPublica story he met a 137,000 dollar goal in a day, Facebook and Stripe pledged 50,000 dollars a year each, and the Linux Foundation's Core Infrastructure Initiative gave 60,000 dollars.
- A Chrome security team slide deck on communicating HTTPS problems to users shows how hard it is to convey nuances like the SHA-1 warning.
- Tor cannot be fixed by adding delay, padding or nodes: the Internet resists anonymity, a nation-state observing enough of the network can correlate traffic, and Tor is best treated as an experiment and one layer; node operators objected on Twitter.
- Emailed links: the visible text may differ from the real destination, scripts may run on click, and hovering shows only the static link; safest is to type the address yourself and never act on prompts you did not seek (such as Flash update pop-ups).
- A cable modem's LAN-side management address (for example 192.168.100.1) is reachable through a router because it is not in the router's own network; useful for diagnostics but a risk if modems have no authentication.
- CloudFlare's Flexible SSL encrypts only between visitor and CloudFlare, not to the origin server; Steve prefers a real free certificate (StartSSL or similar) at the origin.
- HSTS safeguard: browsers only accept an HSTS header delivered over a fully valid HTTPS connection, so a man in the middle cannot inject it over plain HTTP.
- Flash cannot be disabled globally in Chrome; each user must turn it off at chrome://plugins, or launch Chrome with a switch.
- Client certificates exist but rarely used; most TLS authenticates only the server.

# Other segments

- GRC's longest-repeating-string algorithm (used to detect duplicate SpinRite testimonials) is documented under GRC.com/dev/lrs; the Portable Sound Blaster dog-deterrent project is finished and archived in a Google Group.
- Steve published his DigiCert SQRL presentation slides, and answers a question about using SQRL for VPN or remote logins; he guest-appeared on Coding 101 talking about assembly.
- Steve is enjoying "Leviathan Wakes" (The Expanse); two listeners' tweets praise SpinRite.
- Passive-aggressive WiFi network names as image of the week.
