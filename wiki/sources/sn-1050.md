# Summary

The show opens with Oslo transport agency Ruter finding, in a Faraday-cage test, that Chinese Yutong electric buses can be disabled remotely through undocumented cellular modems; Ruter pulled the SIM cards. Edge 142 and Chrome 142 both add LLM or vision-model "scareware" blockers, and Steve uses a CTV News story about an elderly Ontario couple who lost over one million Canadian dollars to a pop-up tech-support scam to show why such protection may help the general public, even though he turns it off for himself. OpenAI's Aardvark, an agentic GPT-5 code-vulnerability scanner in private beta, is discussed alongside Google's Big Sleep and the burden such tools place on volunteer projects like FFmpeg. A run of quick items covers Italian age verification for 48 sites, Russia's plan to mandate Russian-only software and throttle Telegram and WhatsApp, 187 new malicious npm packages, Australian BadCandy infections on unpatched Cisco IOS XE devices, GitHub's 2025 Octoverse (TypeScript overtakes Python) and Windows 11's new Administrator Protection. After listener feedback on NIST passwords, DNS poisoning, Teams Wi-Fi tracking and client-side hashing, the main topic is AI browsers (ChatGPT Atlas, Copilot Mode, Comet, Dia and others). Steve cites The Verge's warning and Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta" (private data, untrusted content, external communication) and concludes the technology is inevitable but will bring a rough patch because prompt injection has no reliable fix.

# Key points

- Yutong electric buses (about 300 in Oslo, 550 elsewhere in Norway) have undocumented remote-control and disable capability via diagnostics and battery/power systems; Dutch VDL buses tested did not. Part of a wider pattern (cranes, cars, solar inverters) of hidden remote access in Chinese equipment.
- Edge 142 Scareware Blocker uses a local computer-vision model (needs more than 2 GB RAM and 4 cores) and is on by default; a separate "scareware sensor" that reports suspected scams to SmartScreen is off by default and never active in InPrivate. Microsoft says each user report protected about 50 others on average. Chrome 142 added a comparable LLM-based scam detector. Steve supports it for non-expert users but disables it himself (privacy and battery cost).
- Scam case: a couple in their 70s were groomed for five months after a fake-virus pop-up and gave scammers 900,000 CAD in gold and about 102,000 CAD in bitcoin despite bank warnings. Lesson: legitimate agencies never ask for gold bars or bitcoin-machine deposits.
- OpenAI Aardvark: analyses a repository to build a threat model, scans commits, validates findings by triggering them in a sandbox, and proposes Codex patches; 92% recall on benchmark repositories, 10 CVEs found in open source, pro-bono scanning for select non-commercial projects. OpenAI cites 1.2% of commits introducing bugs. Leo notes FFmpeg maintainers' complaints about Google's Big Sleep reports without fixes.
- Steve and Leo expect AI bug-finding and test-writing to help defenders but note current compute costs make free package-registry scanning unaffordable today.
- Italy's AGCOM lists 48 adult sites that must stop accepting self-declared age from 12 November 2025.
- Russia is considering a law forcing commercial companies to replace foreign software with Russian software (state bodies by 2028) and telecoms are blocking SMS and call verification for Telegram and WhatsApp to push the state messenger MAX.
- 187 more malicious npm packages found and removed in one week.
- BadCandy: a non-persistent Lua web-shell implant on Cisco IOS XE devices exploiting CVE-2023-20198; Australian Signals Directorate assessed more than 400 devices compromised since July 2025 and over 150 still compromised in October. Advice: patch, and restrict the web UI. Steve criticises Cisco for not hardening by default; Leo relays Alex Stamos's advice to scan yourself with Shodan or Nmap.
- GitHub 2025 report: more than 36 million new developers, 180 million total, about 1 billion commits, TypeScript overtook Python and JavaScript in August 2025 as most-used language; AI use is standard (80% of new developers use Copilot in week one). Anders Hejlsberg is porting the TypeScript compiler to Go for roughly 10x speed.
- Windows 11 24H2/25H2 preview update KB5067036 introduces Administrator Protection, off by default: just-in-time admin tokens from a hidden profile-separated account, requiring Windows Hello re-authentication, destroyed after use. Microsoft cites about 39,000 token-theft incidents per day.
- Listener feedback: min-password-age and rotation anecdotes (the company later dropped rotation and checks for compromised passwords instead); a listener correction on how a remote attacker learns a resolver's port and query ID (induce a query to an attacker-controlled nameserver); ransomware non-payment may reflect cold economics; Teams location indicator was already available in logs; password length beats complexity (Password Haystacks, SN 303); client-side hashing still needs server-side re-hashing, and password managers do both; honeypots must be convincing; enabling guest Wi-Fi isolation for IoT after SN 1049.
- AI browsers: ChatGPT Atlas, Copilot Mode in Edge, Gemini in Chrome, Opera Neon, Dia, Perplexity Comet and others. Researchers found Atlas memory injection and Comet hidden-instruction hijacking; OpenAI and Perplexity call prompt injection a frontier problem. Agents are gullible, attackers can retry endlessly, and browser memory builds invasive profiles. Vivaldi has declined AI features. All are Chromium-based; Ladybird is a from-scratch alternative aiming for beta in 2026.
- Simon Willison (coined "prompt injection" in 2022) describes the lethal trifecta: private data plus untrusted content plus an external communication channel lets an attacker exfiltrate data via an LLM; he says guardrails claiming 95% detection are a failing grade and users must avoid the trifecta themselves. Steve doubts LLMs can be constrained.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a "Speed Limit 25 Unless Otherwise Posted" sign immediately before a 45 sign.
- Banter on manual clocks, Wi-Fi clocks needing network segmentation, and Leo asking ChatGPT to draw a picture of himself and Mikah Sargent.
- A truck-driver listener at episode 75 of the archive asks whether 63 plus signs would be a strong password; Steve answers with length and the Password Haystacks idea.
- Steve receives about 100 listener emails per week and cannot air them all.
