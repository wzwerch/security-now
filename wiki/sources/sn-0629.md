# Summary

Steve is joined by Father Robert Ballecer (Padre) and first explains the cause of the recent audio glitches: an ageing cable plant at his house combined with an underpowered Windows 7 machine driving a new USB audio interface. The Equifax story continues with advice on fraud alerts versus credit freezes and the revelation that the company's chief security officer had no security background, followed by a look at claims of default credentials. The EFF resigned from the W3C because the Encrypted Media Extensions standard was advanced without protection for security researchers. A new Payment Request API gets praise as a clean, user-approved way for sites to ask browsers for payment details. Steve argues that SMS-based account recovery is not a second factor and is exposed through SS7 interception. The CCleaner distribution breach, the BlueBorne Bluetooth vulnerabilities and Google's 60-day purge of unused Android backups are covered. The main topic is Apple's Intelligent Tracking Prevention in iOS 11, which Steve calls a brilliant, automatic fix for cross-site cookie tracking and which advertisers condemned.

# Key points

- Audio trouble resolved: two overlapping faults (coax/modem signal problem and a too-slow PC for the USB interface), a lesson in layered troubleshooting.
- Equifax protections: fraud alert (90 days, extendable; also an extended 7-year alert for victims) versus credit freeze (whitelisting per lender, state-dependent fees and duration). Freezes suit people who rarely need credit; alerts rely on lenders calling the contact number.
- Equifax's CSO reportedly held a music degree and had no security background; her online presence was scrubbed after the breach. Unverified rumours claimed an admin/admin control panel.
- EFF resignation from the W3C: it wanted a covenant barring members from using DRM laws (DMCA, EU equivalents) against researchers and modifications; the W3C did not adopt it. Steve notes DRM in a browser is inherently breakable but argues researchers must be protected; he prefers one well-built browser DRM over many plugins.
- Payment Request API: sites request specific payment data, the browser (not an add-on) holds the encrypted data and asks the user to approve; no new attack surface beyond existing browser password storage, and it avoids typing card numbers that keyloggers could capture.
- SMS second factor: used for account recovery it becomes an alternative first factor ("or" rather than "and"); SS7 has no endpoint authentication, so messages can be intercepted by cheap rogue-base-station hardware. Recommendation is time-based one-time-password apps; Google's in-app prompt is considered safe since it uses TLS over the internet.
- CCleaner breach: for about a month from mid-August the 32-bit v5.33 download (and the Cloud version) carried Floxif malware on the vendor's distribution server; about 2.27 million downloads; fixed in 5.34 on 12 Sept; found by Cisco Talos; the malware created an Agomo registry key under the Piriform software key and included a domain-generation scheme, but the command server was taken down. The legitimate installer signature stayed valid because the extra payload was appended.
- BlueBorne (Armis): multiple zero-click Bluetooth stack flaws affecting Android, Windows, Linux and older iOS, exploitable within range without pairing. Google, Microsoft and Linux patched; Apple was clean from iOS 10; Samsung was reportedly unresponsive; unpatchable devices such as car stereos and TVs remain at risk. Advice: turn Bluetooth off when unused.
- iOS extension "Inspect" shows a visited site's HTTPS certificate details.
- Google deletes Android device backups after 60 days of device inactivity.
- Intelligent Tracking Prevention (WebKit): on-device machine learning classifies domains that track cross-site (signals: unique subresource domains, subframe domains, redirect targets); cookies of classified domains are purged if the user has not visited them as a first party in 30 days, kept but partitioned if visited within 30 days but not 24 hours, and usable for sign-in flows within 24 hours of interaction. Six advertising groups published a protest letter; Steve notes ads still work but profiling is harder, and bouncing redirects are a possible workaround.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a SQRL mug with the "protect your nuts" joke; the SQRL protocol's nonce is called a "nut".
- SpinRite listener question: recovery from phones works only if the device exposes itself as a plain USB drive; SpinRite can rescue a dying drive a limited number of times.
