# Summary

Steve Gibson is joined by Father Robert Ballecer (standing in for Leo Laporte) for a news-heavy episode. It opens with an out-of-cycle Adobe Flash patch for a zero-day that FireEye found being used by the Chinese group APT3 in a phishing campaign against aerospace, defense and other sectors; Steve walks through how the exploit defeats ASLR and DEP and stresses that the real fix is not to run Flash at all. Next come Microsoft's routine root-certificate store update (which Steve considers a non-scandal) and a handy root-certificate auditing tool, Google quietly shipping an "Ok Google" hotword module in Chrome/Chromium, and Samsung bundling a utility that disables Windows Update. A research paper showing that GnuPG keys can be recovered from the electromagnetic emissions of a laptop (even with an AM radio) is covered, followed by the privacytools.io resource, a study of ISP throttling around content delivery networks, and a NoScript whitelist problem where an expired trusted domain could be re-registered. Listener-oriented tips (a deterministic password generator, Google Contributor), the leap second, and TV shows round out the news. The main topic is academic research on "Astoria," a Tor client modified to avoid circuits where one autonomous system sees both entry and exit; Steve concludes the privacy gain only pays off at a significant performance cost. The episode closes with a SpinRite explainer on partial sector recovery.

# Key points

- **Adobe Flash zero-day (FireEye, "Operation Clandestine Wolf"):** APT3 phished US aerospace, defense, engineering, telecom and transport targets; a flaw in FLV parsing plus vector corruption and return-oriented programming defeated ASLR and DEP. Fixed version 18.0.0.194; Steve advises not having Flash enabled at all (iOS never had it, browsers auto-update it, NoScript blocks it).
- Steve ties this style of attack to the likely mechanism of the OPM breach and notes complexity and legacy support as the root enemy of security.
- **Microsoft root store update:** outrage over "secret" new trusted roots is, in Steve's view, normal vendor behavior; he recommends the small RCC root-certificate auditing tool to spot unexpected roots such as Superfish or antivirus HTTPS-interception certificates.
- **Google "Ok Google" hotword in Chrome/Chromium:** a hidden binary NaCl module is downloaded and not listed among extensions; Google says it is opt-in but Debian packaging showed it active; criticism centers on transparency and "the tyranny of the default."
- **Samsung:** an OEM utility (disable_windowsupdate.exe) turned off Windows Update to avoid driver replacement problems, leaving customers unpatched; compared with Lenovo's Superfish.
- **EM side channel on GnuPG:** researchers extract keys within seconds from the electromagnetic leakage (around 1.7 MHz) by sending crafted ciphertexts; variants use an SDR in pita bread and finally a plain AM radio plugged into a phone.
- **privacytools.io:** crowd-driven curated list of privacy tools (VPNs with no logging in non-US jurisdictions, Firefox add-ons, about:config tweaks, encrypted email/messaging); Steve switched from Adblock Plus to uBlock Origin.
- **ISP throttling study (MeasurementLab):** AT&T customers got a fraction of a megabit from the GTT CDN versus about 21 Mb/s for Comcast customers, illustrating peering ratio disputes and the Net Neutrality debate.
- **NoScript:** researcher re-registered an expired domain on NoScript's default trust list; Giorgio Maone patched within days. Steve cleared his own whitelist and recommends periodically doing so.
- **Master Password app:** deterministic password generation from name, master password, counter and domain name.
- **Google Contributor:** pay-per-month to see no ads from participating sites; does not stop tracking.
- **Leap second (June 30, 2015):** historic problems in 2012; Google smears the second over the day; some stock and secure-network systems are shut down around it.
- **Tor and Astoria:** Tor has grown from about 33 to over 6,000 nodes, but up to 58% of default circuits are exposed to network-level attackers who see both ends; up to 43% of Alexa Top 500 site paths per country were unsafe and China was worst (85.7% circuits). Astoria uses an IP-to-ASN database, node trust over time and bandwidth awareness, but is only favorable in high-security settings because of the performance cost.

# Other segments

- TV: praise for the new series "Mr. Robot" and a second season of "Halt and Catch Fire."
- SpinRite: explanation of DynaStat and partial sector recovery (returning most bits of an unreadable sector helps databases, directories and imaging).
- SQRL: Steve mentions a breakthrough on one remaining problem, to be discussed later.
