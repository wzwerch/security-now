# Summary

Steve follows up on uBlock Origin: a surprise weekend mailing to about 10,400 list subscribers explained how to suppress the "Sign in with Google" prompts, and he now recommends enabling uBlock Origin's Annoyances and Social Widgets filter lists, which also remove cookie banners, help pop-ups and app banners. National Public Data's owner has filed for bankruptcy, which Steve uses to argue for bonding and insurance requirements for data aggregators. He debunks fears that the .io domain will vanish when the British Indian Ocean Territory is handed to Mauritius, summarizes October Patch Tuesday (118 flaws, two exploited), and reports Mozilla's emergency fix of a use-after-free in Firefox's animation timeline that was exploited against Tor Browser users. The main topic is BIMI (Brand Indicators for Message Identification): how SPF, DKIM and DMARC underpin it, how Verified and Common Mark Certificates work, and Steve's own rigorous identity-verification experience with DigiCert, which stalled because the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine, used to prove a year of logo use, was offline after a DDoS and breach.

# Key points

- Google sign-in pop-ups: custom filter (a network block on Google's sign-in iframe/gsi endpoints) works for many; the broader fix is enabling EasyList or AdGuard "Annoyances" lists in uBlock Origin; also enable Social Widgets lists. If a site breaks, disable the blocker for that site temporarily. Chrome users on Manifest V3 would lose independent list updating.
- Chrome has about 37 million uBlock Origin users versus Firefox's seven million; Brave and Arc say they will build blocking into the browser; Steve says he is happier than ever to use Firefox.
- Listener tip: uBlock Origin can also hide cluttered interface elements in web apps (for example AI buttons), a feature Steve realizes he under-uses.
- National Public Data (Jerico Pictures) filed for bankruptcy; filing admits hundreds of millions of people may be affected, facing over a dozen class actions plus FTC and state scrutiny, and insurance declined coverage; assets were listed as a handful of home computers and about $33,000.
- Steve proposes legislation requiring large data aggregators to post bonds and carry insurance so risk is privatized.
- The .io ccTLD belongs to the British Indian Ocean Territory; after the October 2024 handover to Mauritius, ISO may remove the "IO" code and IANA policy would retire the TLD, but Steve doubts that it will really disappear given its popularity.
- October 2024 Patch Tuesday: 118 vulnerabilities, two actively exploited, three critical.
- Firefox and Tor Browser: use-after-free in Animation timelines (CVSS 9.8) exploited in the wild, exploit sample sent by ESET; fixed in Firefox 131.0.2, ESR 128.3.1 and 115.16.1 within 25 hours, and Tor Browser 13.5.7.
- A Server edition of Windows is not a good desktop substitute: many application installers refuse it or charge more.
- Router advice follow-ups: Eero (remote management via app, Eero Plus filtering, low bufferbloat) and Peplink (recommended by Michael Horowitz of routersecurity.org).
- Teaser: the FIDO Alliance announced the Credential Exchange Protocol for passkey portability (Apple apparently absent); a claimed Chinese quantum break of RSA only factored a 22-bit number.
- BIMI: standard (BIMI Group since 2014) for showing an authenticated brand logo in recipients' inboxes; requires the sender's mail to pass DMARC (SPF plus DKIM) and a logo in the restricted SVG Tiny-PS format; Verified Mark Certificates (registered trademark) or Common Mark Certificates (a year of public logo use) are issued by DigiCert and Entrust; supported by Gmail, Apple, Yahoo, Fastmail and others; Google and Yahoo require DMARC for bulk senders.
- Steve's process: creating the Tiny-PS logo needed Adobe Illustrator and a script, then a phone call to GRC's listed number, a government photo ID upload, and a live video check with the ID held beside his face, plus a DNS proof; final step blocked pending Internet Archive availability.
- Internet Archive: DDoS from 8 October, a defacement through a compromised JavaScript library, and theft of a user database of 31 million accounts (emails, screen names, salted password hashes); services returned read-only and Wayback Machine holds about 916 billion pages; Steve and Leo urge donations.
