# Summary

Steve starts with follow-up on the Windows Wi-Fi flaw CVE-2024-30078: a public GitHub exploit attempt hit a different bug, but a forum seller offered a working exploit for about $5,000 and, more worryingly, described delivery through compromised routers or access points, so the "nearby attacker" limit is weak. Disabling Wi-Fi on an unpatched machine does protect it. He then covers the US Commerce Department's ban on Kaspersky (no new sales from 20 July, updates cut off from 29 September), saying he is of two minds, and the Kremlin's extension of its own ban on foreign IT services. He rejects the Austrian group noyb's complaint that Google's Privacy Sandbox is deceptive tracking, explains that GRC's new email contains a harmless first-party open-tracking pixel, relays security researcher Orange Tsai's request for help with a US visa to speak at Black Hat, and adds more thoughts on Recall including third-party leakage. The main story is a Wired report on researchers who recovered a forgotten 20-character RoboForm-generated password protecting 43.6 bitcoin by exploiting a pre-2015 generator that derived passwords directly from the one-second system clock. Steve's lesson is that password generators must have open, disclosed designs.

# Key points

- CVE-2024-30078 follow-up: public repo exploit initially targeted an oversized-SSID overflow, not the real bug in nwifi.sys (Dot11Translate80211ToEthernetNdisPacket); a $5,000 exploit sale was advertised, with routers or saved networks as vectors. Turning off Wi-Fi mitigates; corporate patch delays are risky this month.
- Kaspersky: Commerce Department ban on sales and updates to US customers, citing Russian government influence but giving no specific evidence; precedent is the 2017 DHS federal ban tied to the NSA-contractor tool incident. Steve notes antivirus runs in the kernel, so the trust issue is real, but also that Kaspersky has long helped global security. The Kremlin extended its ban on "unfriendly-country" IT services from 1 January.
- noyb filed a complaint with Austria's data protection authority claiming Chrome tricks users into enabling Privacy Sandbox; Steve says Sandbox explicitly avoids tracking and the complaint confuses tracking with profiling, while the rest of the ad industry resists the change.
- GRC email launched: first summary sent to 4,239 subscribers and then 4,330; pixel stats show half of readers use Gmail and a quarter Thunderbird. Steve sends mail himself (no third-party cloud) and explains why a pixel pointing only back to GRC is not third-party tracking.
- Orange Tsai (Pwn2Own champion, ProxyLogon/ProxyShell researcher) has been unable to enter the US since 2021 and asks readers with appropriate connections to help before Black Hat/DEFCON in early August, asking that appeals be personal and low-key.
- Recall: a listener points out third-party leakage (your correspondents' Recall records your email); Steve argues Microsoft would not exfiltrate data but may use Recall locally for ad relevance, and that Microsoft faces a harder task than Apple because Windows hardware is open.
- Errata: the show is in its 19th year, not the 20th; the XP firewall and raw-socket changes came with Service Pack 2, not SP3.
- RoboForm case: "Michael" generated a 20-character password in May 2013 for a wallet holding 43.6 BTC, lost it, and Joe Grand and Bruno reverse-engineered RoboForm 7.x, finding the generator used the Unix time in seconds with no per-install salt, so passwords were reproducible by time-travelling the clock. After searching date ranges and parameter sets they recovered it (generated 15 May 2013). The generator was changed in v7.9.14 (June 2015) without disclosure of how or any customer notice.
- Steve's conclusion: he cannot recommend RoboForm; password generators must be open-design (Bitwarden and 1Password are cited), and unpredictability (entropy collection, hardware noise sources such as reverse-biased diodes) is the core requirement of cryptographic randomness.
