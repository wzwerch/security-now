# Summary

Steve opens with privacy fallout: Facebook told shareholders it expects an FTC fine of $3 to $5 billion tied to the 2011 consent decree and Cambridge Analytica, and a congressional committee sent Google ten questions about Sensorvault, its long-retained precise location database. He then looks at Windows 10 feature-update uptake (the troubled 1809 release has stalled at about a third of machines), reports that Lawrence Abrams traced April's antivirus conflicts to the CSRSS elevation-of-privilege fix (CVE-2019-0735), and notes the May 2019 update blocks installs when a USB or SD device is attached. Other stories: NIST's improved combinatorial testing (fuzzing) toolkit, an unattributed open database describing 80 million US households, United Airlines seatback cameras, a tip to disable web notifications in Firefox and Chrome, and Steve's Verizon femtocell. The main topic is a Recorded Future report on the economy of credential stuffing, showing how automated dark-web shops, checker software, leaked credential lists and rented proxy botnets make mass login guessing highly profitable.

# Key points

- Facebook: recorded a $3 billion accrual (range $3 to $5 billion) for the FTC inquiry into its user data practices, which examines whether it violated a 2011 consent decree; the CFO warned of ad-targeting headwinds; Zuckerberg wants a "privacy-focused social network". The Russian fine was only about $47.
- Sensorvault: House Energy and Commerce committee letter to Google asks what is stored, who has access, retention back to 2009, opt-out behaviour, accuracy and third-party sharing, with a May 7 response deadline.
- Windows 10 adoption (AdDuplex data): earlier feature updates climbed quickly, but 1809 stalled after its withdrawal; Steve suspects enterprises may skip it.
- April 9 update conflict: CVE-2019-0735, a Windows CSRSS elevation-of-privilege fix, was identified as what broke Avast, Avira, McAfee and Sophos enterprise products; AV vendors must hook deep into Windows and break when Microsoft changes internals.
- NIST Combinatorial Coverage Measurement within ACTS: with an algorithm from Austria's SBA Research it now handles up to 2,000 variables for five-way input combinations, useful for aircraft, cars and power plants.
- vpnMentor found an open cloud-hosted database (24 GB) with names, ages, income bands, addresses, homeowner status and dwelling type for about 80 million US households (no one under 40), but could not identify the owner and asked the public for help. Other recent leaks: rehab patients, Facebook records on public S3 buckets, legal documents on Elasticsearch.
- Windows 10 1903 Insider upgrades fail with "This PC can't be upgraded" when a USB drive or SD card is attached because drive letters may be reassigned.
- United Airlines seatback displays with embedded cameras caused alarm; the airline said they were for future features. Steve advises physical shutters over indicator lights.
- Steve's tip: set `dom.webnotifications.enabled` to false in Firefox's about:config, or switch off "ask before sending" under Chrome's notification site settings, to stop notification prompts.
- Credential stuffing economy (Recorded Future): automated shop marketplaces emerged around late 2014; checkers such as Storm, Black Bullet and Sentry MBA run credential lists through proxy networks with plug-in configs; a typical 1 to 3% hit rate on 100,000 records, accounts selling for roughly $0.50 to $3.50 each, and an example investment of about $550 yielding roughly $20,000. Supply across big shops is in the tens of millions of accounts. CAPTCHAs are defeated by the tools, so multifactor authentication is the real defence, but sites often prefer convenience.

# Other segments

- Picture of the week: pie chart of Windows 10 version distribution.
- Steve bought a Verizon femtocell for his poor-coverage neighbourhood; Leo suggests carriers often provide them for free.
- SQRL Windows client is at release candidate 3; the new EV code-signing certificate stopped triggering Defender and SmartScreen after Microsoft reviewed it.
