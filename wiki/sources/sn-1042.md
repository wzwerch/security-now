# Summary

Steve starts by explaining why email now works better than X for listener interaction (his weekly mailing reaches about 18,465 subscribers with no bounces). News follows: the "Scattered LapSus Hunters" coalition demands that Google fire two Threat Intelligence Group researchers; the Croatian CA Fina issued 12 unauthorized test certificates for 1.1.1.1, trusted by Microsoft's root program but never by Chrome, Mozilla or Apple; ransomware group LunaLock threatens to feed stolen artwork to AI trainers; Mozilla extends Firefox ESR 115 security updates for Windows 7/8/8.1 to March 2026; and the US Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 is due to lapse on 30 September unless renewed (the WIMWIG Act would extend it ten years). Trend Micro tested whether vibe coding turns published threat reports into working malware and concludes publication must continue but attribution must move beyond matching techniques. Steve notes the UK versus Apple encryption dispute is still alive per a Financial Times report. The main story is the 8 September 2025 npm attack in which a phished maintainer's account was used to publish malicious versions of 18 hugely popular packages (over two billion weekly downloads) to hijack cryptocurrency transactions in browsers. After a note about why BYTE magazine died, the final segment covers Google's planned "disruption unit" and proposals to grant private firms "letters of marque" for offensive cyber operations, which Steve finds queasy and Leo thinks should remain a government-only function.

# Key points

- GRC's email system: the weekly podcast summary went to 18,465 recipients without bounces; Steve lost his paid X checkmark and says X now yields little valuable feedback. A listener reports being so absorbed by "Project Hail Mary" that he missed three episodes.
- Scattered LapSus Hunters (Scattered Spider, Lapsus$ and ShinyHunters alumni) posted a Telegram ultimatum asking Google to fire Austin Larsen and Charles Carmakal and stop investigating them, with no evidence of an actual Google breach; the Salesforce-related ShinyHunters data theft was disclosed by Google in August.
- Fina CA (Fina RDC 2020, under Fina Root CA) issued 12 certificates for 1.1.1.1 between February 2024 and August 2025, apparently for internal testing, signed by a key trusted by the Microsoft root program and an EU trust service list. Cloudflare says all were revoked and no abuse is known. Steve's theory: a technician used the easy-to-type "1.1.1.1" as a test name. He stresses that Certificate Transparency logs exist so owners can detect such issuance but are rarely monitored, and that any trusted CA can sign for any domain.
- LunaLock hit the Artists & Clients commission platform on 30 August, demanding $50,000 and threatening to leak data and submit artwork to AI training sets.
- Firefox ESR 115 will keep receiving security updates for Windows 7, 8, 8.1 and macOS 10.12-10.14 until March 2026; Steve returned from Brave to Firefox because Brave dropped Windows 7 support, and notes that his DNS Benchmark runs on Windows 95 through 11.
- CISA 2015 info-sharing law lapses 30 September; the House Homeland Security Committee advanced the WIMWIG Act (10-year extension) while some Republicans, including Senator Rand Paul, want language barring alleged CISA "censorship".
- Trend Micro experiment (Claude-4-Opus with an uncensored model route) recreated parts of the Earth Alux espionage toolkit from its own public report: output was a starting point needing expertise. Risks: copycat campaigns and muddied attribution that relies on tactics and indicators of compromise; recommendation is to keep publishing but attribute by intent, objectives and targeting. Steve also mentions OpenAI's research on why models hallucinate (training rewards guessing over "I don't know").
- UK vs Apple: the Financial Times, citing Investigatory Powers Tribunal filings, says the UK's Technical Capability Notice still demands global access to iCloud data, so DNI Gabbard's tweet of resolution may be wrong; everything is under gag orders.
- npm compromise of 8 September 2025: a maintainer ("qix") was phished with a fake "update your 2FA" email from an npm.js.com look-alike registered three days earlier, with a 10 September deadline. Packages affected include chalk, debug, ansi-styles, strip-ansi, supports-color, color-convert, ansi-regex, wrap-ansi, slice-ansi, error-ex, simple-swizzle, color-string, is-arrayish and others; error-ex alone gets about 47 million downloads weekly. The injected obfuscated JavaScript hooked fetch, XMLHttpRequest and wallet interfaces to swap cryptocurrency destination addresses with look-alikes. A build failure ("fetch is not defined") exposed it. Steve calls the npm model a castle of cards; Leo notes most developers never see transitive dependencies.
- BYTE magazine's demise: Tom Halfhill's FAQ explains that CMP, after buying McGraw-Hill's publications, shut BYTE in 1998; Byte.com revived it online until 2009.
- Letters of marque: CyberScoop reports Google's Sandra Joyce announced a cyber "disruption unit" at a Center for Cybersecurity Policy and Law (CCPL) event on 26 August; the paper "To Hack Back, or Not Hack Back?" and officials (Pentagon, CIA, USCYBERCOM nominees) show growing appetite for offensive operations; proposals include Congress (not the President) granting private firms letters of marque. Steve is uneasy about "retaliation" and doubts cyber capability deters because it cannot be displayed; Leo argues governments, not companies, should hold such power, warns of escalation and that the grid is vulnerable.
- Historical anecdote: during the Code Red/Nimda era, the DoJ said remotely disinfecting infected servers would still be illegal.
