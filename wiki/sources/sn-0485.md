# Summary

Steve drops his planned Tor deep dive to cover the Sony Pictures breach. News first: the POODLE padding attack survives the disabling of SSLv3 because roughly 10 percent of sites, mostly big ones behind F5 and A10 load balancers, run TLS stacks that do not check the padding format TLS requires; F5 already has a fix. A Linux backdoor was found for the Russian-linked Turla (Snake/Uroburos) espionage suite, which until now ran only on Windows; it sits silently on an Internet-facing server and opens a shell back to whoever sends it a specially crafted packet. Judge Richard Posner is reported saying phones the government cannot search should be illegal and that privacy is mainly about hiding bad behavior, drawing a rebuttal from Cory Doctorow. A Minnesota judge refuses to dismiss the banks' suit against Target, noting that its FireEye alarms were ignored; replacement of cards cost about 400 million dollars. Steve then catalogs the Sony data dumps: employee Social Security numbers, plaintext credentials and password files, certificates, financial records, contracts, unreleased films and the co-chairman's mailbox, from a claimed 12 TB, released by a group calling itself Guardians of Peace. Steve and Leo conclude that a single flat network, blacklist-style computing and sheer size make such breaches hard to prevent for any large company, and that simplicity and compartmentalization are the only real defenses.

# Key points

- POODLE aftermath: because SSL specified padding loosely, block-cipher CBC mode could be attacked; TLS fixes the padding format, but F5 and A10 appliances did not verify it, leaving about 10 percent of sites exposed. F5 issued an update, and Adam Langley published the finding.
- Firefox 34 removes SSLv3 completely, and Chrome planned to follow.
- Turla/Snake/Uroburos: a state-sponsored espionage platform at least six years old, attributed to Russia on the basis of working hours and a UTC+4 time zone, with victims mostly in Ukraine, Lithuania, the UK and a few other Western countries. It uses fake Flash-update watering holes and phishing.
- The new Linux component builds on a 14-year-old open source backdoor (cd00r), is statically linked, stripped, needs no root, hides from process listings, and sniffs traffic with libpcap and a raw socket for a magic TCP or UDP packet, then connects out and hands the attacker a shell. It therefore only works on directly Internet-facing servers, not behind a proxying load balancer.
- Attackers use a Windows foothold to find and infect Linux servers, which often get less scrutiny.
- Judge Posner told a conference it should be illegal to make phones the government cannot search and that mass NSA collection is fine; Cory Doctorow answered that he confuses secrecy with privacy.
- Target: one year after the breach of 40 million cards and personal data on up to 110 million people, a judge ruled that banks can pursue Target for negligence, since its FireEye intrusion detection (a 1.6 million dollar system) raised alerts that went unheeded. A SANS editor worried the lesson might become to avoid knowing; Leo notes the coming chip-and-PIN liability shift to the party with the weakest technology.
- Sony Pictures: the extortion email was ignored, then data began to leak on 25 November 2014, including four unreleased films downloaded over 100,000 times, then tens of thousands of files with tens of thousands of Social Security numbers, around 500 plaintext logins, 121 FTP credentials, server certificates, bank and wire details, tax returns, licensing contracts, incident reports and executive mail. The malware spread through Windows file shares, wiped drives and carried a list of 50 Sony server names, so it was tailored to the target.
- Attribution was unclear: rumors pointed to North Korea over the film "The Interview," the group's English was clumsy, and Leo noted insider or misdirection theories; the FBI sent flash alerts to other companies and was chasing downloaders of the leaked files.
- Steve's lessons: flat monoculture networks, default-allow (blacklisting) operating systems and ordinary employee habits such as a "passwords" file on the desktop make large organizations nearly impossible to secure; compartmentalized networks and simplicity help. He compares it to RSA's breach via one employee opening a malicious attachment.
- Leo adds that the leaked Sony data raises the same ethical question as the celebrity photo leak, and cautions against schadenfreude.
