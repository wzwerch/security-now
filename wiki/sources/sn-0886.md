# Summary

Steve starts with a US Army Cyber recruiting tweet, then addresses the flood of listener questions about LastPass's August 2022 disclosure that an attacker used a single compromised developer account to take source code and technical information; the company said it saw no evidence of customer data or vault access. Steve reasons that the zero-knowledge vault design means the breach does not change his decision, keeps using LastPass, but says he would pick Bitwarden if starting today. Next are the FTC lawsuit against location data broker Kochava and an FCC inquiry into carrier geolocation practices, followed by a pointer to a sweeping California children's online safety bill that Leo and Techdirt criticize for implying age verification of all visitors. Excerpts of a Russian ransomware operator's interview show how quickly criminals weaponize freshly published proofs of concept. Steve also covers DuckDuckGo's Email Protection leaving beta, around 80,000 still-unpatched Hikvision cameras, SpinRite 6.1 progress, and two Ben-Gurion University covert channels (ETHERLED and GAIROSCOPE) for leaking data out of air-gapped machines.

# Key points

- LastPass disclosed a breach of its development environment through one compromised developer account; source code and proprietary technical information were taken. Leo noted it was "no evidence" rather than a categorical denial and linked it to the wider Twilio/Okta-style social engineering wave, in which Cloudflare resisted because of hardware security keys.
- Steve's view: with client-side PBKDF-based encryption the vault remains safe if the local decryption code is not tampered with; switching managers would be an emotional rather than rational choice absent new facts, and changing the master password would not help. He prefers open source (Bitwarden) for vettable architecture.
- FTC sued data broker Kochava (Idaho) for selling precise geolocation tied to advertising IDs that can reveal visits to clinics or places of worship; Kochava had sued the FTC first and announced a "privacy block" feature.
- Steve was blocked from Kochava's site by NextDNS filtering, which he had just started trying.
- FCC probed 15 mobile carriers on geolocation practices; retention ranged up to five years (AT&T). Steve is skeptical the FCC can act: earlier proposed fines of over $200 million remained pending, and instantaneous resale makes retention limits moot.
- California Senate passed (33-0) a children's online safety bill; Leo and Techdirt argue it effectively forces every site to determine whether visitors are under 18.
- Russian ransomware operator Mikhail Matveev (alias Babuk) told The Record that rival crews often collide in the same victim network, that he started with BlueKeep, got into Capcom via a Fortinet VPN flaw, and that after a Fortinet proof of concept he had about 48,000 entry points but worked through under 3%.
- He described a Dutch logistics firm compromised via ProxyLogon with domain-wide reused admin passwords and weak NAS backups; the buggy decryptor left VMDK files at zero bytes, yet a $2 million payment was made. He claimed ransom payments fell after Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
- Steve's takeaway: these criminals are not elite researchers; they live off the patch-delay window by weaponizing public proofs of concept immediately.
- DuckDuckGo Email Protection (free, @duck.com) strips trackers from forwarded email and offers unlimited disposable aliases; DuckDuckGo said 85% of beta emails contained trackers; messages are not stored.
- CYFIRMA found about 80,000 of 285,000 internet-facing Hikvision cameras still vulnerable to CVE-2021-36260 (command injection patched Sept 2021), spread over 2,300 organizations in 100 countries; the flaw was used by the Moobot Mirai-variant botnet and is on CISA's exploited list.
- ETHERLED: malware controlling network card LEDs (via firmware/driver control, link-speed changes, or enabling/disabling the interface) can leak data optically at roughly 1 to 100 bits per second to a nearby camera; Steve notes short secrets like keys need little bandwidth.
- GAIROSCOPE: ultrasonic tones from a PC speaker excite a smartphone's MEMS gyroscope at resonance, leaking data at about 8 bits per second to a phone up to 8 meters away without microphone access.
- Listener feedback: Microsoft says removing local admin rights makes most attacks (including the previous week's Bumblebee UAC bypass) ineffective; Cybereason acknowledged the point.

# Other segments

- SpinRite 6.1: Steve finished reviewing the DynaStat data-recovery code after the I/O driver changes and is about to start running and testing it; long beta planned for 6.1 with 6.0 owners eligible.
- Steve noted John Carmack's talk on using debuggers, sharing his debugging philosophy.
- TV and book chatter: "The Old Man", "Patriot", and Dennis Taylor's "The Singularity Trap"; Steve's former InfoWorld copy editor Michael Miller.
- Listener Ed consolidated password managers down to Bitwarden.
