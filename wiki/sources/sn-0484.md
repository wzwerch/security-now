# Summary

Leo is away, so tech journalist Mike Elgan co-hosts this listener Q&A. Steve starts with Firefox 34: Yahoo replaces Google as the default search engine for most locales, SSLv3 is disabled, early HTTP/2 and ALPN support arrive, and a Hello chat client is built in. He then complains about how buggy iOS 8 is and discusses a Washington Post piece on how rude anonymous comments sway readers, with Mike adding that paid commenter "armies" in Russia and China exploit this. The listener questions cover how to push a blog onto HTTPS once it has a certificate, what to do about an employer that decrypts HTTPS traffic with a firewall, the removal of the OCSP-enforcement setting from Firefox's UI (it still works through about:config), why root CA certificates live until 2037 while intermediates are shorter-lived, and how a student should anonymously report a data leak in an online class. Further items include a ransomware attack that came in through an exposed Remote Desktop port on a company PC whose backups had silently stopped, the Norse-mythology origin of the Regin malware name, why CryptoLink is shelved while crypto law is unsettled, and a useful site for steering relatives to safer computers. Next week's episode is announced as a deep dive on research into de-anonymizing Tor users.

# Key points

- Firefox 34 changes the default search provider to Yahoo (Yandex in some regional locales), disables SSLv3, adds early HTTP/2 and ALPN support, many web-crypto APIs, and the Hello real-time chat client.
- iOS 8 is described as unusually buggy (keyboard rendering and calendar glitches); Steve says the problems are widespread, not just the hosts' devices.
- A piece on online comment culture argues that hostile anonymous comments measurably change how readers judge an article; Steve and Mike discuss optional verified identities and letting authors curate comments, and note paid astroturfing operations by governments and parties.
- To move a site to HTTPS after getting a certificate: change internal links to HTTPS, avoid mixed content, post only HTTPS links, and add a permanent redirect from HTTP; search engines then update their index. The EFF's free automated DV certificate project is due in the second quarter of 2015.
- A listener finds that his employer's firewall decrypts and rescans all HTTPS traffic, detected by comparing certificate serial numbers with GRC's HTTPS fingerprint service. Steve says a VPN would bypass the interception because it uses private certificates rather than the public CA hierarchy, but the firewall may block VPNs.
- Firefox removed the OCSP "force" option from its preferences UI, but the underlying setting (ocsp.required) remains honored and can be set in about:config.
- Roots have very long lifetimes (2037) because their private keys are kept offline; day-to-day signing is done by shorter-lived intermediate certificates, so an intermediate compromise does not force a root to be distrusted. DigiNotar's instant collapse is the incentive for CAs to protect keys.
- A student found a CSV of all class grades on an online course site; Steve advises reporting it on paper, printed at a copy shop and paid for in cash, because color laser printers embed identifying yellow dots and electronic reports leave a trail.
- A company's PC was hit by ransomware that arrived through an exposed Remote Desktop Protocol port and encrypted Office and Outlook files, and the machine's backup had stopped silently; Steve urges never exposing RDP and verifying backups work.
- The name Regin comes from a Norse-myth dwarf; Steve passes on an unconfirmed rumor that Western intelligence services used it and that AV vendors knew of it for years without saying so.
- CryptoLink, Steve's designed Trust-No-One VPN, stays shelved: he is finishing SQRL and SpinRite 6.1/6.2 first, mentions the use of old writs to compel phone decryption and the NSA chief's attack on Apple, and would only ever release it as freeware.
- A relative-friendly website, HowToReplaceYourPC.com, advises ordinary users on choosing safer, cheaper computers (a MacBook Air if in doubt; a Chromebook for simple use).
- Show notes will now be linked from the podcast feed to GRC's PDF show notes instead of a blank wiki page.
- Next week: a deep dive on research claiming Tor traffic can be de-anonymized at a high rate using features of Cisco routers.
