# Summary

Google's May Android bulletin fixed 37 flaws, including CVE-2021-22600, a Linux kernel double-free privilege escalation already in CISA's exploited catalog. Connecticut's privacy bill became law automatically after five days, and Steve reiterates that sites must honor the Global Privacy Control signal without extra prompts. A quick ransomware roundup (supermarkets, schools, a Costa Rican utility, Coca-Cola claims, and Illinois' Lincoln College closing for good after an attack) leads into the US State Department's up-to-$15 million reward offer for Conti members; the FBI counts over 1,000 Conti victims and more than 150 million dollars in payments. Cybereason's Operation CuckooBees describes Chinese group Winnti (APT41) stealing intellectual property from manufacturers for years, and the FBI director calls China the biggest threat. A White House memorandum on quantum-resistant cryptography draws Steve's skepticism, since quantum machines are far from breaking keys and federal systems are not patched. Nozomi found that uClibc and uClibc-ng generate sequential DNS transaction IDs, reopening the Kaminsky-style poisoning risk for many IoT devices. F5 patched a CVSS 9.8 authentication bypass in BIG-IP's iControl REST for current versions only. After listener items, including PayPal's odd password character limits, Steve spends the main segment on the FIDO Alliance, Apple, Google and Microsoft passkey announcement, explaining that it adds Bluetooth-based phone authentication and cloud-synced FIDO credentials, and comparing it with his abandoned SQRL design.

# Key points

- Android May 2022 patch: 37 flaws, one critical in MediaTek/Qualcomm components; CVE-2021-22600 (CVSS 7.8, double free in the Linux packet socket code) was exploited in limited targeted attacks and had been fixed in desktop Linux distributions months earlier.
- Connecticut SB 6 is law (fifth state after California, Colorado, Virginia and Utah); opt-out preference signals like Global Privacy Control must be honored with no challenge dialogs.
- Ransomware snapshot: Conti hit Costa Rican government and a town utility (state of emergency declared), Ireland's health service a year earlier (about 100 million dollars of recovery cost), and US healthcare and 911 centers; the ContiLeaks chat leak in February did not stop it.
- State Department rewards: up to 10 million dollars for information on Conti leaders and another 5 million for arrest or conviction; similar offers existed for DarkSide and REvil.
- Lincoln College in Illinois closed after 157 years following a December ransomware attack on top of pandemic losses.
- Operation CuckooBees (Cybereason): Winnti/APT41 stole IP, source code and blueprints from defense, energy, biotech, aerospace and pharmaceutical firms since about 2019 using ERP flaws, signed kernel rootkits, new malware (DEPLOYLOG, WINNKIT) and CLFS abuse in a "house of cards" chain; victims were unaware; enabling factors were unpatched systems, poor segmentation, forgotten accounts and no multifactor authentication; FBI director Wray says China opens a new counterintelligence case about every 12 hours.
- Quantum memo (NSM-10): federal agencies must prepare for quantum-resistant cryptography; Steve says real attacks need thousands of stable qubits (IBM then offered 27 to 127), factoring-based RSA is the worry, and Steve tells Leo that elliptic-curve keys such as Ed25519 sidestep the factoring problem (a simplification; elliptic-curve schemes are also expected to fall to a large quantum computer); agencies should first patch and reboot.
- uClibc/uClibc-ng DNS flaw (CVE-2022-30295, Nozomi Networks): the resolver increments a 16-bit transaction ID sequentially, so spoofed replies can poison DNS caches; many embedded Linux products (Netgear, Linksys, OpenWrt-based) may be affected; Steve's fix idea is a per-boot unpredictable seed, for example from packet timing, keying a cipher over a counter; original uClibc has been unmaintained since 2012.
- F5 BIG-IP: patches for 43 bugs, with one critical iControl REST authentication bypass (CVSS 9.8) allowing unauthenticated commands via management or self IPs; a proof of concept appeared within days; versions 11.x and 12.x stay unpatched; CISA added five more old exploited bugs (three from 2014) to its catalog.
- Listener feedback: PayPal limited passwords to a small set of symbols; overlay networks (Hamachi-style) as a way to avoid port forwarding; reading advice for Michael McCollum's novels.
- Passkeys analysis (FIDO white paper "Multi-Device FIDO Credentials", WebAuthn Level 3): FIDO relaxes the hardware-key stance, lets a phone act as a roaming authenticator over Bluetooth for logins on another device, and expects platform vendors to sync FIDO credentials in the cloud so they survive device loss; "passkey" is a vendor marketing term, not a FIDO term.
- Steve's view: this makes FIDO practical but weaker than physical keys, depends on the security of the platform accounts, still needs a username (it replaces only the password), and is less elegant than SQRL, where one master key deterministically derives per-site keys and a printed backup suffices; passwords will never go away.
- Privacy aside: Leo describes why data broker location data on abortion clinic visits changed his view on privacy.
