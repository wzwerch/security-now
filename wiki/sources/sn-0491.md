# Summary

In a quiet news week, Steve and Leo discuss a New York Times story by David Sanger, backed by a published Snowden-era NSA exchange, which says the NSA had implanted malware in North Korean networks (partly by piggybacking on South Korean access) years before the Sony attack, explaining the US government's confidence in attribution. Leo cautions that the reporter relied on anonymous officials and has a history of relaying government claims, and a security blogger called it not credible. Steve then devotes the show to cryptographic backdoors after the Cameron and Obama remarks on terrorist communications. He argues the popular claim that giving governments access necessarily weakens crypto is technically wrong: a true backdoor is a secret flaw, but systems like iMessage already encrypt one message key to several recipients' public keys, so a government (or each vendor, as in the Clipper Chip's escrow) could simply hold a private key that is added as an extra recipient. The security then depends on guarding that private key, as certificate authorities do. Steve stresses he does not advocate this; the real issues are policy, the burden of key management, and that criminals would use unescrowed cryptography. Leo agrees the argument must be accurate even when one dislikes the conclusion, and notes the economic damage such mandates would cause US and UK technology firms.

# Key points

- Sanger's report: the NSA began penetrating North Korea's networks around 2010 via Chinese transit links, Malaysian connections, and South Korean implants ("fourth-party" and "fifth-party" collection), so it may have watched the Sony attackers; North Korea's hacking force is estimated around 6,000 people under the Reconnaissance General Bureau and Bureau 121.
- Leo urges skepticism about unnamed-official sourcing, noting the same reporter's pre-Iraq-war reporting and a critical Errata Security post, and that James Clapper has a credibility problem; Steve finds the leaked internal NSA text technically plausible but unproven.
- Definition: a cryptographic backdoor is a secret known to the designers that bypasses normal key protection; the dual elliptic curve random bit generator is cited as a possible example, and secrets are hard to keep.
- Multi-recipient encryption: public-key crypto is too slow for bulk data, so a random symmetric key encrypts the message and that key is separately encrypted for each recipient's public key; adding a government or vendor escrow key would be a transparent extra recipient, not a weakness in the math.
- Steve stresses the policy debate is separate and that he opposes mandated access; it is why he shelved the CryptoLink VPN project. Leo points to Skipjack and Clipper Chip, and to the earlier criticism that secret algorithms cannot be vetted, so any escrowed scheme should use public algorithms.
- Practical limits: criminals can use unescrowed cryptography; companies already inspect TLS traffic with proxies; an alternative mentioned is metadata collection or compelled decryption on demand.
- Leo argues mandates would devastate US and UK tech exports; UK listeners warn Cameron may mean it, noting UK Internet traffic is already filtered.
