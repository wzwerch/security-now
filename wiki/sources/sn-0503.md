# Summary

Mike Elgan co-hosts while Leo is away. The episode (raw title is the placeholder "EPISODE: #503"; the transcript header gives "Listener Feedback #210") starts with the EFF's success against the Personal Audio podcast patent, where prior art undermined the claim. Steve follows up on CNNIC: Mozilla followed Google by rejecting CNNIC certificates signed after 1 April, while Apple and Microsoft had not, and the rogue intermediate certificate turned out to be valid for only two weeks. The Simda botnet of roughly 770,000 machines was taken down by a joint private and law-enforcement effort. Apple's Rootpipe local privilege escalation was fixed in OS X 10.10 only, so Steve repeats the advice to avoid daily admin accounts. Further news covers Kaspersky's decryption keys for CoinVault ransomware, US proposals for split-key encryption access, widespread bad cipher-suite ordering at banks, and the Dyre Wolf phishing-plus-phone fraud. The ten listener questions include how Wi-Fi helps phone location, SpinRite and PCIe SSDs, self-signed certificates, secure-email providers, a routable IP at a college, TrueCrypt keyfiles, corporate TLS interception and EV certificates, and Windows' on-demand root store.

# Key points

- Podcast patent: the EFF filed prior-art evidence with the patent office showing the patent asserted by Personal Audio was not novel; Mike notes the Eastern District of Texas is favourable to patent trolls.
- CNNIC aftermath: Mozilla adopted Chrome's stance (no trust for CNNIC certificates signed after 1 April 2015) while Apple and Microsoft kept the root; Microsoft's on-demand root program even fetched a CNNIC root on a fresh Windows 10 install. The intermediate certificate had a lifetime of only two weeks. Steve plans to discuss the Great Cannon next week.
- Simda botnet: about 770,000 PCs in 190 countries, spread through Java, Flash and Silverlight flaws, with polymorphic backdoor code; it edited the Windows hosts file so lookups for the Facebook connect domain and Google Analytics resolved to its 14 command servers. Fourteen servers were seized in a coordinated action; the US had about 22 percent of infections.
- Rootpipe: a local privilege-escalation bug in OS X found by Emil Kvarnhammar and reported to Apple in October; fixed only in 10.10, with older versions left unpatched. Steve's advice is to run daily work from a non-admin account (some malware had used the bug).
- Kaspersky obtained decryption keys for CoinVault ransomware and set up a public no-ransom service for victims.
- Encryption policy: reporting on NSA head Rogers' split-key escrow idea and White House options such as mirror accounts; Matthew Green warned that any mistake would nullify the guarantees. Steve says the outcome is binary, either a mandated decryption capability exists or not.
- Cipher-suite ordering: after the Bank of America RC4-first finding, many listeners reported similar or worse results at their own banks; Steve says servers should place strongest suites first and keep weak ones at the bottom only for compatibility.
- Verizon and Symantec reports cite phishing and user error as the usual entry point; Steve repeats the rule not to act on anything that was offered rather than sought.
- Dyre Wolf (reported by IBM): malware that detects a banking site, shows a fake notice telling the customer to phone a number, and connects to a staffed call centre.
- Wi-Fi geolocation works because access-point identifiers have been mapped against GPS position; Mike argues Google's Wi-Fi collection in Germany was unfairly criticised.
- Listener warning: the Configuration Mania Firefox add-on reset existing about:config settings.
- HP held Class A network 15 and, via DEC, 16 (a correction to the previous week).
- SpinRite 6.1 plan: talk AHCI directly, drop the BIOS, reach roughly half a terabyte per hour; PCIe SSDs will be supported.
- Self-signed certificates are fine for private services with controlled clients but cannot establish trust for the public.
- Secure email: Steve is sceptical of providers that publish no technical details, and notes email lacks built-in end-to-end security, so protection holds only if the other party uses the same service or a link-based retrieval.
- A routable public IP on a campus Wi-Fi network is risky; Steve suggests putting a small travel router in front of personal devices.
- TrueCrypt keyfiles: the audit's weak CRC mixing does not meaningfully weaken a volume protected by a keyfile plus password, absent an attacker present at setup.
- Corporate TLS interception: a Windows group policy can push the proxy's root certificate; EV certificates are not spoofed because browsers pin EV roots, but Internet Explorer lets internal CAs set EV flags, Chrome uses the Windows store, and Firefox, with its own store, notices interception.
- Since Windows Server 2008 R2 and Windows 8.1, the Windows root store is filled on demand from Microsoft, so a typical machine holds only a couple dozen roots, whereas Firefox ships hundreds.
