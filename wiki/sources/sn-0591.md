# Summary

The last fresh episode of 2016 groups many stories where law and technology collide. Steve comments cautiously on alleged Russian hacking around the US election, arguing that skilled attackers can get into almost anything given the porous state of software and the human factors involved, and Leo urges focusing on the broader cyber threat. Yahoo disclosed a 2013 breach affecting over a billion accounts, including unencrypted security questions. Legislative and court items include a South Carolina bill requiring porn filters on computers (with odd fee-based opt-outs), an Australian ruling forcing ISPs to block piracy sites, a Florida appeals court ordering a suspect to reveal a phone passcode, and the US Supreme Court agreeing to hear a patent venue case. On the technical side Steve covers Chris Evans' GStreamer exploits against Linux desktops, the Mac FileVault and Thunderbolt DMA fix revealed after patching, and Proofpoint's analysis of the DNSChanger malvertising kit that hides an AES key in ad images and attacks home routers through the browser. He also discusses the proposed US vehicle-to-vehicle mandate, photographers asking camera makers for encryption, a risky web front end to Let's Encrypt, the end of Google Contributor, and the huge IPv6 allocation size.

# Key points

- Russian hacking: no insider facts; Steve's view is that targeted attacks such as spearphishing against campaign figures are essentially unstoppable and that the broader grid and infrastructure risk matters more. Leo cautions against tying it only to election outcome.
- Yahoo: the newly disclosed billion-plus accounts breach included names, phone numbers, birth dates, hashed passwords and unencrypted security questions; reporting says the company ignored its security staff's requests to avoid inconveniencing users. Steve advises ensuring no overlap with accounts you care about and keeping old addresses dormant rather than deleted because Yahoo recycles them.
- South Carolina bill: would require sellers to include obscene-content blocking on devices, with sellers able to pay 20 dollars per device to opt out and adults to pay to disable the filter; likely to die in committee.
- Australia: a federal court ordered ISPs to block The Pirate Bay and several other sites by default; VPN searches surged; copyright holders stay involved in how blocks are implemented.
- Florida appeals court: compelled disclosure of a phone passcode is not self-incrimination because it is a surrender rather than testimony, appearing to cut against the strongbox-key versus safe-combination distinction from an earlier Supreme Court case.
- The US Supreme Court will hear TC Heartland v. Kraft, which could end the dominance of the Eastern District of Texas for patent suits; Leo recounts TWiT's patent-troll experience and the EFF's role.
- Chris Evans showed that Fedora with Chrome could be hit by drive-by file download, automatic indexing and unsandboxed GStreamer decoders, and a scriptless heap-corruption exploit of the FLIC decoder, noting obscure default decoders enlarge attack surface. A second researcher found an Ubuntu crash-reporter file-handler flaw. Steve concludes systems built for speed are fundamentally exploitable.
- Mac FileVault: the 10.12.2 update closed a hole where a Thunderbolt DMA device could read the plaintext FileVault password from RAM before macOS enabled DMA protection after a reboot; the researcher credited Apple's complete fix.
- DNSChanger malvertising (Proofpoint): ads hide an AES key in image bits to decrypt code that fingerprints home routers (166 fingerprints versus 55 earlier), uses default credentials to change DNS and sometimes opens admin ports externally; it works from Chrome on Windows and Android. Advice: change default router credentials.
- NHTSA proposed rule would mandate vehicle-to-vehicle radio communication, with firewalling and privacy requirements; Steve notes the added attack surface.
- Over 150 filmmakers and photojournalists asked camera makers to build encryption in.
- SSLforFree.com can generate the server private key in the browser or on its server for Let's Encrypt; Steve says to generate your own key and submit only a signing request.
- Google Contributor is being shut down with refunds ahead of a relaunch; pfSense's SG-1000 appliance mentioned.
- Errata: IPv6 allocations give each subscriber a 64-bit network prefix and a 64-bit host space, not 64 thousand addresses.
- Webkay page shows how much a browser reveals, including battery state.
