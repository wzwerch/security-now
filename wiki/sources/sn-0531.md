# Summary

Steve opens with a New York Stock Exchange decision tree for deciding whether to disclose a breach, then returns to 1Password: after reading its technical documents he explains that the one-file-per-item vault design pushed the company to leave metadata unencrypted for speed, and says its response was poor even though the documentation is open. He rebuts a critic's claim that LastPass leaks metadata through its favicon proxy. Press coverage suggesting the NSA breaks encryption turns out to be a re-run of the Logjam and weak Diffie-Hellman research from episode 509: widely reused 1024-bit primes could be precomputed by a nation-state, and he points to a server test site and the switch to elliptic-curve DHE. Let's Encrypt's intermediates were cross-signed by IdenTrust, and the CA Forum ballot to keep issuing SHA-1 certificates through 2016 was withdrawn after the new SHA-1 collision work. Listener questions cover Haystacks math, ISP ad injection, Privoxy, SQRL and the "what you know/have" legal distinction, VeraCrypt 1.16, DNS behind double NAT, changing ISP IP addresses, NoScript versus content blockers, free computer science curricula and Flash updating on Windows 7.

# Key points

- NYSE-published decision tree for disclosing hacks: it leans toward disclosure unless disclosure would harm the company, compromise security or trigger litigation and discovery looks unlikely.
- 1Password: each vault item was a separate file to ease sync, which scaled badly; encrypting metadata would have required opening thousands of files. The newer format groups items into 16 files keyed on the first hex digit of each item's UUID and encrypts metadata. Steve stays critical of the company's tone, including a tweet saying the data is protected by the sync provider's password.
- LastPass favicon fetching goes through LastPass servers over HTTPS so sites do not see user cookies or IPs; Steve says this is a known, deliberate privacy design, not a metadata leak.
- Western Digital My Passport encryption analysis: abstract mentions RAM leakage, weak key attacks and backdoors; Steve defers the full discussion to an episode a few weeks out.
- Logjam recap: downgrade of Diffie-Hellman to 512-bit export-grade, and the paper's estimate that breaking the most common 1024-bit prime would expose about 18 percent of top HTTPS sites, about two thirds of VPN servers for a second prime and a quarter of SSH servers; NSA leak details are consistent with such a break. Mitigation for server admins: use ECDHE or unique primes; the weakdh test site can check a server.
- Let's Encrypt: free automated domain-validation CA backed by Mozilla, Akamai, Cisco, EFF, IdenTrust and others; its intermediates were cross-signed by IdenTrust so all clients already trust issued certificates; public launch date still not set.
- SHA-1: the CA/Browser Forum ballot was withdrawn after Apple, OpenTrust and Mozilla voiced opposition following the compression-function collision paper; no new SHA-1 certificates after 2015, and existing ones may live through 2016.
- Listener: Haystacks search space counts include shorter passwords, hence 110 rather than 100 for two digits.
- Listener: an ISP (Charter) injected an advertisement banner into HTTP pages; Steve says this only works on unencrypted pages and a VPN or HTTPS avoids it, and fears future ISP-mandated certificates.
- Listener: Privoxy as a regular-expression-capable filtering proxy; Steve recalls Proxomitron.
- SQRL: a password or fingerprint unlock is an implementation detail; a fingerprint falls under "something you have" and can be compelled, a password cannot; Steve insists on a password interlock by default.
- VeraCrypt 1.16 fixed the folder-deletion bug from 1.15 and the TrueCrypt driver privilege-escalation flaw; Steve now says migration is reasonable (system-drive encryption requires decrypting with TrueCrypt first).
- With nested routers, configure the inner router to use public DNS directly rather than forward through the outer router.
- ISPs have no obligation to keep dynamic IPs static; some change them at every reconnect.
- Steve dropped NoScript in favour of uBlock Origin and iOS blockers; some sites break even when whitelisted.
- Flash correction: Internet Explorer only auto-updates Flash on Windows 8 and later, not Windows 7.
