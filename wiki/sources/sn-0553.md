# Summary

A news-heavy episode. The government told the court it had accessed the data on the San Bernardino iPhone and no longer needed Apple's help; Steve and Leo debated what that meant, noting a report that the data was extracted but not yet decrypted and wondering whether the exploit would be disclosed. California's AB-1681 would require smartphones sold from 2017 to be decryptable by the maker, with a 2,500 dollar penalty per device. A magazine feature linked TrueCrypt's lineage to E4M and a former arms dealer, and Matthew Green tweeted this as fact. StartSSL had let anyone obtain a certificate for any domain by editing the email address parameter on its validation form. Two more hospital systems were hit by ransomware (Locky at a Kentucky hospital and a large Washington health system), while Hollywood Presbyterian's earlier payment was recalled. The Badlock site pre-announced a serious Samba and Windows SMB flaw to be patched on 12 April. The Wi-Fi Alliance was working on a Device Provisioning Protocol for IoT onboarding. Steve released Never10, a free tool that sets Microsoft's own registry controls to block the Windows 10 upgrade and locks them read-only, and described his new machine and the NetWorx tool that can read router traffic counters over SNMP.

# Key points

- Government filing: it has "successfully accessed" the phone's data. NBC's reporter said officials described the data as encrypted and needing time to decode; Steve says nothing is verified. Leo expects the FBI will keep the method secret and the demand for backdoors will continue.
- California AB-1681 amends the phone kill-switch law; sold on or after 1 Jan 2017 phones must be unlockable by maker or OS provider; the attorney general or district attorneys enforce. Leo thinks it would be challenged in court. The Burr-Feinstein text still unseen.
- TrueCrypt: reported to descend from E4M written by Paul Le Roux; development ceased around the time of his arrest; VeraCrypt recommended; Steve plans to use BitLocker on his Windows 7 laptop; ATA drive passwords can be bypassed by manufacturers for law enforcement.
- StartSSL: domain validation email address was a client-supplied parameter, so an attacker could redirect the validation message to their own mailbox and obtain a certificate for any domain; Steve says it shows the public key infrastructure lacks verification of CAs, though Let's Encrypt has reduced reliance on such free CAs.
- Ransomware in hospitals: a Kentucky hospital declared an internal emergency over Locky spread by a spam attachment and was rebuilding PCs one at a time (ransom demand about four bitcoin); MedStar Health in the Washington area, with 10 hospitals, lost access to systems and reverted to paper; Hollywood Presbyterian had paid 40 bitcoin earlier.
- Badlock: site announced that Microsoft and the Samba team would patch a serious SMB/Samba flaw on 12 April (Patch Tuesday), expecting quick exploitation; the SMB service should not be internet-exposed and Steve uses it only inside a VPN.
- Wi-Fi Alliance Device Provisioning Protocol: aims to let an authenticated smartphone onboard devices with no screen while keeping credentials hidden; Steve hopes it avoids the mistakes of the WPS PIN scheme.
- Never10: an 81 KB no-install utility from GRC (a few KB of code plus an icon and signature) that checks the Windows Update client, installs the required update if needed, sets the two registry values that disable the upgrade, and removes the read-only protection when reversed; it also made Windows delete already-downloaded upgrade files in testing. About 27,000 downloads in days, becoming GRC's most downloaded item that week. A new SHA-256 code-signing certificate triggered SmartScreen warnings at first.
- Other GRC download figures given: DNS Benchmark about 2.53 million, SecurAble about 3.7 million, LeakTest nearly 8 million.
- NetWorx can poll a router's WAN interface counters over SNMP to monitor whole-network bandwidth.
- Steve's build: Silverstone case, Noctua fans, eight-core overclocked Intel CPU, NVMe boot drive, mirrored 3 TB drives with heat sinks, fanless multi-output graphics cards, 64 GB RAM (128 GB tested but slows memory clock), modular Corsair power supply.
