# Summary

Steve follows up on the PKfail issue (test Platform Keys marked "DO NOT TRUST" still shipping in roughly one in ten PCs) and explains that Binarly's command-line check is unreliable and prone to false negatives, so he wrote a small signed Windows tool, IsBootSecure?, over the weekend. He then covers the Firefox cookie confusion: Firefox's Total Cookie Protection isolates cookies per site, which GRC's 22-year-old Cookie Forensics test does not understand, and Mozilla's own documentation describes a heuristic that lets third parties use cross-site cookies a few times. The W3C's Technical Architecture Group published a finding on 26 July 2024 that third-party cookies must be removed. Steve reviews the CrowdStrike aftermath: a shareholder class action, Delta Air Lines' dispute with CrowdStrike (limitation-of-liability clause, a refused offer of on-site help) and a passenger class action. He thanks listeners for the flood of feedback to securitynow@grc.com. The main topic is DigiCert revoking 83,267 certificates because a DNS CNAME-based domain validation omitted a required underscore prefix, contrasted with Entrust's past reluctance to revoke. Steve then shows that no tested browser actually detects a revoked certificate (revoked.grc.com) and says the industry is heading to short-lived certificates (about 90 days) with ACME automation instead.

# Key points

- PKfail: Binarly estimated about 10% of PCs still carry the AMI sample Platform Key; a GRC newsgroup member found one on a newly bought Dell.
- Binarly's PowerShell check needs an extra UEFI module, relaxed script blocking and Secure Boot; it hides errors and tends to report false negatives.
- New freeware IsBootSecure? (about 21 KB, signed) reports BIOS vs UEFI, Secure Boot state, and the Platform Key certificate's serial, issuer, subject and validity dates; a GUI version was planned for the following week.
- Firefox: Total Cookie Protection keeps one cookie jar per first-party site, but its UI exposes no switch, so GRC's Cookie Forensics test still flags cookies unless third-party cookies are force-disabled via custom settings.
- Mozilla's documented exception heuristic (sites may request cross-site cookie access, allowed about five times or 1% of unique sites per session, then the user is prompted) is called a kludge that deceives users who tick "block third-party cookies".
- Steve plans to update Cookie Forensics to test inter-site isolation (he registered grctech.dev as a second unaffiliated domain for that purpose).
- W3C TAG finding (26 July 2024): third-party cookies are harmful and must be removed from the web platform; replacements must be purpose-built and not recreate tracking. It follows Chrome's retreat from removing them.
- Leo and Steve mention uBlock Origin's coming trouble with Chrome's Manifest V3 as a reason to stay on Firefox.
- CrowdStrike: shareholder class action (filed 30 July in W.D. Texas, share price down about 32% in 12 days); Delta claims about $500 million in costs, CrowdStrike replies that liability is contractually capped at single-digit millions and that Delta declined on-site help; a passenger class action followed.
- Limitation-of-liability clauses in SaaS contracts usually cap damages at roughly twelve months of fees; many enterprises never negotiate them.
- CrowdStrike's much-awaited root cause analysis arrived just before recording; Steve deferred it.
- DigiCert: the random value in CNAME-based Domain Control Validation lacked the mandated leading underscore after architecture changes starting 2019 and a UX change on 11 June 2024; about 0.4% of validations, 83,267 certificates across 6,807 customers, all revoked by 3 August 2024 7:30 UTC; CISA warned of possible disruption.
- A listener noted a real, theoretical collision risk with dynamic-DNS subdomains; no abuse was found, and dynamic DNS providers will block leading underscores.
- Steve contrasts DigiCert's compliance with Entrust's long habit of not revoking mis-issued certificates.
- revoked.grc.com serves a deliberately revoked certificate: Chrome, Firefox and (now) Safari all accept it; SSL Labs flags it as revoked. Revocation checking effectively does not work in browsers.
- Direction of travel: shorter certificate lifetimes (13 months now, perhaps 90 days) with ACME automation, since revocation cannot be relied on.
