# Summary

The show opens with the consensus attribution of the May WannaCry attack to North Korea, then covers a coming Firefox 58 privacy feature that blocks reading pixels back from an offscreen canvas (taken from the Tor browser), a fingerprinting method that avoids cookie rules. Google will remove HTTP Public Key Pinning (HPKP) from Chrome because it is too brittle. Steve explains why an image as a second factor is replayable and weaker than TOTP or public-key challenge-response. Felix Krause's camera permission warning, a pair of reports on breaking Google's audio and image reCAPTCHA, a Portuguese ISP's zero-rated service bundles, and the hijack of Coinhive's DNS through a password reused from the Kickstarter breach follow. Bad Rabbit ransomware spread through fake Flash updates with an NSA-leaked SMB exploit; Windows 10 gains Controlled Folder Access; LG's SmartThinQ robot vacuum had a camera-hijack flaw; and Amazon Key is introduced. The closing segment revisits the Reaper botnet using F5 Labs' more measured analysis: it exploits nine known IoT vulnerabilities, has not attacked anyone, and its true size is uncertain.

# Key points

- WannaCry: the UK and others concluded North Korea (a hacker group of roughly 6,000 people) was responsible; SMBv1 flaw also hit Windows 7 and Server 2008; Microsoft patched even XP.
- Firefox 58 (January 2018): blocks canvas pixel readback to defeat canvas fingerprinting, following Firefox 52's block on font enumeration. Fingerprinting is rising because it escapes EU cookie rules.
- HPKP removal planned for Chrome in May 2018: trust-on-first-use pinning is dangerous because a changed certificate locks out returning visitors; adoption was tiny (around 0.04% of top sites).
- Visual-object second factors are replayable and fuzzy; hierarchy of quality is password, TOTP (replay-proof but server holds a shared secret, cf. RSA SecurID breach), then public-key challenge-response such as SQRL.
- iOS camera permission: once granted, an app in the foreground can silently capture photos, video and face data; audit Privacy > Camera and Location Services regularly.
- CAPTCHA wars: unCAPTCHA combined six speech recognizers to solve Google's audio challenge with about 85% accuracy in about 5.4 seconds each; a vision bot solved image challenges at roughly 57-67% across services. Google's passive "I'm not a robot" check is preferred.
- Portuguese ISP sells zero-rated message, social, video, music and cloud bundles; Leo and Steve prefer pay-by-the-byte neutral pricing over zero rating.
- Coinhive DNS hijack: attackers logged into the Cloudflare account using a password reused from the 2014 Kickstarter breach with no second factor, redirected coinhive.com and diverted all embedded miners; a one-line script include is all a site needs. Most script miners mine Monero rather than bitcoin.
- Bad Rabbit ransomware (Ukraine and Russia): spread via fake Flash installers and the NSA-derived EternalRomance SMB exploit; ransom about 0.05 bitcoin. Advice: never download anything a website offers you, use NAT routers, disable UPnP, segment IoT onto a guest network, patch IoT.
- Windows 10 Fall Creators Update adds Controlled Folder Access in Windows Defender (off by default) to block untrusted apps from changing protected folders; Steve and Leo see it as another reason third-party AV is unnecessary.
- Check Point's HomeHack: flaw in LG SmartThinQ let a remote attacker control a Hom-Bot camera vacuum; LG patched over the air, which Steve holds up as the required IoT model.
- Amazon Key: Cloud Cam plus smart lock (Yale or Kwikset over ZigBee) lets an Amazon courier unlock the door for recorded in-home delivery.
- Satoshi Nakamoto is thought to hold about 5% of bitcoin (roughly $6 billion); TunnelBear zeroes the last byte of visitor IPs in analytics; a listener corrected that JS miners use Monero.
- DV versus EV: the distinction only matters in the CA model, not for DANE/TLSA certificates delivered over DNSSEC.
- Reaper revisited: F5 Labs scanned 750,000 devices matching its nine exploited vulnerabilities (growing about 85,000 per day) and estimated a potential 3.5 million; it has an update mechanism and hasn't been used for DDoS, so its purpose may be research or restraint, like Conficker. Rebooting clears it from RAM; update router firmware.
