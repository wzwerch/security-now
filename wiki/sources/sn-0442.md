# Summary

This listener-feedback episode comes right after the password-principles show, so most questions are about passwords. Steve starts with the February 2014 Patch Tuesday: seven bulletins, three critical remote-code-execution fixes (Direct2D, Internet Explorer, VBScript) and three important ones, all reaching back to Windows XP, which has about 55 days of support left. The FIDO Alliance published its specifications the same day. Leo and Steve criticize Comcast for turning customer-premises routers into public "Xfinity" hotspots without opt-in, and for apparent Netflix throttling shortly after the court ruling against net neutrality rules. Brian Krebs reported that the Target point-of-sale breach began on 15 November 2013 with network credentials stolen from an HVAC contractor, Fazio Mechanical Services. Steve also notes that a fresh Java install now ships disabled in browsers, that a law firm lost all its files to CryptoLocker through a mapped server drive, and that a new CryptoLocker variant was detected by only about half of the antivirus engines at VirusTotal. The hosts call NBC's Sochi "hacking" segment, which used unpatched machines and deliberately risky actions, irresponsible scare journalism. In the Q&A, Steve explains why password dots are pointless, why lockout and composition rules have trade-offs (denial of service, helping attackers narrow guesses), why chip-and-PIN cards resist compromised terminals, and why an isolated legacy XP machine can be reasonable. His conclusion is that passwords themselves should be abandoned in favor of key-based schemes like SQRL.

# Key points

- February 2014 Patch Tuesday: seven bulletins; critical RCE in Direct2D, Internet Explorer and VBScript, important fixes in XML, Forefront and .NET. Microsoft's deployment-priority and exploitability-index charts help triage.
- Windows XP support ends on April 8, 2014; Leo and Steve note later patches will act as pointers to unfixed XP flaws.
- EFF "The Day We Fight Back" against mass surveillance and Safer Internet Day fell on the same day; Steve sees protest fatigue compared with the SOPA blackout.
- Comcast Xfinity WiFi Home Hotspot: provider routers broadcast a second public network for other Comcast customers without opt-in; Steve worries about router firmware quality (recalling the port 32764 backdoor), while customers must phone Comcast to disable it.
- Leo reports a measurement showing Netflix traffic at roughly 235 Kb/s on Comcast but about 3 Mb/s through a VPN, evidence of throttling; Steve points out that most users have no provider choice.
- Target breach: attackers entered via credentials stolen from refrigeration/HVAC contractor Fazio Mechanical; vendor remote access that was not segregated from payment systems is the lesson.
- Oracle's current Java installer leaves the browser plug-in disabled by default, a big improvement, though older installs lack auto-update.
- CryptoLocker: a law firm lost all work product when an employee ran a fake voicemail attachment and the encryptor reached a mapped network drive; the payment window of about 72 hours passed. New variants evade antivirus.
- NBC/Richard Engel Sochi story criticized: unpatched machines, added Java and Flash, malicious sites, a deliberately enabled third-party app install on Android, and no disclosure on air. The takeaway: the risk is no different there than anywhere else.
- Echoing passwords as dots is a holdover from printing terminals and adds no real security; SQRL's password dialog will offer a Show option.
- Lockout policy: temporary versus permanent lockout is policy; a listener points out that lockout lets attackers deny service to many accounts at once, which Steve agrees is a real risk for banks using guessable account numbers.
- Case-insensitive password acceptance (Chase) probably means the case is stripped before hashing; Steve hopes salted, iterated hashing (PBKDF2) is used. Password-similarity checks can be done at change time when the old password is presented.
- Composition rules reveal the keyspace to attackers; Steve imagines per-user policies but dismisses them as another secret to keep.
- Chip and PIN: the EU saw about 80 percent less card fraud; a chip performs challenge-response so its secret never leaves the card, the same principle SQRL uses.
- Legacy XP after end of support: for fixed-function machines not used to browse, Steve thinks continued use is acceptable; Leo argues for open source where long-term support matters.
- Maximum password length limits (8 to 16 characters at some sites) undermine password-manager use.
- Microsoft research reportedly estimated about one in five Microsoft account passwords were exposed through reuse (per Leo).
- SQRL access codes will be all lowercase and slightly longer, since typing special characters on touchscreens is burdensome; entropy per character is about 4.7 bits.

# Other segments

- SQRL: Steve is iterating the design because the user interface keeps feeding changes back into the cryptography, with keying management just redesigned.
- DigiCert: Steve describes renewing GRC's EV certificate in about ten minutes and merging media.grc.com into one certificate.
- SpinRite field report: a Windows 7 blue-screen loop fixed by a Level 2 run; the leftover unreadable sector read fine on a second pass.
- Link shorteners: Steve would like to write a GRC shortener (domain grc.sc) but it ranks behind SQRL and SpinRite 6.1.
- Off-topic: GRC's peak staffing, and Leo on Bezos-style frugality.
