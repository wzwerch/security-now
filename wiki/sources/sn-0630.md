# Summary

Steve and Father Robert Ballecer open with the SQRL slogan that websites get no secrets to keep and with Steve's stress on monitoring network traffic. Back from a private security conference, Steve relays a belief in the upper security community that China was behind the Equifax intrusion, which would imply targeted use of the data rather than mass sale; Padre has seen no spike in stolen identity listings. Experian's PIN recovery for credit freezes is shown to rely on easily obtained personal data. The ISO/ANSI standardisation of the NSA-origin Simon and Speck ciphers kept only the strongest variants because of distrust after the Snowden revelations. CCleaner news worsens: the command server was configured to deliver a second-stage payload to machines in about 20 target company domains, and more than 20 machines received it, pointing to a state-sponsored supply-chain attack. ESET found FinFisher spyware delivered through ISP-level redirection of popular software downloads. Steve warns that turning radios off in iOS 11's Control Center does not truly disable them, covers the FTC v. D-Link ruling, a new Broadcom L5 GPS chip, boarding-pass barcodes, Adobe's accidental private-key leak, and ends with Google Project Zero's DOM fuzzer results across five browsers.

# Key points

- Equifax attribution: unproven belief that China did it, targeting individuals; Padre also notes earlier compromise in March, unpatched Struts, a default-credential server and executives with no security qualifications.
- Experian's "request your PIN" flow lets anyone with basic identity data recover a freeze PIN, undermining the freeze; Steve ties this to SQRL's deliberate no-recovery design and 24-digit rescue code.
- Dual EC and the standards trust problem: unexplained "magic numbers" in NSA designs; after Snowden documents, ANSI/ISO dropped the weaker Simon and Speck variants and kept only strong ones. Steve expects future standards to use publicly witnessed random generation of constants.
- CCleaner: the 5.33 32-bit installer carried a backdoor with a first stage that just checks in; the server filtered by domain (about 20, including Cisco) and delivered a second-stage payload to over 20 machines; roughly 2.23 million downloads. Piriform's initial downplaying was inaccurate. Padre likens the selective activation to Stuxnet.
- Hash-on-the-same-page checks are weak because an attacker who replaces the file can replace the hash too.
- ESET: FinFisher (previously exposed by WikiLeaks) was delivered in seven undisclosed countries by ISPs redirecting downloads of WhatsApp, Skype, Avast, VLC and WinRAR; Steve notes that a state could also produce valid certificates through a trusted CA.
- iOS 11 Control Center toggles only disconnect peripherals; Bluetooth and Wi-Fi stay on, Bluetooth reportedly turns back on at 5 a.m. and when leaving the area; only the Settings app switches truly turn them off. Steve also calls iOS 11 the buggiest release he has seen.
- FTC v. D-Link: the judge dismissed three of six claims for lack of alleged actual harm; Steve agrees and argues that laws banning fixed default admin credentials are needed.
- Broadcom BCM47755: first low-power L5 GPS chip, sampled for 2018 phones, aiming at about 30 cm accuracy and half the power through a shorter, higher-frequency signal that resists multipath.
- High-density barcodes on boarding passes contain far more personal data than the printed text; do not post photos of them.
- Adobe published its private PGP key by choosing "Export All" in the Mailvelope extension; the key was revoked and replaced. Both hosts criticise the UI for putting the dangerous option next to the safe one.
- Project Zero's Ivan Fratric ran his new open-source DOM fuzzer for about 100 million iterations per browser: Chrome (Blink) 2 bugs, Firefox (Gecko) 4, Internet Explorer (Trident) 4, Edge 6, Safari (WebKit) 17; the run is estimated to cost roughly $1,000 of cloud compute, showing fuzzing is cheap and valuable.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: cartoon of face recognition on the new iPhone with an NSA fan.
- SpinRite roadmap: 6.1 adds AHCI native hardware support, 6.2 adds low-level USB, 7.0 gets the big features; SpinRite can already create a bootable USB stick.
- Listener story of a school recovering data with SpinRite.
