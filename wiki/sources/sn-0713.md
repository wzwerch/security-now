# Summary

Over the weekend Mozilla let an intermediate signing certificate expire, which made Firefox disable popular add-ons such as uBlock Origin and LastPass until version 66.0.4; Steve explains the certificate-chain mechanics and a temporary workaround of setting the clock back a day. Google announced three- and 18-month auto-delete options for Location History and Web and App Activity after the Sensorvault controversy. Kaspersky's tracking of the exploit seller "Volodya" shows state-backed APT groups buying zero-days and one-days from independent hackers. Mozilla followed Chrome in banning obfuscated code in extensions. A hacker called Subby broke into at least 29 IoT botnet command-and-control servers because their operators used default credentials. ESET found the D-Link DCS-2132L cloud camera sends video unencrypted and accepts unauthenticated firmware. The episode also covers Edge compatibility quirks with Google Docs and Google Earth and Build 2019 announcements, then ends with a Malwarebytes view of cryptojacking after Coinhive closed in March 2019: scripts persist, rivals such as CryptoLoot and WebMinePool remain, but new campaigns have shifted to other monetisation.

# Key points

- Firefox add-on outage (May 4, 2019): an intermediate certificate that signed many add-ons expired; Firefox verifies signatures at load, so add-ons were disabled; Firefox 66.0.4 fixed it. Steve's workaround: briefly set the system date back one day before launching Firefox. He stresses roots are long-lived while intermediates limit exposure of the root key.
- Google auto-delete: options to keep data until manually deleted, 18 months or 3 months for Location History and Web and App Activity; "pause" rather than off; links to myactivity.google.com and the Maps timeline.
- Volodya (aka BuggiCorp): Ukrainian-linked seller of Windows zero-days for about three years, with prices up to $200,000, to at least three cyberespionage groups and crime gangs (including the Fancy Bear/APT28 group earlier), also selling one-day exploits derived from patches; Kaspersky believes he wrote the Win32k zero-day fixed in March.
- Mozilla policy effective June 10, 2019: extensions may be minified or machine-generated but must supply human-readable source and a reproducible build; deliberately obfuscated code is rejected, following Google's January 2019 rule (70% of malicious extensions used obfuscation).
- Subby (interviewed by Ankit Anubhav of NewSky Security): used honeypots to capture botnet C2 addresses, scanned for management ports and brute forced root and short passwords; broke into at least 29 C2 servers, claimed about 25,000 unique devices and nearly 300 Gbit/s of attack capacity; operators were novices following tutorials who left default credentials or forgot to replace dummy C2 addresses.
- D-Link DCS-2132L (ESET): video travels unencrypted both camera-to-cloud and cloud-to-viewer through a proprietary TCP tunnel on port 2048, firmware updates are unauthenticated, and D-Link took no action in six months; Steve says to remove or avoid pointing it at sensitive areas. Leo notes the Wyze cam uses TLS with per-device keys.
- Bug bounty flows by country: US offers about $16 million, Canada 1.2 million; recipients led by the US (4.15 million), India (3.1 million), Australia and Russia.
- Edge: Google Docs flags the Chromium Edge user-agent as unsupported; Google Earth does not run because it depends on Portable Native Client, with WebAssembly port pending. Build 2019 promised tracker-blocking privacy controls, IE11 mode and Windows 7 support.
- Cryptojacking after Coinhive: Malwarebytes still sees about 50,000 daily attempts at Coinhive domains from uncleaned sites and routers, more than one million daily blocked CryptoLoot requests, newer router injections using WebMinePool, and fewer new mining campaigns as attackers move to browser locks, fake updates, malvertising and web skimmers; Steve sees a consent-based miner as an interesting alternative to ads.

# Other segments

- Picture of the week: engineering flowchart built from wood, WD-40 and duct tape.
- Filemail limits uploads per day, so Steve again recommends Firefox Send (encrypted in the browser, 1 GB or 2.5 GB with account).
- Leo and Steve discuss Microsoft's shift toward Chromium, a Linux kernel in Windows and a tabbed Windows console; ShieldsUP! has tested over 102 million shields.
