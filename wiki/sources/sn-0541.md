# Summary

Because the previous week was a holiday replay of the Vitamin D episode, this episode catches up on two weeks of news. Steve shows Google's IPv6 adoption chart (about 10 percent after 20 years of the protocol), reports that the "Get Windows 10" setting re-enables itself for some listeners, and compares browser and OS market shares. He explains that Windows 10 and 8.1 device encryption automatically back up disk recovery keys to the user's Microsoft account, which Matthew Green criticises but Steve sees as a deliberate recoverability tradeoff lacking clear notice and opt-out. A VentureBeat count of vulnerabilities by product is, Steve argues, misleading because severity matters. Google fixed more critical Android Mediaserver flaws; the first JavaScript-based ransomware kit (Ransom32, built on NW.js) appeared as a ransomware-as-a-service offering; the Wi-Fi Alliance finalized 802.11ah (HaLow) for low-power long-range IoT; and researchers showed smartwatch motion sensors can leak typed digits. GRC switched to SHA-256 certificates. Other discussion covers Let's Encrypt passing a quarter million certificates, a joke Mozilla CA application, messaging fragmentation, a LastPass 4.0 emergency access feature in response to a listener's secret-sharing question, and Star Trek creator Gene Roddenberry's floppy disks being recovered.

# Key points

- IPv6 is 20 years old; Google's measurement shows roughly 10 percent adoption with the US strongest; ISPs allocate huge address blocks per customer.
- GWX: listeners report Windows 7 and 8.1 "get Windows 10" settings being turned back on and the 30-day rollback failing; GWX Control Panel remains Steve's recommended countermeasure. Microsoft's marketing chief was reportedly aware of the backlash.
- Market share: Windows 10 climbing to roughly 10 to 12 percent, Windows 7 still the largest; browser share since 2008 shows IE falling to about 14 percent and Chrome above 50 percent.
- Windows 10 device encryption uploads recovery keys to the user's Microsoft account by default (feature dating to Windows 8.1); users can delete the uploaded copy or use a local account and rotate the key; Steve compares with SQRL's one-time rescue code.
- Vulnerability counts for 2015 put Mac OS X, iOS and Flash at the top, but OpenSSL had few entries yet great impact; counts penalise vendors who disclose and double count shared Windows code.
- Android monthly bulletin again patched critical remote-execution flaws in the Mediaserver library reachable via multimedia messages; Steve wants phones to receive continuous security updates like desktops.
- Ransom32: ransomware written in JavaScript on NW.js with an executable wrapper, sold as a service; it could be ported to Linux and macOS. Steve notes JavaScript outside a browser is unsandboxed.
- Sysinternals SigCheck 2.4 beta adds non-Microsoft root certificate checks, useful for auditing the root store.
- Let's Encrypt has issued over 250,000 certificates; Steve recounts a spoof Mozilla root-program application from "Honest Achmed" that was rejected.
- Side channels: a smartwatch's motion sensor can infer keypad entries, joining other side channels such as lip reading, power analysis and electromagnetic eavesdropping.
- 802.11ah HaLow: sub-gigahertz Wi-Fi with up to about 1 km range, roughly 100 kbps, mesh relaying, power management and contention minimization for IoT devices.
- GRC replaced SHA-1 certificates with SHA-256 just before year end; older XP-era clients can no longer reach GRC.
- Messaging fragmentation: Leo suggests bridging clients; Steve notes closed platforms resist it.
- LastPass 4.0 introduced emergency access with a delayed approval to let a trusted person request your vault; Steve will review its design and explains Shamir's secret sharing as an alternative.
- Star Trek's Gene Roddenberry's 5.25-inch disks from custom word processors were recovered by a data-recovery firm after it reverse-engineered the format.
- Steve and Leo discuss that the term "law enforcement" in his comments loosely includes intelligence agencies.

# Other segments

- Reminder about the SQRL rescue code concept, TV and film notes (Colony, The Expanse, Mr. Robot), a Twitter-following app that avoids needing an account, and Leo's travel-router firewall and shipboard satellite Internet.
- Elaine's transcription praised; listener Q&A planned for next week.
