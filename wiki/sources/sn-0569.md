# Summary

A second Ninth Circuit panel (Facebook v. Power Ventures) acknowledged that users can authorise others to use their credentials, easing fears from the Nosal ruling, but the EFF wants en banc review of the vague Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. A House report found the FDIC was repeatedly breached from China in 2010 to 2013 and that its CIO suppressed reporting, which Steve uses to argue for auditing lines independent of executives. Long-time Tor contributor Lucky Green announced he would shut down the Tonga bridge authority and other relays without explanation. Steve reflects on how AI assistants require broad personal data. A listener released CertWatch, a freeware tool Steve and Leo had wished for, which alerts on changes to the Windows certificate stores. Vectra Networks showed that Windows' 20-year-old Point-and-Print feature installs kernel printer drivers from any printer or fake printer without prompts, even via Internet Printing Protocol, and Microsoft could only add a prompt. Juniper's Junos had a flaw that accepted self-signed certificates whose issuer name matched a trusted CA. The main topics are Messenger's Secret Conversations extras (message franking for abuse reports and encrypted on-device storage), the CryptoDrop ransomware detector (which looked much better after reading the paper), and MIT's Riffle anonymity network, which Steve judges impractical.

# Key points

- CFAA: Facebook v. Power Ventures recognises that valid credential delegation can be authorisation; EFF still calls for en banc review because "authorization" is undefined.
- FDIC: Chinese attackers compromised executive workstations and servers; incidents went unreported to US-CERT; former CIO accused of misleading auditors; separate lapses involved USB drives holding personal data.
- Tor: Lucky Green will permanently shut the Tonga bridge authority on August 31 and destroy its keys; bridge authority IPs are embedded in Tor software; no explanation given, a month after Jacob Appelbaum's resignation.
- AI assistants (Siri, Google Assistant, Alexa, Cortana, Facebook chatbots) improve with context, so they will need continuous broad data collection.
- CertWatch scans Windows certificate stores hourly and reports additions and removals, addressing MITM certificates installed by software or malware.
- Point-and-Print (Windows 95 onward): clients silently install drivers from a network printer, bypassing UAC and running with system rights; Vectra demonstrated infecting through a rogue printer, a compromised print server or remote IPP; Microsoft added a prompt but cannot remove the behavior; admins can disable Point-and-Print by policy; Steve compares it to the old metafile design flaw.
- Juniper Junos PKId bug: a self-signed certificate naming a trusted CA issuer could bypass peer validation; no known exploitation.
- Errata: the Cisco SG300 uses an IOS-like CLI but not IOS itself.
- Ubiquiti EdgeRouter X (about $50): five routed interfaces, up to about 500 Mbit/s routing, PPTP and IPsec support, no Wi-Fi; Steve calls it the affordable answer for isolating IoT networks, replacing multiple "dumb routers" or a managed switch.
- Message franking: the sender creates a random nonce, HMAC-tags the message to make a franking tag, encrypts both; Facebook's server adds a reporting tag (an HMAC over the franking tag and sender, recipient and time metadata under its own key); the recipient can later report with plaintext, nonce and tags so Facebook can verify the sender sent exactly that without ever having seen it.
- Messenger storage: messages are protected with AES-GCM using a local on-device key wrapped by a remote per-user key delivered after Facebook login; disappearing messages merely hide content after a timestamp.
- WhatsApp was blocked again in Brazil.
- CryptoDrop: monitors file type changes, similarity-hash drops and Shannon entropy rises; tested on hundreds of ransomware families it detected all of them, often with zero or few files lost, with 7-Zip the only false positive among 30 apps; availability unclear.
- Riffle (MIT): shuffles equal-sized traffic from all participants to defeat traffic analysis, which makes it very slow and impractical compared with Tor; Steve notes Tor's main weakness is a state-level observer correlating traffic.
