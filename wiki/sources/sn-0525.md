# Summary

Steve opens with a correction: glass is not a fluid, and old window panes are thicker at the bottom because of how crown glass was made. He then defuses worry about a planned Black Hat Europe talk on stealing LastPass data: it relies on malware already running on the machine and a "remember password" convenience, so the advice is to avoid that mode or protect it with biometrics, a tradeoff Steve also designed into SQRL with a short re-entry hint. He quotes Matthew Green's explanation that iMessage is end-to-end encrypted but depends on Apple's directory supplying correct public keys, so key management equals trust. Canary Tokens are praised as a free tripwire service in the spirit of cartographers' fake map towns. Let's Encrypt issued its first certificates on schedule with general availability expected in mid-November. The main segment is an interview with Patrick Jackson, cofounder and CTO of Disconnect, who explains that his product aims to educate and block third-party tracking rather than all ads, protects mobile apps via a local configuration profile, and is working with the EFF on Do Not Track. Jackson predicts big changes within six months as iOS 9 content blockers arrive, and Leo continues to worry about the open web.

# Key points

- **Glass myth:** old panes were uneven from the crown glass spinning process, not from flowing; Steve also realizes glass hard disk platters could not be spun if glass flowed.
- **LastPass at Black Hat Europe:** research requires an already compromised machine and the "remember password" feature; countermeasures are not remembering the password or requiring a fingerprint before fill; PIN-only schemes are brute-forceable.
- **SQRL compromise:** after a full password entry (a roughly five-second EnScrypt), later unlocks use only the first few password characters with a one-second hash and wipe on any error.
- **iMessage:** end-to-end with RSA and AES and ECDSA signatures; trust depends on Apple's key server returning only the intended recipients' keys; Steve says it is secure enough for casual use, while Threema leaves key management with users.
- **Canary Tokens (canarytokens.org):** generate tripwire links or documents that notify you when opened; self-hosting recommended; Leo keeps a decoy passwords file in cloud folders. Map-maker "trap streets" are the historical analogue.
- **Let's Encrypt:** first certificates issued in early September; staged ramp-up to general availability around mid-November; covers only domain-validated certificates.
- **Disconnect interview:** the product shows real-time counts of third-party connections and blocks malicious, unexpected third-party content while leaving first-party sponsors alone; relationships with plugins mean each embedded widget adds an unvetted party; sites with dozens of trackers are often unaware; iOS app uses an on-device configuration profile (blocking, not proxying) to cover apps; the Android app was pulled by Google and an EU complaint was filed; the company is working with the EFF to make Do Not Track meaningful; Jackson expects ad networks to move toward opt-in and native advertising.
- **Open web debate:** Leo argues blockers may push content into apps; Jackson replies that app-level blocking is also possible; Leo concedes nobody is entitled to track users without consent.
- **Ad ethics at TWiT:** Leo describes the IAB podcast upfront and his policy of vetting advertisers.
