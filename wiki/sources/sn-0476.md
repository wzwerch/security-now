# Summary

Steve notes that the show now spends most of its time chasing breaches. The headline is the JPMorgan Chase intrusion, disclosed through a regulatory filing: attackers (believed to be in Russia) started in June, were noticed in July, reached over 90 servers with top-level privileges and took names, addresses, email addresses and phone numbers of about 76 million households and 7 million small businesses, but apparently no passwords, card numbers or Social Security numbers; reports said nine other financial institutions were also hit. The hackers methodically inventoried installed software and searched for unpatched flaws. A headline that Yahoo servers were owned through Shellshock turned out to be a different command-injection bug in a sports team's log-parsing script, exploited by a mutated Shellshock probe. Researchers at DerbyCon published open-source firmware modification tools for Phison-based USB flash drives, reviving the BadUSB discussion, but deliberately omitted self-replication code. A Bugzilla flaw let anyone register an email address in a privileged domain without verification. Steve answers a question on how SpinRite handles uncorrectable sectors. Listener feedback covers HTTPS breaking transparent ISP caching, a Massachusetts court compelling decryption passwords, what to do with attack logs, the meaning of Ethernet, a referrer-header side effect of moving to HTTPS, MAC filtering, random MAC addresses, drive density and speed, why ECC is stronger per bit than RSA, and how to tell whether an outsourced service is truly Trust No One.

# Key points

- JPMorgan breach: data on roughly 76 million households and 7 million small businesses exfiltrated, root-level access on dozens of servers, forensic indications of Russian origin, and no evidence of fraud. The company did not have to disclose because of the lack of financial impact until a filing; attackers inventoried software and probed for known unpatched flaws.
- Yahoo: the Shellshock-related story was inaccurate; three sports API servers were hit through a separate injection bug in a monitoring script, and servers may have been used to scan for more victims.
- BadUSB follow-up: Adam Caudill and Brandon Wilson released tools to modify Phison USB controller firmware (hidden partitions, bypassing the drive's password feature) but withheld self-replicating malware code; they urge vendors to add firmware code signing, and Steve notes the released code lowers the bar somewhat.
- Bugzilla: with a non-default setup that grants rights by email domain, account creation without email verification lets outsiders obtain access to confidential bug reports.
- SpinRite on drives with unrecoverable sectors: a drive only relocates a sector it can eventually read, so SpinRite uses techniques to get a final read, accepts best-effort data, rewrites it and lets the drive remap it. A drive that shows many uncorrectable areas should be treated as untrustworthy.
- HTTPS defeats transparent ISP caching proxies, raising bandwidth costs, though CDNs mitigate this.
- A Massachusetts high court ruled a defendant could be compelled to give a decryption password when ownership was already admitted, which challenges the belief that memorised secrets are protected by the Fifth Amendment.
- Law enforcement will not take server logs from admins who see exploit attempts; agencies prioritise cases with substantial demonstrated losses.
- Ethernet is a protocol that can run over coax, twisted pair or fiber; a shared medium uses collision detection while switches learn MAC addresses.
- Moving a site to HTTPS strips the HTTP Referer header on links to non-HTTPS sites, which can hide clickthroughs from advertisers; a meta referrer tag (never, origin, default, always) lets sites control this, supported at the time by Chrome and Safari.
- MAC filtering and hidden SSIDs add little security because MAC addresses are visible on the air and easily spoofed; iOS randomisation should not break connections to known networks.
- Random MAC addresses would be workable today but the standard assumed globally unique 48-bit addresses with a manufacturer prefix.
- Higher areal density generally means higher sustained throughput, and SATA bandwidth still exceeds drive speeds.
- ECC is stronger than RSA per bit because cracking asymmetric keys is a number-theoretic problem, not brute force; smaller keys make it faster.
- To test whether a cloud vendor is truly Trust No One, ask how they would recover your data if you lost all access: if they can, they hold the keys; a real TNO provider warns that recovery is impossible.
- Windows 10 Technical Preview telemetry and keylogging disclosure is seen as normal for beta software.
