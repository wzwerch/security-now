# Summary

Steve first defends Mozilla after the uproar over Firefox's new terms of use and the removal of its "never sell your data" promise: he reads it as lawyers avoiding overbroad claims in jurisdictions where "sale" is defined widely, notes the licence wording was quickly tightened, and stays with Firefox. Signal's president Meredith Whittaker says Signal will leave Sweden if it passes a law forcing providers to give police access to message content, and the Swedish Armed Forces warn that such a law would introduce exploitable weaknesses. The Bybit follow-up shows the 1.5 billion USD theft actually began with a compromise of the multisig wallet provider Safe{Wallet}, with only about 42 million frozen so far. Mozilla reaffirms support for Manifest V2 (and thus full uBlock Origin) while Chrome retires it. The long middle segment covers an ACM call for standardizing memory-safety terminology and the ISRG "Prossimo" effort, explaining memory-safe versus unsafe languages and advising newcomers to learn Rust, Go, Swift, Python or Java. After short items (Australia's Kaspersky ban, Gmail's planned move from SMS codes to QR codes, a SpinRite recovery story, feedback), the headline topic is academic work from NDSS 2025 showing a passive Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface can jam a single chosen WiFi device while one 5 mm away keeps working.

# Key points

- Firefox: the FAQ promise "never sold your data" was dropped; Mozilla says its partner data sharing is anonymised or aggregated and that legal definitions of "sale" are very broad. A new licence clause on user-input data was reworded within hours. Steve accepts that a free browser needs revenue and says he trusts Mozilla; Leo adds Mozilla depends heavily on Google payments and has about 6 percent share.
- Whittaker repeats Signal's threat to exit (previously aimed at the UK Online Safety Act) if Sweden mandates access; the open question is what Apple does about iMessage. Steve and ChatGPT coin the acronym NOCRYPT for such laws.
- Bybit aftermath: Chainalysis helped freeze roughly 42 million of about 1.5 billion USD; the FBI attributes the theft to Lazarus (TraderTraitor). The attacker injected malicious code into Safe{Wallet}'s domain to hit Bybit's multisig flow, a managed-service/supply-chain style compromise. Bybit's bounty leaderboard (the Lazarus Bounty site) had paid only about 4,000 USD to 17 hunters.
- Mozilla's blog reaffirms support for both Manifest V2 and V3, including blockingWebRequest, the API uBlock Origin needs; Chrome's phase-out removes the full uBlock Origin there. Leo plans to try NextDNS-based filtering instead.
- ACM Communications piece (21 authors) argues for a shared framework for specifying memory safety so buyers can require it. Steve predicts procurement rules will soon demand memory-safe code and that suppliers will be held liable.
- MemorySafety.org / ISRG Prossimo: Rust TLS to replace OpenSSL, Rust drivers for Linux, Hickory DNS resolver, memory-safe AV1, zlib, sudo, NTP, curl and Apache work. Memory-safe languages include Rust, Go, C#, Java, Swift, Python and JavaScript; unsafe ones are C, C++ and assembly.
- Bug classes explained: out-of-bounds read/write and use-after-free. Statistics quoted: about 70 percent of Microsoft vulnerabilities, 60 to 70 percent in iOS/macOS and about 90 percent in Android are memory-safety issues; Slammer, WannaCry, Heartbleed, Stagefright and Ghost are cited examples. Memory-safe languages also help stability and multithreading.
- Steve argues C is arguably more dangerous than assembly because the compiler can surprise the programmer, but advises newcomers to adopt Rust, Go, Swift, Python or Java.
- Australia ordered government bodies to remove Kaspersky software by 1 April, citing foreign-interference risk without public evidence of wrongdoing.
- Gmail plans QR-code sign-in instead of SMS codes; Steve notes the reports lack detail and a QR code alone does not stop an attacker who scans their own, so it likely implies a companion app (like his SQRL design).
- A listener's dead 4 TB Kingston portable SSD was revived by a SpinRite Level 3 pass (three days under DOS); Steve says he has moved all GRC servers back to spinning disks after SSD failures but with RAID 6 and NAS backups. Kingston firmware once froze drives after 2,982 hours of uptime.
- Listener feedback: disabling ADP uploads the keys to Apple so Apple can decrypt again; 0patch offers 5 years of Windows 10 micro-patches (about 27 USD per year); Entrust is leaving the CA business after Chrome distrusted its certificates and its customers moved to Sectigo.
- Research: "Spatial-Domain Wireless Jamming with Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces" (NDSS 2025). A passive, low-cost printed-circuit RIS first listens to a victim's transmissions to infer position, then reflects ambient radio so the jamming energy is cancelled or concentrated at chosen locations. It denied service to one WiFi device while another at 5 mm kept 25 Mbit/s. It is protocol-agnostic and hard to detect because the surface itself emits nothing; Steve notes there is no action item but intelligence agencies will take interest.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a USB-C charging cable plugged into an AC wall socket slot.
- Tool tip: SoftPerfect NetWorx for watching router traffic with a logarithmic graph; Steve mentions using Performance Monitor remotely over a private path rather than exposing SMB.
- Listener note that Viscount's FREEDOM access-control system is used in US DHS field offices (follow-up to the previous episode).
- Banter on free services versus paying for software, micropayments, and Steve turning 70 soon.
