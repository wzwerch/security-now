# Summary

Steve revisits the Texas ransomware story and now suspects the 22 affected towns shared an outsourced IT and payment-services provider rather than falling to a coordinated multi-target campaign. He then covers the Kazakhstan government's attempt to force citizens to install its own root certificate for interception, which Mozilla, Google and Apple answered by blocking that certificate. The main security stories are two open-source supply chain incidents: a backdoor planted in the Webmin build (CVE-2019-15107, exposed by a no-notice DEF CON disclosure) and a second batch of backdoored RubyGems libraries. Google announces it will build breach-notification into Chrome, Apple fixes a regressed jailbreak, and Microsoft patches its Android RDP client. After SQRL and file-sync updates, the final half is a detailed look at Google's Privacy Sandbox explainers (conversion measurement, Trust Tokens, privacy budget, FLoC, identity sharding) and Apple's competing tracking-prevention work. Steve sees promise in profiling without tracking, while Leo argues that cohort targeting still enables manipulation and that Google's 52% revenue figure is weak evidence.

# Key points

- Texas: the ransom demand reported was $2.5 million; the number of affected municipalities fell to 22; two towns (Borger, Keene) said utility payments were down and one mayor blamed an outsourced IT provider, so Steve concludes a single managed service provider was the entry point.
- Kazakhstan: ISPs told customers to install a government root certificate that was used to intercept traffic to roughly three dozen social and communication sites; Firefox, Chrome (via CRLSet and Chromium source) and Safari blocked the root; Mozilla advised affected users to remove it and change passwords.
- Webmin: a backdoor in the build infrastructure (visible in releases 1.882 through 1.921, downloaded from SourceForge) allowed unauthenticated root command execution via the password-reset page; it was on by default only in 1.890 (about 421,000 downloads); about 218,000 Internet-exposed instances, 13,000+ on the default-vulnerable version, and exploitation began the day after the disclosure. Steve criticizes the unannounced disclosure and notes admin portals should never be public.
- RubyGems: 11 libraries with a second backdoor (preset credentials, environment-variable exfiltration, a crypto-miner), about 3,600 downloads; rest-client versions were backdoored via an old developer account with a cracked password.
- Supply chain trend: earlier cases include event-stream, bootstrap-sass and strong_password; HD Moore warns that stolen tokens and keys enable repeat attacks and that default-to-latest dependency tools raise the risk.
- Chrome will warn when logging into sites that suffered breaches; Google's Password Checkup study found 1.5% of logins compromised and 26% of warned users changed passwords.
- iOS 12.4 reintroduced a previously fixed jailbreak; fixed in 12.4.1.
- Microsoft updated its Remote Desktop client for Android after server-to-client attack research.
- Privacy Sandbox: Google argues cookie blocking pushes advertisers toward fingerprinting and cites a 52% publisher revenue drop; Leo cites academic work suggesting about 4% uplift from cookie-based targeting and a newspaper that switched to contextual ads without losing revenue.
- Proposed APIs include conversion measurement, Trust Tokens (based on Cloudflare's Privacy Pass), a privacy budget against fingerprinting, and FLoC cohorts; Apple's Intelligent Tracking Prevention releases and ad-click attribution proposal are the contrast.
- Leo's concern: cohorts can still be used to target vulnerable groups; he doubts the standards process will deliver, recalling Do Not Track.
