# Summary

With GRC back online, Steve ran a full Q&A show. He summarised Apple's formal court reply to the FBI's All Writs Act demand, noted that a different judge had just refused a similar order for a drug-case iPhone, and expected the issue to land with Congress. He and Leo debated what a passcode protects: Apple's key derivation takes about 80 ms per guess and cannot be sped up, so a long passphrase defeats brute force, but Leo argued that any compelled firmware change could instead exfiltrate data once the phone is unlocked, so strong phone security is inconvenient and ultimately depends on trusting the vendor. A forensics researcher offered a formal definition of "backdoor" that Steve adopted. A vendor analysis found most critical Microsoft vulnerabilities in 2015 would be mitigated by removing administrative rights. Steve recommended a classic scanner for probing IoT devices on one's own LAN. Listener questions covered containing the browser, corporate TLS inspection, an outbound-port-restricted variant of the router isolation scheme, where to draw the line for IoT, NAS placement, paid firewall subscriptions, IPv6 and NAT, DHCP with chained routers, and a Chrome flag to mark HTTP as insecure.

# Key points

- Apple's reply argues the order would force creation of a crippled, less secure OS, that "just this once" is untrue given other pending requests, that Congress rather than a closed courtroom should decide, and that all-writs reasoning could extend to turning on microphones or cameras. A New York judge denied a similar order the day before.
- Definition quoted from a forensics researcher: a backdoor is a component active without the owner's consent, subverting undisclosed purposes, under an undisclosed party's control. Steve says the FBI request is not a backdoor under that definition but an easier way to brute force.
- Apple's security paper: the passcode is entangled with a device-unique key and iterated so one attempt takes about 80 ms; escalating delays and the ten-strike wipe are software measures. A long alphanumeric passcode makes brute force impractical, but updates, backups and keyboards remain trust points.
- Avecto analysis of 2015 Patch Tuesday: about 85 percent of critical vulnerabilities mitigated by removing admin rights, 99.5 percent of Internet Explorer ones; yet vulnerability volume rose 52 percent year over year.
- Steve gives a history of admin versus non-admin rights from Unix to Windows and Vista, and notes he runs Firefox with reduced rights via DropMyRights.
- Internal port scanning of IoT devices: the Foundstone SuperScan tool (Windows) or Nmap on Unix can reveal which ports devices open.
- Upcoming topics: the DROWN attack on obsolete SSLv2 support and a cache-timing attack on OpenSSL (CacheBleed).
- SlySoft (AnyDVD) shut down under legal pressure.
- Listener Q&A, browser containment: NoScript is impractical, uBlock Origin helps, Sandboxie fails against read-only data theft, virtual machines are better but cumbersome; Qubes and Tails mentioned.
- Corporate TLS-inspection appliances are defensible in a workplace but employees should avoid personal banking on them.
- A clever variant: put the untrusted IoT router behind the safe one and let it block all outbound traffic except ports 80 and 443; this requires a router with outbound filtering.
- Where to draw the IoT line: anything that works on the isolated network (Roku, Blu-ray players, bulbs) should live there.
- NAS placement: lay drives flat, keep them cool with airflow, watch for slowing performance as an early warning rather than listening.
- Firewall subscriptions may be pointless if the device cannot see inside TLS traffic.
- IPv6: consumer routers may pass IPv6 through with no translation, so a stateful border firewall is needed; Mac OS firewall is off by default; ShieldsUP! for IPv6 comes after SpinRite 6.1.
- Chained routers must use separately numbered networks; manual WAN addressing fixes DHCP collisions without reducing isolation.
- Chrome had a flag to mark non-secure origins as insecure.
- DVD players cannot protect keys, but a phone depends on a secret the user supplies, so only guessing works.
