# Summary

Steve marks Let's Encrypt issuing its billionth certificate and uses the history of slow HTTPS adoption to argue that automated domain-validated certificates still invite look-alike phishing domains, wishing browsers would someday show whether a human verified the certificate holder. Pakistan's proposed online-content rules, demanding 24-hour takedowns and local servers, led Facebook, Google and Twitter (via the Asia Internet Coalition) to threaten withdrawal. A data breach exposed Clearview AI's customer list, showing wide use by law enforcement and some companies. Switzerland moved against Crypto AG after the CIA/West German ownership revelations, and Apache Tomcat servers needed urgent patching for the Ghostcat AJP flaw. Steve revisits OCSP stapling, finding Must-Staple was the right fix but browsers and servers never implemented it, and Leo notes Let's Encrypt had to revoke millions of certificates after a CAA bug. After a SpinRite AHCI progress note, the main topic is Kr00k, where WiFi chips from Broadcom and Cypress send leftover buffered data encrypted with an all-zero key after a disassociation, affecting over a billion devices.

# Key points

- HTTPS history: introduced 1994, about 7 percent of top sites in 2015, crossed 50 percent only around 2018; drivers were Snowden, Firesheep-style session theft, Google ranking signals, browser warnings and free Let's Encrypt/ACME certificates (90-day lifetimes).
- Let's Encrypt only issues domain-validated certificates; a 2017 study found about 15,000 PayPal look-alike certificates among its output; Steve wants human-verified certificates distinguished.
- Pakistan's "Citizens Protection Against Online Harm Rules 2020": remove flagged content within 24 hours, local offices and servers, fines up to 500 million rupees; the AIC warned services could become unavailable to about 70 million users; India is considering similar rules.
- Clearview AI breach: customer list leaked; BuzzFeed reported users including ICE, DOJ, FBI, CBP, Interpol trials, and companies such as Bank of America, Walmart, Macy's and others (several denied); law enforcement customers in dozens of countries.
- Crypto AG: Swiss export ban on its products, a former Supreme Court judge to investigate, and a criminal complaint against unknown persons.
- Ghostcat (CVE-2020-1938): Apache Tomcat's AJP connector, exposed by default across versions 6 to 9 over 13 years, allows file read and, with uploads, code execution; roughly 890,000 to over a million servers visible; mass scanning started; upgrade 7.x, 8.x or 9.x.
- OCSP Must-Staple: a flag in the certificate forcing browsers to demand a fresh stapled response; at the time only Firefox honoured it and Apache and nginx implementations were weak; with it, very long certificate lives could be safe; Let's Encrypt was revoking about three million certificates over a CAA checking bug, which Chrome would not even notice.
- Kr00k (CVE-2019-15126, ESET): on disassociation the WiFi chip zeroes the session key but still transmits up to 32 KB of buffered frames using the all-zero key, so they are effectively plaintext at the WPA2 layer; affects Broadcom and Cypress chips in Amazon, Apple, Google, Samsung, Raspberry Pi, Xiaomi devices and Asus and Huawei access points; Cisco listed at least 14 affected products; HTTPS or a VPN still protects content.
- SpinRite: AHCI driver work has started; USB, NVMe and UEFI come later.
