# Summary

Steve reports a backdoor in many off-brand routers using the built-in username and password "super," found by researchers who reverse-engineered firmware and counted over 200,000 reachable devices with WAN administration enabled; he again urges replacing router firmware with open alternatives such as Tomato, DD-WRT or OpenWRT. He shows InstantCryptor, a browser-based file encryption service for Dropbox and Google Drive that encrypts with AES-CBC but does not authenticate, so a wrong password yields garbage instead of an error, and explains the encrypt-then-MAC fix. Short news items cover threats against Twitter's founder by Islamic State supporters, Google's 25 million dollar purchase of the .app top-level domain and its plans for private TLDs like .dev, and Google's "knowledge-based trust" research that would rank pages by factual accuracy. Steve also remembers Leonard Nimoy and a conversation with Gene Roddenberry, reviews "Citizenfour," and promotes the Podcast Awards. The main feature is an interview with Lee Pike and Pat Hickey of Galois, who helped demonstrate the 60 Minutes car hack under DARPA's HACMS program. They explain that modern cars run dozens of ECU computers on an unauthenticated CAN bus, can be attacked through telematics modems, media players, diagnostic tools and keyless entry, that manufacturers are cost sensitive, and that formal methods, which mathematically prove software correct, offer a path to secure embedded systems.

# Key points

- Router backdoor: firmware in cheap routers from brands such as Digicom, Alpha Network, Pro-Link and Planet Networks accepts "super" for username and password, with remote administration on by default; over 200,000 exposed devices were found. Steve says router firmware is too critical to leave to manufacturers and recommends open-source replacements and disabling WAN administration.
- InstantCryptor: encrypts in the browser with a SHA-256 password hash and AES-256-CBC but lacks authentication, so it cannot detect a wrong password or tampering; the fix is to add an HMAC over the ciphertext, verified before decryption, and to harden the password into a key.
- Islamic State supporters posted a death threat against Twitter co-founder Jack Dorsey after accounts were removed; Leo notes the group is media savvy.
- gTLDs: Google paid 25 million dollars for .app; Amazon bought .buy and .spot; Google applied for about 100 TLDs including .blog, .dev, .cloud and wants .dev for itself; the auctions fund ICANN.
- Knowledge Vault and knowledge-based trust: Google researchers describe a fact base of 1.6 billion facts, 271 million rated confident, and a proposal to rank sites by factual correctness; Leo worries about who decides what is true.
- Car hacking background: modern cars have up to 50 ECUs and economy cars about 20 networked over the CAN bus, an 1980s broadcast bus with no authentication and 8-byte payloads, so any compromised unit can impersonate others such as the brake pedal sensor.
- Attack paths: physical access to the diagnostic port, malicious media or CDs exploiting buffer overflows, dealer diagnostic computers connected to the Internet, and remote telematics cell modems (a University of Washington/UCSD attack used audio tones to exploit a modem and reprogram firmware, and cars' phone numbers sit in predictable blocks); key fob and keyless entry theft already occurs; microphones can eavesdrop; attacks on privacy and tracking matter as well as safety.
- No known in-the-wild attacks beyond key fob theft; the same suppliers feed many makes, so a flaw can span brands. Cost pressure rules out adding security chips to every ECU; safety and security can conflict (unlocking doors after a crash).
- Formal methods: Galois builds provably correct embedded software for DARPA's HACMS, with computers checking proofs, eliminating bugs like buffer overflows; static analysis tools are helpful but noisy and operate at too low a level; design-level verification (as Amazon uses for distributed algorithms) is valuable.
- Steve's takeaway: regulation likely needs a catastrophe to drive change; connected, autonomous and vehicle-to-vehicle systems add attack surface and privacy risks (tracking via V2V broadcasts); Leo notes the move to drive-by-wire removes mechanical fallbacks.
- Referenced paper: "Securing the Automobile: A Comprehensive Approach."
