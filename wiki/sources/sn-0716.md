# Summary

Most of the episode follows the BlueKeep saga, now named, in its second week. Multiple firms (McAfee, Check Point, Kaspersky, Zerodium, ESET commentary, an independent analyst) confirmed private working exploits without publishing code, while McAfee's write-up described a flaw in how the MS_T120 virtual channel can be bound to a channel other than 31, causing heap corruption. Robert Graham scanned the Internet and found about 950,000 vulnerable hosts; GreyNoise saw scanning from Tor exit nodes. Steve repeats that RDP belongs behind a VPN and argues that legal barriers prevent vendors or white hats from proactively closing such holes in memory, even though 0patch showed a no-reboot micropatch is feasible. Earlier he covers SandboxEscaper dumping several unpatched Windows privilege-escalation zero-days after a public outburst, Google's admission that G Suite admin-set passwords were stored unhashed since 2005, the Windows 10 May 2019 Update with Windows Sandbox, Firefox 67 features, and research showing more than half of validly signed malware used Comodo/Sectigo certificates.

# Key points

- SandboxEscaper released about five Windows 10 zero-days within days (Task Scheduler-related privilege escalation, Windows Error Reporting, IE11 issue, more); one was already fixed; all local-only privilege escalations that matter when chained; she cited grievance against the FBI and a wish to sell to non-Western buyers; Forbes coverage also raised mental health concerns.
- Google disclosed that G Suite admin console stored unhashed passwords (a 2005 design error, enterprise accounts only) and a separate January 2019 incident lasting up to 14 days; no evidence of misuse. Steve notes similar Facebook and Twitter admissions and pitches SQRL's "no secrets to keep" model.
- Windows 10 version 1903 (May 2019 Update): Windows Sandbox (Pro and above, needs virtualization and ample RAM, fresh image each launch, uses Hyper-V), light theme, update deferral controls, Search/Cortana split; Leo advises waiting for Microsoft to offer it.
- Firefox 67: lower memory use and faster rendering, unloading of least-recently-used tabs under memory pressure, optional fingerprinter and cryptominer blocking, per-extension control in private windows, WebRender GPU rollout beginning on Windows 10 with NVIDIA cards.
- Chronicle research on VirusTotal found 3,815 filtered malware samples signed with valid certificates in one year; Comodo/Sectigo issued 51.3 percent; around 21 percent of the certificates were later revoked; Steve explains timestamped code signing and why new certificates start with no reputation.
- BlueKeep confirmations: McAfee, Check Point (crash proof of concept), Kaspersky, Zerodium, MalwareTech and another analyst; NLA blocks unauthenticated exploitation; changing the default port is not security.
- RiskSense released a scanner/Metasploit module; Graham's rdpscan found about 923,000 to 950,000 vulnerable hosts out of 7.6 million port-3389 responders.
- GreyNoise saw sweeping BlueKeep scans from Tor exit nodes, likely a single actor compiling target lists.
- Steve's policy argument: lawful, overseen proactive patching of critical wormable flaws should be possible; he cites the 0patch 30-byte in-memory fix.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a ransomware timeline map through 2016.
- SQRL talk at OWASP Los Angeles went well; plans to use diagrams instead of slides; Dublin OWASP invitation confirmed.
- Leo ran the rdpscan tool on TWiT's network and found no open RDP.
