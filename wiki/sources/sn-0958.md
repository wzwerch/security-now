# Summary

Steve opens with Microsoft's disclosure that the Russian group Midnight Blizzard (formerly Nobelium) used a password-spray attack against a legacy, non-production test tenant account in late November 2023, then used that account's permissions to read email of senior leaders and cybersecurity and legal staff. The breach was detected on 12 January 2024 and disclosed on a Friday night. Steve and Leo see the lesson as "forgotten legacy systems are the weakest link" and criticise the PR-style wording. Next, an unidentified actor stole about $7.5 million of civilian grant payments from the US Department of Health and Human Services via spear-phishing. Mozilla published an issue tracker ("Platform Tilt") documenting how Apple, Google and Microsoft disadvantage Firefox. Brave announced it will retire its Strict fingerprinting-protection mode, which Steve explains in terms of "farbling" (small noise added to fingerprintable Web APIs). CISA's Ransomware Vulnerability Warning Pilot reported about 1,200 early-ransomware notifications and 1,700 vulnerable-system notifications in its first year. Samsung's S24 gets seven years of updates, Google reworded Chrome's Incognito notice, and Steve shares a SpinRite 6.1 status report about buggy BIOSes and USB drives above 137GB. The second half is listener feedback on AI-faked media, learning to program, an abandoned flashlight app turning into a subscription trap, Privacy Sandbox, the hacked LG washer, and why backbone ISPs null-route DDoS targets.

# Key points

- Microsoft / Midnight Blizzard: password spray against a legacy test tenant without MFA gave a foothold; the account's excessive permissions reached a small percentage of corporate mailboxes (leadership, security, legal). Microsoft says no product vulnerability, no customer, source-code or AI-system access. Microsoft promised to apply current security standards to legacy systems even at the cost of disruption. Steve's lesson: attackers go for the weakest entry point, and old systems need their security revisited (compare LastPass's stale PBKDF iteration counts).
- Critics noted the Friday-night disclosure, the timing right after the Secure Future Initiative announcement, and the earlier Storm-0558 (China) breach four months before; Microsoft hosts the Ukrainian government network on Azure.
- HHS grant-payment system: spear-phishing between March and November 2023 hijacked payments to five grant recipients ($7.5M). Steve's advice: training plus strong outbound filtering (he cites ADAMnetworks) to neuter clicked links.
- Mozilla "Platform Tilt" tracker: Apple (no third-party engines on iOS, JIT, default-browser handling, extensions, etc.), Google/Android (no browser-data import, Google app opens Chrome, degraded Google Search result pages for non-Chrome user agents) and Microsoft/Windows (no programmatic default-browser prompt, Edge pushed as "recommended", Windows Search, Copilot and widgets always open Edge). Steve sees pervasive pettiness; Apple's security argument is partly legitimate.
- Brave retires Strict fingerprinting mode in version 1.64: it breaks sites, under 0.5 percent of users used it, and those users stand out and are easier to fingerprint. Standard mode keeps "farbling": subtle noise in Canvas, WebGL, audio, user-agent, plugins, hardware concurrency, device enumeration and dark-mode APIs. Strict replaced values with fully random ones.
- CISA RVWP (started 30 Jan 2023): scanning of critical-infrastructure systems for ransomware-linked vulnerabilities; about 1,200 notices of early-stage ransomware activity and 1,700 of vulnerable systems in 2023 (roughly eight per day). Steve strongly supports good-guy scanning.
- Samsung S24 line gets seven years of OS and security updates, matching Google; Steve sees update longevity becoming a competitive advantage.
- Google changed the Chrome Incognito disclaimer after the lawsuit to say sites and services (including Google) can still collect data.
- Listener Q: detecting AI-faked media. Steve: "not all problems have good solutions"; camera-signing schemes (Canon, Content Authenticity Initiative) will be cracked like DVD keys. Future generations will simply not trust what they see.
- Listener Q: how to start programming. Steve and Leo recommend Python, CS50, "How to Think Like a Computer Scientist" and "How to Design Programs"; the key is to start building.
- Listener Q: a popular Android flashlight app (1M+ downloads) suddenly showed video ads and a very expensive subscription. Steve's guess: the developer sold the app and keys to a monetisation scammer.
- Listener Qs on Google Privacy Sandbox: sites cannot learn visitor interests (unlike FLoC); Protected Audience and Fenced Frames partition storage but do not protect against malvertising; keep uBlock Origin.
- LG smart washer botnet follow-up: could be via exploitable embedded IP stacks, UPnP, a compromised router or a known device flaw; outbound firewall rules (for example in pfSense) can block such devices.
- Why ISPs like Cogent simply null-route DDoS targets: modern attacks are non-spoofable HTTPS floods that need specialised services such as Cloudflare to filter.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a glass-panelled door with a peephole.
- SpinRite 6.1: AMI BIOS bug patched so USB drives over 137GB work; HP and Lenovo BIOSes turned out buggy (illegal-opcode crashes), new plan to follow; SpinRite 7 will not depend on the BIOS; ValiDrive and ShieldsUP! mentioned.
- Aside on VR/Vision Pro, the film "Brainstorm", Bitwarden renewal ($10/year), and iOS not showing images in the previous show-notes PDFs (fixed).
