# Summary

Steve titles this pure-news episode at his wife's suggestion. Dropbox disclosed that a phishing campaign impersonating CircleCI captured a developer's GitHub credentials and hardware-key one-time code, letting an attacker copy 130 code repositories (no customer data or core infrastructure); Steve praises the fast response and coins the "phishing email attack surface" that grows with every outsourced service. OpenSSL's two CVE-2022-3602 and CVE-2022-3786 flaws in 3.0.x were explained and rated high rather than critical. The FTC settled with ed-tech company Chegg after four breaches of about 40 million consumers' sensitive data caused by basic lapses. FinCEN counted about $1.2 billion in reported ransomware payments in 2021, Akamai described reusable phishing toolkits, Kela priced initial access broker listings, and Group-IB/Orange described the OPERA1ER group's patient African bank heists using about 400 money mules. Further items: more DeFi thefts, Russia choosing a Linux replacement for Windows after Microsoft pulled out, a Red Cross proposal for a digital emblem, a RICO indictment over a tax-return fraud ring, Microsoft's claim that China's mandatory vulnerability reporting law fuels its zero-day use, and the UK NCSC's plan to scan UK address space for known vulnerabilities, which Steve loves. Closing items cover Mastodon's growth, a mailing list for SpinRite, Sync.com and SpinRite 6.1 progress.

# Key points

- Dropbox: fake CircleCI login pages harvested a GitHub password and a one-time code relayed from a hardware key; 130 repositories (modified third-party libraries, prototypes, security team tools) were copied; credentials rotated the same day, logs reviewed, outside forensics hired. Steve's lessons: even sophisticated developers fall for phishing, outsourced services (and managed service providers like SolarWinds) enlarge the attack surface, and organisations should run breach drills like school fire drills. Leo adds that using a provider such as Bitwarden's hosting may be safer than self-hosting.
- OpenSSL CVE-2022-3602 is a four-byte stack overflow in X.509 name-constraint checking (via a crafted email address) after chain validation; CVE-2022-3786 overflows with only dot characters, so it is a denial of service. Downgraded from critical to high because of stack protections; clients connecting to malicious servers are the main worry, servers only if they request client certificates. Fixed in 3.0.7.
- FTC v. Chegg: sensitive student data (including religion, sexual orientation, disabilities, parents' income) in AWS S3 with a single all-powerful key, no MFA, plaintext storage, outdated password hashing until 2018, no training until 2020 and no data deletion policy; four incidents (three phishing, one former contractor). The settlement requires a data inventory, retention schedule, deletion on request and MFA.
- FinCEN: $1.2 billion in ransomware payments in 2021, about three quarters going to Russia-linked gangs; Leo ties ransomware's success to cryptocurrency.
- Akamai Q3 2022: 299 phishing kits tracked, each reused on at least three days (average nine); Adobe and M&T Bank lures used across hundreds of domains; phishing-as-a-service and free hosting help attackers. Akamai also saw DNS over TLS usage rise from 25% to 65% among its enterprise customers, though over 70% of global DNS remains plain UDP.
- Kela: over 570 initial access listings in Q3, about $4 million combined; average price $2,800, median $1,350, about 190 new listings per month, searchable by target type.
- OPERA1ER (Common Raven/DESKTOP-group): 35 intrusions in 15 African countries from 2018 to 2021, months of patient lateral movement in bank systems followed by coordinated transfers to about 400 money mule accounts and synchronized ATM cash-outs; confirmed $11 million and suspected $30 million-plus.
- DeFi: Skyward Finance lost about $3 million; Solend lost $1.26 million to an oracle attack on the Hubble USDH currency.
- Russia's Ministry of Digital Development considered Astra Linux, ALT OS and Red OS to replace Windows after Microsoft stopped updates and downloads there.
- ICRC report on a "digital emblem" to mark protected medical and humanitarian networks in cyber conflict; Steve doubts it will deter attackers or prevent abuse.
- US DOJ (Middle District of Florida) RICO indictment: a group bought network access to CPA and tax prep firms on the dark web, stole returns, set up six sham preparer firms and filed over 9,000 fraudulent returns worth about $36 million.
- Microsoft Digital Defense Report: Chinese state actors used more zero-days in the past year, which Microsoft links to China's 2021 law requiring vulnerability reports to the state; it lists five examples (Zoho ManageEngine x2, SolarWinds Serv-U, Atlassian Confluence, Exchange). Steve finds the evidence thin but suspects Microsoft knows more.
- UK NCSC scanning: periodic scans of UK-hosted internet-facing systems for known vulnerabilities, from two identified IP addresses with an `X-NCSC-Scan` header, minimal data collected, opt-out available. Steve wants the US to do likewise or push ISPs to notify customers; Leo recalls ISPs blocking outbound port 25 to curb spam.
- Listener feedback: Firefox can make Ctrl-Tab use most-recently-used order; Steve still recommends Sync.com (zero-knowledge, versioning, no Linux client) and uses Windows `mklink` junctions to sync existing folders.

# Other segments

- Picture of the week: a tall security gate built from horizontal bars that works as a ladder.
- Twitter verification fees: Steve and Leo will not pay; Mastodon surged to about 655,000 active users, and Leo invites Steve to TWiT.social.
- Steve plans a mailing list to announce SpinRite 6.1 and send show-note links weekly.
- SpinRite 6.1: data recovery driver testing finished; command-line redesign includes selecting drives by any listed field (model, serial, type); 406 registered testers; alpha next.
- Leo mentions a story of stolen cryptocurrency stored on a physical device and found by authorities.
