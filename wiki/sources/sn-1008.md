# Summary

The episode opens with Meta's decision to replace third-party fact-checking with Community Notes and cryptographer Matthew Green's worry that encryption could be next; Steve thinks that unlikely, citing CISA's new mobile guidance recommending end-to-end encrypted apps such as Signal and the Wall Street Journal report that Charter, Consolidated Communications and Windstream were also hit by Salt Typhoon. watchTowr Labs describes how they registered expired domains once used by attackers' backdoors and saw more than 4,000 live compromised hosts, including government systems, phone home. The US launched the FCC-run Cyber Trust Mark label for consumer IoT devices, Syncthing shipped a rare bug-fix release, and listener feedback on unencrypted email covers the store-and-forward design of SMTP, why TLS only protects hops, certbot DNS plugins that make Let's Encrypt usable for mail servers, enterprise patching practices and why SonicWall does not push firmware automatically. Steve also shares ChatGPT o1's science-fiction recommendations (he starts Neal Asher's Gridlinked). The main topic is the structure of HOTP (RFC 4226) and TOTP (RFC 6238): an HMAC-SHA1 output of 20 bytes is reduced by a "dynamic truncation" step to a 31-bit integer taken modulo one million, which Steve calls needlessly convoluted compared with long division of the full hash by ten; he concludes the six-digit codes are not biased and that perceived repeats are apophenia.

# Key points

- **Meta moderation change**: Meta ends US third-party fact-checking for Community Notes and moves trust and safety teams to Texas; Green fears government pressure on encryption; Steve argues weakening encryption would hurt the US given Chinese espionage, and notes endpoints already see plaintext.
- **CISA Mobile Communications Best Practice Guidance**: first recommendation is to use only end-to-end encrypted communications (Signal or similar).
- **Salt Typhoon victims**: reportedly seven named US carriers (AT&T, Lumen, Verizon, T-Mobile plus Charter, Consolidated, Windstream) with two more unnamed.
- **Abandoned C2 domains (watchTowr)**: researchers re-registered about 31 lapsed domains used by backdoors and logged 4,000+ unique live backdoors and 300 MB of logs, including web shells on government and university systems in Bangladesh, China, Nigeria, Thailand and South Korea; shows how forgotten malware keeps calling home (and echoes how defenders earlier pre-registered domain-generation-algorithm domains).
- **US Cyber Trust Mark**: voluntary FCC label for consumer IoT with a QR code to a registry (support period, update policy), based on NIST criteria and tested by accredited labs; excludes medical devices, vehicles, industrial gear and products from listed Chinese companies. Steve is hopeful vendors will compete for it.
- **Syncthing v1.29.2**: fixed a crash caused by a directory name with a u-umlaut within two days; Steve praises mature software that rarely needs updates, contrasting with Notepad++ churn.
- **Email in transit**: TLS to a receiving SMTP server gives one-way authentication and only per-hop protection, because mail is store-and-forward; real confidentiality would need payload encryption (S/MIME, PGP) or mutual TLS; SPF and DKIM already authenticate senders. Listeners show certbot can issue certificates for mail servers via temporary web server or DNS TXT plugins (RFC 2136, Cloudflare), though it is a kludge. Steve argues that only web certificates should be forced to short lifetimes.
- **Automatic updates for enterprise gear**: a listener warns automatic updates can cause outages and describes strict corporate controls (segmentation, bastion hosts, whitelisting, two-week patch windows); an MSP explains many SonicWall devices belong to small businesses without support and that scheduled cloud firmware updates cost extra.
- **TOTP/HOTP mechanics**: shared secret delivered via QR code; HMAC-SHA1 over the counter or 30-second time step gives 160 random-looking bits; dynamic truncation uses the low four bits of the last byte as an offset to pick four bytes, clears the top bit to get a 31-bit value, then takes it modulo 1,000,000. Steve notes any four bytes would do and that unique-digit codes occur about 15 percent of the time (9x8x7... counting yields about 15 percent with six distinct digits); slight non-uniformity from modulo is negligible. SHA-1's collision weaknesses do not affect its use as a pseudorandom function.
- **Perfect Paper Passwords** (Episode 115, 2007): Steve's own design used repeated long division of a large number by the alphabet size.
- **Apophenia and pareidolia** explain why users think they see repeated-digit patterns.
