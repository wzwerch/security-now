# Summary

Steve opens with the shaky future of CISA: Congress was again offering only a short-term extension (through end of September) of the 2015 information-sharing law with its liability protections, while Senator Rand Paul drafted a bill that would strip those protections, which Steve fears would make industry stop sharing threat data. He then covers Ireland's newly passed lawful interception law, which applies to all communications (encrypted or not, plus metadata) and creates a legal basis for state use of covert surveillance software, and the pushback from EDRi, which wants a full ban on spyware in Europe and documents a roughly 12 billion euro commercial spyware market. Steve's view is that governments are gradually legislating away strong encryption and that mandated on-device software is the likely end state. Next comes the Forbes story that Microsoft gave the FBI BitLocker recovery keys (Guam COVID-fraud case); Steve argues default drive encryption with cloud key escrow is still net pro-consumer, but listeners should know the keys can be disclosed under a court order and can opt out via policy plus key rotation. Listener feedback covers AI replacing enterprise developers (Alex Neihaus), a UK ISP developer who knowingly exposed databases to the internet for cost reasons (Gavin), and what ISPs can still see (SNI/ECH, DoH, VPN/Tor). The main topic is Check Point Research's analysis of VoidLink, a sophisticated Linux/cloud malware framework written almost entirely by AI under one developer's direction using spec-driven development in ByteDance's Trae IDE, reaching a working implant in under a week. Steve concludes the AI benefit is very asymmetric in favour of attackers and expects an explosion of malware volume and variety, while defenders' real problems (misconfiguration, unpatched servers, human factors) will not be fixed by AI.

# Key points

- CISA: Steve praises the agency (founded 2015) and its KEV catalog for forcing agencies to fix actively exploited flaws; the 2015 information-sharing law and the state/local grant program were extended only temporarily in a funding bill, and Rand Paul (Senate Homeland Security chair) drafted a bill removing the liability protections. Industry stopped sharing when protections lapsed, so Steve wants a long-term (10-year) reauthorization with retroactive protection.
- Ireland passed a lawful interception law replacing a 1993 statute: powers apply to any digital device or service (IoT, email, messaging), to content as well as metadata, whether encrypted or not, and even to parcel services; it also gives a legal basis for covert surveillance software (spyware). It follows similar German plans discussed the week before and an EU "Roadmap for lawful and effective access to data".
- Steve's reading: governments learned that modern encryption cannot be cracked, so they now go around it (pre-encryption access via spyware or a mandated on-device app, as Russia and China do); Leo notes zero-day spyware exploits are expensive and short-lived.
- EDRi launched a Spyware Document Pool and a position paper calling for a full EU spyware ban; at least 14 EU member states have used spyware against journalists, activists and opponents, and an informal anti-spyware interest group was launched in the European Parliament on 21 January.
- Spyware market facts cited: about 12 billion euros per year, 80+ governments as customers, 49+ vendors in 2023, many tied to Israel; Paragon was bought in 2024 for up to 900 million euros; broker prices of roughly 5-7 million USD for iPhone exploits, up to 5 million for Android, 3-3.5 million for Chrome/Safari.
- Microsoft confirmed to Forbes it provides BitLocker recovery keys on valid legal orders (about 20 requests a year, many unanswerable because the key is not in the cloud); first known case was an FBI warrant in Guam for three laptops. Matthew Green and the ACLU criticised the default escrow; Apple and Meta let users keep recovery keys beyond their own reach.
- A 2013 Mashable story recalled that the FBI had informally asked Microsoft's BitLocker team for a backdoor; the team refused but pointed out that printed recovery keys can be obtained by warrant.
- Steve's takeaway on BitLocker: default device encryption on modern Windows 11 PCs is a big privacy win against thieves; escrow keys exist for people who forget logins; Pro/Enterprise/Education users can disable escrow via two policies ("do not store BitLocker recovery information in Azure AD / Microsoft account") and rotate the recovery key, and should print the key on paper. Alex Stamos reportedly agreed it is not a "hair on fire" issue.
- Alex Neihaus (listener): AI should upskill developers, not replace them; repeating the outsourcing mistake with AI risks quality and security in financial and healthcare systems. Leo worries about the lost junior-developer pipeline.
- Gavin (listener) consolidated many managed databases at a small UK ISP by exposing them publicly with strong passwords for about four years because private networking or static IPs were too costly; they have since moved to private subnets and were never knowingly compromised. Steve judged the decision a responsible, informed tradeoff.
- ISP privacy: ISPs see destination IPs, unencrypted DNS and (without ECH) the TLS SNI; more than half of traffic is TLS 1.3 now; Firefox uses DoH to Cloudflare by default while Chromium only upgrades opportunistically; a router doing DoH helps, and only Tor or a VPN hides destinations. A router behind the ISP's modem changes nothing.
- VoidLink (Check Point Research): a modular Linux/cloud malware framework with eBPF and LKM rootkits, cloud enumeration and container post-exploitation modules; developer opsec failures (an exposed open directory) revealed AI-generated sprint plans for three "teams" across 30 weeks, showing the developer used spec-driven development with the Trae Solo assistant in ByteDance's Trae IDE starting late November 2025, producing a first implant in under a week. Earlier AI malware, e.g. FunkSec, came from inexperienced actors.
- Steve's conclusion: AI favours attackers asymmetrically; script kiddies can now write their own malware, so expect a flood of new malicious code, while defenders still fail at basics like patching and misconfiguration.
