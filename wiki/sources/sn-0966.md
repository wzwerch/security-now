# Summary

Mikah Sargent again fills in for Leo. Steve reports Voyager 1 may not be dead: engineers "poked" its memory and it returned a readable dump of the Flight Data Subsystem. He then discusses Tim Berners-Lee's essay on the web's 35th birthday, which laments platform centralisation and the personal-data market and promotes Solid data pods; Steve doubts the public cares enough or that anything will change, since the free web is paid for by tracking. A New York Times story on General Motors vehicles sending detailed trip data to LexisNexis, raising a driver's insurance premium, is called a horrific privacy violation. Listener feedback occupies a long segment on passkeys: shared computers, storage in password managers versus phones, family sharing, risk of the password manager becoming the single point of compromise, catch-all domain emails, and further input on the nginx disclosure case from a HackerOne programme owner. The main topic is Morris II, a lab-demonstrated worm using adversarial self-replicating prompts that spreads between GenAI-powered email assistants without any click; the authors predict real-world GenAI worms within a few years.

# Key points

- Voyager 1: a memory "poke" produced a readable memory readout from the Flight Data Subsystem, renewing hope of recovery; round-trip about 45 hours.
- Tim Berners-Lee (12 March 2024): argues power concentration and the data-profile market defeat the web's original ideals; backs the Solid protocol and personal online data stores (PODs), citing Flanders as an example. Steve is sceptical that users care.
- Vehicle telematics: GM data (via the OnStar Smart Driver feature) reached LexisNexis Risk Solutions, which produced a 258-page report listing 640 trips with hard-braking and acceleration events (not locations) and fed insurer risk scores; other makers have similar partnerships. The driver's premium rose 21 percent. Consent is buried in fine print.
- Steve's broader point: firms profit until legislation stops them; consumers largely accept free services in exchange for tracking.
- Passkeys on shared machines: store them on a phone or FIDO key and log in via QR code so the shared computer never holds them.
- Passkeys cut secret storage by half and remove it from servers, where most thefts occur (Willie Sutton reasoning); storing them in a password manager shifts risk but no major password-manager-extension compromise has been seen.
- Passkey sharing is supported (e.g. Apple shared password groups); FIDO is working on an import/export portability format; Bitwarden passkey support was still incomplete (no mobile, not in exports).
- Passkeys are a better instantiation of "know/have/are": the local secret signs a challenge instead of being compared with a stored copy.
- Masked-email and catch-all custom domains avoid some blocking but a personal domain is trackable, unlike mass services such as duck.com.
- HackerOne programme owner: version banners can mislead because distributions backport patches, so proofs of concept are required; formal programmes also give researchers legal indemnity.
- Steve explains his move away from Twitter for feedback to a GRC email list (worry about paid-only X); suggests contacting a company's board as a last resort for an ignored disclosure.
- Chrome tip: install a vetted extension unpacked in developer mode to freeze it against malicious updates.
- Morris II (Cornell Tech: Ben Nassi, Stav Cohen, Ron Bitton): adversarial self-replicating prompts in an email make an assistant that uses RAG or whose workflow depends on model output replicate the prompt and carry out spamming or data exfiltration; demonstrated in a lab on Gemini Pro, GPT-4 and LLaVA with text and image payloads; disclosed to OpenAI and Google. Authors call it an architectural issue of the GenAI ecosystem and expect real worms within two to three years.
- Steve compares the prompt-as-code issue to SQL injection and buffer overflows and doubts such systems can be made safe.
