# Summary

The whole episode reviews the cyber consequences of Russia's invasion of Ukraine during its first two weeks. Ukraine's digital minister created a volunteer IT Army (175,000 sign-ups growing to nearly 270,000 within the show's preparation) with a public target list of Russian and Belarusian businesses, banks and state sites, while a Kyiv firm offered bounties for taking down Russian sites and NATO's cyber defence centre invited Ukraine in. Many hacktivist and criminal groups declared sides, including Anonymous affiliates, AgainstTheWest and ContiLeaks (an insider leak of hundreds of Conti internal files) versus Conti, Killnet, UNC1151 and Sandworm. Starlink was activated in Ukraine after a public request to Elon Musk; Ukrainian carriers cut service to Russian and Belarusian phones. Russia blocked Facebook, Twitter and Western news outlets; Google demonetized and in Europe blocked Russian state media and tightened protections for Ukrainian users; Apple, Microsoft and Namecheap withdrew from Russia, the latter with a seven-day notice Steve considers a breach of commitment. Telegram use exploded (groups up six-fold), Coinbase blocked over 25,000 addresses but refused blanket bans, Russia published a list of 17,576 IPs and 166 domains it blamed for DDoS and advised turning off automatic updates and using Russian DNS, drafted rules to permit software piracy, and appeared to be preparing to disconnect from the global Internet via RuNet. Steve stresses mutual dependence, implicit cross-border trust such as certificate authorities, and the risk that automatic updates could be weaponized.

# Key points

- IT Army of Ukraine: crowd-sourced DDoS against listed Russian companies, banks (Sberbank, VTB, Gazprombank) and state portals including Roskomnadzor; Steve notes participating is not legal for outsiders just because the cause seems just.
- Cyber Unit Technologies pledged 100,000 dollars in rewards for taking down Russian sites; NATO's CCDCOE invited Ukraine as participant.
- Hacktivist alignments tracked by Recorded Future's The Record: pro-Ukraine (Anonymous and affiliates, GNG, NB65, DeepNetAnon, AgainstTheWest, ContiLeaks, Belarusian Cyber Partisans) versus pro-Russia (Conti, Killnet, UNC1151 from Belarus, Stormous, Sandworm with its Cyclops Blink malware against WatchGuard firewalls); many claims are unverified or false.
- Starlink: Musk activated service and delivered terminals after a public request; Ukraine's multiple western fiber links also helped keep it online; phone carriers blocked Russian and Belarusian handsets.
- Russia blocked Facebook (after Meta restricted state media) and Twitter, plus BBC, VOA, DW and RFE/RL, threatened prison of up to 15 years for "fake news" about its forces, and demanded Google stop ads and videos it called misinformation.
- Google: demonetized Russian state media, blocked RT and Sputnik channels in Europe, removed hundreds of channels, added SOS search alerts, disabled live Maps traffic in Ukraine, raised authentication challenges and extended Advanced Protection and Project Shield to protect over 100 sites.
- Apple halted sales in Russia and Microsoft suspended new sales and said it acted against attacks on more than 20 Ukrainian organizations.
- Namecheap told Russian registrants to move domains within a week and later offered free hosting to anti-regime sites; Steve argues contractual commitments should be honored and notes moving domains was infeasible with banks and card networks cut off.
- Telegram: use up sharply (about six of ten Russians per Flashpoint); Check Point saw most groups devoted to unverified war news, scam donation drives and volunteer hacking; Steve repeats his long-standing distrust of its home-made crypto.
- Coinbase and Binance blocked sanctioned addresses (over 25,000 at Coinbase) but declined to freeze all Russian users.
- Russia's NCCCI published 17,576 IPs and 166 domains (including FBI, CIA, USA Today) as DDoS sources and advised organizations to disable automatic updates and third-party plugins and to use Russian DNS; Steve reflects that automatic update channels could become an attack route in a war.
- Russia planned to relax intellectual property limits to continue using sanctioned vendors' software without payment (Microsoft, Cisco, Oracle, NVIDIA, IBM, Intel, AMD had suspended sales), though cloud-dependent licenses would still fail.
- A leaked ministry letter, posted by Anonymous, was said to instruct organizations how to prepare for RuNet and disconnection; Steve links this to last summer's DNS dry run.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: man tells his wife a website is safe because it asks for every personal detail.
- Deferred to next week: an EU proposal about TLS certificates that browser vendors oppose.
- Steve admits he was once skeptical of cyberwarfare and compares the situation to mutually assured destruction.
