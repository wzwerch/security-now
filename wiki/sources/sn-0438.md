# Summary

After a quiet January Patch Tuesday (four non-critical bulletins, with XP's end of support 83 days away), Steve covers the Target breach, now 110 million customers and blamed on malware in point-of-sale terminals, with similar reports from Neiman Marcus. He explains a new DDoS technique: attackers send small spoofed requests for the old NTP "monlist" command, and each answer is roughly 200 times larger, enabling attacks around 100 Gbit/s, which can only be filtered upstream by large ISPs. Other news includes nine speakers boycotting the RSA conference, an ex-NSA mathematician saying the agency has more data than it can understand, an FBI agent casually asking Wickr's founder for a back door, and the GSM cipher being deliberately weakened. Steve reports the complete command set of the port 32764 router backdoor, which lets a remote attacker dump credentials, run a shell and re-enable WAN administration, and offers a GRC check link. A federal appeals court struck down the FCC's net-neutrality rules on jurisdictional grounds while affirming its authority in principle. The main segment reviews the ANT catalogue from Der Spiegel: passive radio-reflecting implants powered by almost nothing, interdiction (physical access) installs, firmware persistence in Cisco, Juniper and Huawei routers and in hard-drive firmware, SIM-card and cell-phone implants, and a layered stack of cooperating exploits partly built by a contractor. Steve calls it targeted tradecraft, not mass surveillance, and notes that the slides are about five years old.

# Key points

- January Patch Tuesday was small: four non-critical bulletins; Steve plans to move from XP to Windows 7.
- Target: 40 million, then 70 million, further records disclosed (about 110 million accounts); attack attributed to point-of-sale malware; details scarce.
- DDoS evolution: SYN floods, then spoofed-source reflection, DNS amplification, and now NTP monlist reflection: a 234-byte request returns up to 600 peer addresses over about 100 packets (roughly 206 times amplification); the command was known to be dangerous since 2010 and affects old servers still answering on UDP port 123; large attacks must be filtered at ISP ingress points.
- RSA conference boycott by security speakers grew to nine over the alleged payment from the NSA.
- William Binney said the NSA collects so much it cannot understand it; a Wickr founder said an FBI agent asked her at a conference to add a back door; GSM encryption was weakened to a 64-bit key with ten bits zeroed after government pressure in the 1980s.
- Port 32764: thirteen commands include dumping the configuration with admin password and Wi-Fi key, setting variables, opening a shell, writing files, and restoring defaults (re-enabling WAN admin). Cisco promised firmware updates, and a few models expose it to the Internet; Steve provides a quick check and says to block it.
- Net neutrality: the D.C. appeals court said the FCC lacked authority under its chosen classification of broadband providers but accepted broad authority to promote deployment; Leo notes discrimination worries such as sponsored data.
- ANT concepts: "interdiction" means physical tampering with a target; "implant" is hardware or software that persists across reboots and upgrades; many devices are passive radio-frequency retro-reflectors that only respond when lit by a continuous microwave beam, so sweeps cannot find them, and demodulation requires the transmitter's phase.
- Examples: a VGA cable implant that reflects video (30 dollars), LoudAuto room-audio retro-reflector drawing about 15 microamps, a keyboard and USB-cable (CottonMouth) air-gap bridge with radio, a software-defined radio hidden in a phone (Genesis), a portable GSM base station (Typhon HX, rented for 175,000 dollars), IrateMonk hard-drive firmware persistence (WD, Seagate, Maxtor, Samsung), SIM Toolkit implants GopherSet and MonkeyCalendar that SMS out contacts and location.
- Router implants: BananaGlee (Juniper NetScreen and Cisco PIX/ASA firewall exfiltration, by contractor Digital Network Technologies), JetPlow (Cisco firmware persistence hosting BananaGlee), SchoolMontana, SierraMontana and StuccoMontana for Juniper J, M and T series, and HeadWater for Huawei routers, which can be installed remotely via an upgrade (a joint NSA/CIA effort, TurboPanda). Steve sees no evidence of vendor collusion, and most others need physical access.
- Leo suggests the leak may partly serve to deter adversaries; Steve doubts Snowden was the source.

# Other segments

- Steve recommends TSA PreCheck and notes a Kickstarter for a bootable Windows password-reset key (works unless the disk is fully encrypted).
- Bonus reading: an elliptic-curve primer and an analysis of the RSA Dual_EC backdoor from Cloudflare.
- SQRL progress: tuning the Scrypt-based key derivation so unlocking takes about five seconds, to hinder GPU or ASIC cracking.
- SpinRite works inside VirtualBox, including on Macs; USB-attached drives will have to wait for a later release.
- Leo reports on Steven Levy's visit to NSA headquarters; next episode will be Q&A.
