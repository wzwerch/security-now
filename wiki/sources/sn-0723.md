# Summary

Steve opens with July's Patch Tuesday (77 flaws, two zero-days in Win32k and the print spooler, many browser memory-corruption bugs) and anger among Windows 7 users after a "security-only" update carried a telemetry compatibility component. He explains why he keeps using Windows 7 after support ends in February 2020 while not recommending that for everyone. LaPorte County, Indiana was hit by Ryuk, which also encrypted its backups; the county's insurer covered 100,000 USD of a roughly 130,000 USD ransom, and the US Conference of Mayors adopted a resolution against paying ransoms. Coveware-style ransomware negotiation firms are emerging, and Emsisoft notes some Ryuk victims may recover with known reused keys. A Sophos survey of 3,100 IT managers shows email (33 percent) and web (30 percent) as the top attack vectors. Steve questions the UK ICO's proposed fines against Marriott and British Airways. Mozilla decided to distrust DarkMatter's intermediates. Researchers showed exfiltrating data from air-gapped PCs via keyboard LEDs. The main topic compares DNSSEC (authenticity only), DNSCrypt, DNS over TLS and DNS over HTTPS, and recommends enabling one of the encrypted options.

# Key points

- Patch Tuesday: 77 flaws (16 critical); Russian-attributed zero-days were an elevation of privilege and a spooler issue; Adobe did not ship patches the same day.
- Windows 7 security-only update KB4507456 included the Compatibility Appraiser telemetry tool; Windows 10 had about 40.6 percent share versus Windows 7 about 38 percent; Steve plans to stay on Win7 workstations with strict habits and layered backups, and does not advise others to follow.
- LaPorte County: Ryuk hit domain controllers and backup servers; forensic firm and FBI could not decrypt; ransom paid with insurance covering 100,000 USD; Steve predicts insurers will demand audits and better backups.
- US Conference of Mayors resolution cites at least 170 government ransomware attacks since 2013 and opposes paying; Steve notes IT listeners say simple full backups are harder than they sound (open files, unstoppable servers).
- Sophos/Vanson Bourne survey: email 33 percent, web 30 percent, software vulnerability 23 percent, USB 14 percent, unknown 20 percent; IT teams spend about 26 percent of time on security; 86 percent say they need more skills.
- ICO intends fines of about 99 million pounds on Marriott (Starwood breach, 383 million guest records; passports and some card numbers involved) and a larger one on British Airways (web card skimming over three months); Steve worries heavy fines discourage voluntary disclosure; Leo suggests forced security spending instead. FTC approved a 5 billion USD Facebook settlement.
- Mozilla decided to distrust DarkMatter's subordinate CAs (issued via a QuoVadis intermediate) and reject DigitalTrust's request; Firefox may import Windows roots when a third-party antivirus is present (security.enterprise_roots.enabled); Firefox 69 will add a tracker-blocking report page.
- CTRL-ALT-LED paper: exfiltrating data from air-gapped computers by blinking keyboard LEDs to a camera, achieving roughly 5 kbps with some keyboards.
- Encrypting DNS overview: DNSSEC signs but does not encrypt; DNSCrypt (Curve25519, no PKI, never an IETF standard); DNS over TLS (Android Pie Private DNS, unbound, pfSense; correction that last week's pfSense reference was DoT, not DoH); DNS over HTTPS (RFC 8484, application/dns-message). DNSCrypt-Proxy and Simple DNSCrypt offer client support. Listener reported faster browsing after enabling DoH, likely due to a slow default resolver.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: security risks narrowed to "everyone who works here" and "everyone who doesn't."
- Year 15 of the show begins August 20, 2019; Stranger Things 3 disappointment and product placement complaint.
- Leo notes Google Pixel private DNS defaults point back to Google.
