# Summary

Steve opens with policy tension: Kazakhstan's plan for a state certificate to intercept outbound TLS, FBI director testimony that companies might need to drop end-to-end encryption, and Russia's push to force local storage of citizens' data, which Steve says conflicts with how the Internet works. A bored help-desk worker using Shodan found an exposed MongoDB holding about 21 GB of MacKeeper customer data and disclosed it responsibly. With two weeks left before SHA-1 certificate issuance ends, roughly 40 million people on old browsers and systems would lose HTTPS access; Facebook and CloudFlare describe choosing a SHA-1 or SHA-256 certificate per client based on the signature algorithms announced in the client hello, which Steve finds clever and downgrade-resistant. Google announced distrust of a Symantec root after Symantec said it would no longer comply with baseline requirements, which Steve guesses signals possible loss of control of the key. Further items cover a Bell Canada router with a hidden WPS flaw, a Florida car that reported its own crash, a master's thesis finding practical padding attacks against Telegram's homemade crypto, magnesium supplements, and SpinRite on SCSI RAID drives. The only listener question answered is why synchronous clocks made complex digital circuits possible, which Steve explains through ROM-and-latch state machines.

# Key points

- Kazakhstan: formalizing a national interception certificate would let the border decrypt and re-encrypt all HTTPS; the earlier notice disappeared without explanation.
- FBI Director Comey told senators that end-to-end encryption is a business-model question; Steve notes no solid evidence that Paris or San Bernardino attackers relied on encryption.
- Russia plans to enforce data localisation; Apple, Viber, eBay, PayPal and Booking.com complying, Twitter, Google and Facebook silent. Steve and Leo argue replication across borders is inherent to the Internet.
- MacKeeper maker Kromtech had four exposed Mongo database servers found through Shodan; the finder reported them and the company closed access and claimed no one else connected. Steve highlights the legal risk of researchers who look at exposed systems.
- SHA-1 background: no full collision yet, but October's compression-function result moved timelines forward; GRC's certificates expire at year end and Steve will switch to SHA-256 shortly before then to avoid clock-skew issues.
- About 40 million users (CloudFlare estimates under 2 percent of browsers; Facebook 3-7 percent of its traffic) cannot use SHA-256 certificates: Android 2.x, Windows XP before SP3, OpenSSL 0.9.8-based clients. Facebook and CloudFlare propose "certificate switching" and a legacy-verified certificate class; Facebook open-sourced its switching code in Proxygen and CloudFlare enabled it network-wide by default.
- Steve lists protection against downgrade because the final handshake exchange authenticates the original client hello.
- Google (Ryan Sleevi) will distrust a Symantec Class 3 root because Symantec said it will use the root outside baseline requirements; Steve's conjecture is key-control uncertainty.
- Bell Canada HomeHub 1000 and 2000 series: disabling WPS only hides the beacon, and a fixed PIN that passes the checksum still returns the Wi-Fi passphrase in under a second. Steve recalls WPS's split four-plus-three digit weakness.
- A Ford's emergency-response system called police after a hit-and-run, an IoT irony.
- Telegram: a Danish master's thesis found two practical padding-integrity attacks and other weaknesses; Steve repeats that homemade crypto is a bad idea and contrasts it with TextSecure.
- Steve clarifies he is not a physicist, discusses AOL FDO legacy design, and gives a Lost Fleet book series reference.
- Magnesium health aside: chelated forms such as bisglycinate absorb better.
- SpinRite on SCSI or RAID member drives: yes via a plain controller; it only reads and repairs, and imaging first is not always possible with unreadable drives. Steve will refund anyone dissatisfied.
- Main Q&A: clocks let designers break problems into discrete steps, enabling ROM-plus-latch microcoded state machines; Leo compares it to a traffic cop. Leo briefly mentions a reported Kerberos issue, then corrects himself that it is an old, known weakness.
