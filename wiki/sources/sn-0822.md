# Summary

Steve opens with a "calm rant", titled the great cybersecurity awakening of 2021, arguing that systems are insecure because the economics reward visible features over invisible security, vendors carry no liability, cheap IoT devices are unvetted, and web apps deliberately breach the browser sandbox (for example an email link that leads to a JavaScript-driven malware download). Firefox 90 beta will update itself in the background on Windows via a service, Firefox 89 feels noticeably faster to Steve, and Edge 92 previews an "Automatic HTTPS" feature based on a list of HTTPS-capable sites. New ransomware victims included Fujifilm (QBot handing access to REvil), the Massachusetts Steamship Authority and UF Health. Mandiant's work suggests Colonial Pipeline was entered with a stale, MFA-less VPN account whose password appeared in leaked credential dumps. The DOJ announced seizure of 63.7 BTC (about $2.3M) of the ransom; Steve doubts a technical break and suspects political pressure on DarkSide, while Leo suggests a custodial exchange wallet. WordPress news: Jetpack was force-patched on about five million sites, whereas the Fancy Product Designer plugin had an actively exploited zero-day with no auto-update path. GitHub updated its policy on proof-of-concept and malware hosting. Steve restated why NAT remains a useful extra layer despite IPv6 purists, and reviewed Project Hail Mary. The main topic was Tavis Ormandy's blog post advising against online subscription password managers in favor of browser-built-in ones; Steve and Leo disagree, citing cross-browser use, password generation, and a lack of evidence that well-designed third-party managers are being exploited.

# Key points

- Security rant: 87.8% of desktops run Windows with 50-150 fixes every month; Exchange flaw reported late 2020 was only patched in March; users are blamed for clicking plausible mail; software vendors disclaim fitness in licenses; Steve's conclusion is that nobody would pay the price of real security and the systems are not going to become secure.
- Firefox 90 beta adds Background Update on Windows (checks every seven hours, installs a Mozilla update service); can be disabled in about:preferences. Firefox 89 rated visibly faster by Steve.
- Edge 92 preview "Automatic HTTPS" upgrades HTTP to HTTPS on domains Microsoft judges likely to support it; a stricter always-upgrade toggle exists at the privacy settings. Chrome already defaults to HTTPS for scheme-less URLs; Firefox HTTPS-only mode is opt-in. Steve also discusses HSTS max-age values for GRC and TWiT.
- Ransomware victims: Fujifilm (QBot plus REvil), Massachusetts Steamship Authority (ticketing disrupted), UF Health (two hospitals back to paper).
- Colonial Pipeline (per Bloomberg citing Mandiant): attackers likely used a compromised VPN password from leaked dumps on an unused but still enabled account with no multi-factor authentication. Lessons: disable dormant accounts, require MFA, forced changes to unique passwords could have helped.
- DOJ seized 63.7 BTC (about $2.3M) tied to the May 8 DarkSide ransom after a warrant from a Northern District of California magistrate; the Ransomware and Digital Extortion Task Force was involved. Steve notes that Colonial had barely used DarkSide's slow decryptor and that DarkSide had offered a Monero payment option with a 10% surcharge for bitcoin.
- Jetpack 9.8 fixed a Carousel comments flaw reported privately; patched builds were pushed for every version since 2.0, with about 5.25M downloads in a week. Steve uses this to defend auto-updates (including routers) despite supply-chain concerns raised by listeners.
- Fancy Product Designer (WordPress/WooCommerce, about 17,000 installs): zero-day RCE via bypassing upload checks, attacks seen since January 30, 2021, aimed at e-commerce order data; patched in 4.6.9 on June 2; Wordfence withheld details. Steve says sites should be examined for compromise.
- GitHub Acceptable Use Policy update: content that directly supports active attack or malware campaigns causing harm (malware delivery, attack infrastructure) is prohibited, without a dual-use purpose; this followed the controversial removal of an Exchange proof of concept.
- NAT vs IPv6: Steve clarifies he never claimed only NAT can give stateful filtering (ZoneAlarm did without it); private addressing is an additional layer; STUN/TURN solved peer connectivity; carrier-grade NAT delayed IPv6 adoption.
- Project Hail Mary: Steve loved it and urges reading it before any film (a miniseries would fit better). Leo adds news of the FBI/Australian ANOM encrypted-phone sting and worries it is used to argue for backdoors; a listener tied it to Steve's long-standing point about key management in iMessage.
- Tavis Ormandy's argument: "use a password manager" is vague and dangerous advice; extension-based online managers inject UI into hostile pages via content scripts and communicate over web-reachable channels (WebSockets, postMessage) without proper mutual authentication; they also weaken the browser sandbox; marketing claims that the vendor cannot access your data ignore that a compromised vendor can serve altered code. He recommends simple local managers like KeePass, or the browser's built-in manager.
- Steve and Leo's response: Tavis is technically sound, but cross-browser use, password generation, non-browser app logins, and secure notes make extrinsic managers valuable; the clipboard is an exposure for copy-paste workflows; open-source audited managers such as Bitwarden are preferred, and no exploitation of well-designed managers has surfaced. Steve mentions browser vendors may coordinate on extension security.
