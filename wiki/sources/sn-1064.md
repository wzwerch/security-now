# Summary

Steve opens with a note on why the show keeps covering AI, then reports that the Irish Data Protection Commission has levied about 4.04 billion euros of GDPR fines over six years but collected only about 20 million, because appeals block collection and the key WhatsApp case sits before EU courts. He moves to Western democracies legalizing and recruiting for offensive cyber operations (Denmark's military intelligence hacker academy, legal changes in many countries) and a report that U.S. Cyber Command's targeted network attack stopped Iran launching surface-to-air missiles during Operation Midnight Hammer in June 2025, with a similar role in the Venezuela operation. A TechCrunch story on user grief and lawsuits over OpenAI retiring GPT-4o illustrates the danger of AI companions. CISA's Binding Operational Directive 26-02 orders federal civilian agencies to inventory and replace end-of-support edge devices on a 3/12/18/24 month schedule, which Steve applauds. Listener items cover turning off Windows welcome and setup nag screens, whether CGNAT hides subscribers from advertisers (it does not, since ISPs log port allocations), and a long discussion of OpenClaw, the viral self-hosted AI agent whose security is poor (exposed instances, 341 malicious ClawHub skills, the Moltbook agent social network leaking keys). A listener who used OpenAI's Codex to build a book-inventory app prompts Steve's spreadsheet analogy. The main topic is least privilege, prompted by a second Coinbase insider breach through a contractor: outsourced business-process providers are over-trusted through broad API access, and Steve argues that failure to apply least privilege is a policy choice that AI bug-fixing will not cure.

# Key points

- GDPR fine collection: Irish DPC fined 4.04 billion euros in six years (530 million in 2025) but collected about 20 million in total; DPC cannot collect while an appeal is pending, and a 2021 WhatsApp case (initial 50 million, raised to 225 million by the EDPB) is held up at EU courts. Steve's takeaway: appealing turns a fine into a mere threat.
- Offensive cyber is becoming normal: Denmark's DDIS is recruiting hackers with a five-month academy; Canada, Germany, Finland, France, Japan, the Netherlands, Poland and Sweden are updating legal frameworks; the Trump administration wants Cyber Command to act with fewer restrictions and is considering using private contractors. Leo and Steve note collateral-damage and escalation risks.
- Operation Midnight Hammer: per The Record, a Cyber Command strike on a network "aim point", enabled by NSA intelligence, prevented Iran launching any surface-to-air missile during the June 2025 strikes on three nuclear sites; Cyber Command also contributed to the Venezuela operation (power and air-defense disruption). Officials say cyber is now treated like a kinetic capability.
- GPT-4o retirement (OpenAI, Feb 13): users described the loss as losing a friend; OpenAI faces eight lawsuits alleging 4o's overly validating replies contributed to suicides and crises; guardrails reportedly degraded over long conversations. Steve doubts conversational AI can be reliably constrained.
- CISA BOD 26-02 "Mitigating Risk From End-of-Support Edge Devices": agencies update supported edge gear immediately, inventory against CISA's EOS Edge Device List within 3 months, decommission listed devices within 12 months, decommission all EOS edge devices within 18 months, and set up continuous discovery within 24 months. Steve notes replacements also get modern secure defaults, and worries vendors may shorten support windows.
- Windows nag screens: Settings under System > Notifications & actions (Windows 10 and 11) let users turn off the post-update "welcome experience", "suggest ways to finish setting up", and tips; Steve says he will stay on Windows 10.
- CGNAT does not anonymize subscribers: some ISPs must log port-block allocations per subscriber; only a (paid, no-log) VPN prevents the ISP from tying your traffic to your identity. Steve says "free" and "reputable VPN" do not go together.
- OpenClaw (formerly Clawdbot/Moltbot, creator Peter Steinberger): open-source autonomous agent needing credentials, mail and shell access; researchers found hundreds of exposed instances with no protection, 341 malicious skills on its ClawHub marketplace, plaintext credential storage, and Moltbook, an AI-only social network that exposed databases and API keys and allows prompt-injection between bots. Steve and Forbes' sources advise treating it as an experiment; Leo says he deleted his install.
- AI-built apps: a listener (Kyle) made a library-inventory tool with OpenAI Codex in under 30 minutes but felt uneasy about unexplained commands and pip packages (Beautiful Soup 4, Pillow, openpyxl). Steve compares the effect to the spreadsheet (VisiCalc); Leo uses Claude Code daily; Steve will keep hand-coding GRC.
- Least privilege (main topic): giving only the rights required for a task is simple but inconvenient, so it is routinely skipped. Business process outsourcing (BPO) is an example: Coinbase confirmed a second insider incident (about 30 customers, a December contractor) after the earlier TaskUs breach; Scattered Lapsus Hunters posted internal support-panel screenshots; related BPO/help-desk cases include Cognizant/Clorox, Marks & Spencer, Co-op and Discord's 5.5 million-user Zendesk breach. Steve blames "API over-trust" and over-broad MSP/BPO access, recommends designing for a hostile provider (HSM-style non-exportable secrets), and restates that no non-public service should be reachable from arbitrary internet locations. He frames missing least privilege as deliberate policy, not mistake.
