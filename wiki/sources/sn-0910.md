# Summary

Steve follows up on the ESXiArgs ransomware: CISA published a recovery script that rebuilds virtual machine metadata from unencrypted disks, but within a day the attackers released a variant that also encrypts the main .vmdk files, and scanning of OpenSLP honeypots rose from three to over 40 IPs. Check Point reported criminals selling Telegram bots wired to the unrestricted OpenAI API to generate malicious content. Google is giving away 100,000 Titan security keys to high-risk users in 2023. Brave 1.5 adopts HTTPS-by-default with a small exception list, which Steve notes only brings it to parity with other browsers. Russia is considering exempting "patriotic hackers" from prosecution, AWS will turn on Block Public Access for new S3 buckets in April 2023, Australia is uneasy about Hikvision and Dahua cameras, and Edge will embed Adobe's Acrobat PDF engine. Listener feedback covers server-side secrecy of OTP seeds, a VMware engineer's response about scanning customers, and algorithmic password generators. The main topic is Ascon, chosen by NIST for lightweight authenticated encryption and hashing.

# Key points

- CISA released ESXiArgs-Recover on GitHub, based on a public tutorial, which reconstructs VM metadata from unencrypted virtual disks; a new ESXiArgs version then also encrypted the large .vmdk file, defeating the shortcut. Rapid7 counted nearly 19,000 still-vulnerable ESXi servers (other counts of exposed instances reached 83,000).
- Steve speculates that responsibility for patching fell between hosting provider and customer, each assuming the other would handle it.
- Check Point: criminals use ChatGPT to polish old infostealer code and sell access to the unrestricted OpenAI API via Telegram bots (20 free queries, then about 5.50 dollars per 100); the web interface's refusal rules are not applied to the API.
- Google will distribute 100,000 Titan security keys at no cost in 2023 and partner with IFES and Defending Digital Campaigns; a Google/YouGov study found over half of journalists and political professionals had accounts compromised.
- Brave 1.5 "HTTPS by Default" upgrades navigations to HTTPS unless the site is on a list of about 112 sites known to break, replacing the unmaintained HTTPS Everywhere list. Steve found some listed domains (such as columbia.edu) already redirect properly and others are dead; he is himself fully on Firefox.
- 1Password announced passkey support as a way to unlock the vault without a master password; Bitwarden had bought Passwordless.dev. Steve notes passkey sync across platforms remains a need that third-party managers can fill.
- Russia's State Duma is exploring absolving "Russian patriotic hackers" of criminal liability for attacks in the interests of the Russian Federation, wherever located.
- AWS announced that from April 2023 new S3 buckets default to Block Public Access enabled and ACLs disabled; Steve asks what took so long.
- The Australian reported 913 Hikvision and Dahua devices across 250 government sites; the US and UK had already restricted them. Steve sees "what if?" concerns that cannot be disproven and a trend toward cyber protectionism.
- Microsoft will embed Adobe's Acrobat PDF engine in Edge (Windows 10/11 from March 2023, old engine removed in March 2024).
- OTP seeds are symmetric secrets that websites must store safely; asymmetric schemes like WebAuthn and SQRL give sites only public keys, which are useless to thieves. A listener notes a password manager's URL matching would also protect TOTP codes from lookalike sites; Steve still prefers separating them.
- A VMware engineer wrote that proactively scanning and notifying customers is hard (finding contacts, privacy, free hobbyist ESXi) and that ESXi 8.0 sandboxes all daemons by default; Steve replies that Internet scanning is no longer controversial and the real problem is who to contact.
- Algorithmic per-site passwords (an HMAC of the domain under a secret key) are an old idea; Steve links it to SQRL and his paper-based "Off The Grid" scheme.
- Ascon: NIST selected the Ascon family (designed by Graz University of Technology, Infineon, Lamarr Security Research and Radboud University) as its lightweight cryptography standard after a competition that began in 2018 with 57 submissions; it had earlier won CAESAR. It offers AEAD, hashing and extendable output from one lightweight permutation, with 128-bit keys, nonce-based single-pass operation, no table lookups and side-channel-friendly design.
- Steve argues 128-bit keys are ample (brute force would take on the order of 10^21 years even at an impossible 3.4 billion keys per second) and that quantum computers do not threaten symmetric ciphers in this way; open-source suites for C, Rust, Java and Arduino exist.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: a New York Times article about not sharing your email address overlaid with an email-capture paywall prompt.
- Banter on Chrome spinning up fans and Firefox as default browser.
- Explanation of authenticated encryption versus separate encrypt-then-MAC, and why AEAD with associated data is useful.
