# Summary

The headline is "Flip Feng Shui," a research attack that turns Rowhammer DRAM bit flips into a cross-virtual-machine key compromise by exploiting memory deduplication in clouds. Before that, Steve walks through Mozilla's public case against WoSign, which has three incidents: validation on arbitrary high ports, base-domain certificates for subdomain control that were never fully revoked or reported, and back-dated SHA-1 certificates via the StartCom/WoSign systems. A real autonomous recon-drone contract (Shield AI) shows the earlier drone warning is not far-fetched. A paper on birthday attacks against 64-bit block ciphers (3DES, Blowfish) led OpenVPN and OpenSSL to deprecate them. The first "YAWTTY" (Yet Another Way To Track You) is the ambient-light sensor API. A German phishing study found most students clicked photo links, a USB network-adapter trick can hijack proxy settings and steal credentials from a locked PC, and Steve answers several listener questions and gives solutions to the SHA-1-only appliance puzzle (manipulate the clock, spoof NTP, or front it with CloudFlare's serve-SHA-1-to-old-clients approach).

# Key points

- WoSign (per Mozilla's analysis): (1) certificates issued after validation on any chosen port, including unprivileged high ports; (2) base-domain issuance on subdomain control with a university certificate still unrevoked a year later and no search for other misissuance; (3) the StartCom StartEncrypt service let a request parameter select WoSign roots and produced SHA-1 certificates back-dated to late 2015; WoSign had quietly acquired StartCom. None of this was reported to Mozilla; the community comment thread weighs sanctions.
- Steve argues browsers hold enormous power over CAs now that HTTPS is mandatory, and that trusting every root by default is the wrong model.
- Shield AI won a million-dollar Defense Innovation Unit contract for autonomous indoor-mapping micro-drones; Leo notes domestic surveillance concerns (Baltimore aerial surveillance).
- Block-cipher birthday attacks (ACM CCS paper, later widely known as Sweet32): 64-bit block ciphers such as 3DES and Blowfish leak after enough data; demo recovers an HTTPS session cookie after about 785 GB of traffic over roughly 38 hours with attacker JavaScript, and an OpenVPN token after about 705 GB. OpenVPN 2.3.12 discourages 64-bit ciphers; OpenSSL downgrades 3DES to medium and plans to disable it in 1.1.0. About 12 percent of HTTPS connections still used 3DES according to the paper.
- YAWTTY: the ambient-light sensor API exposes colour and intensity of light, which can help correlate a user across sites.
- German study: 1,700 students received links to supposed party photos; 56 percent clicked via personalised email and 37 percent via Facebook; unpersonalised messages drew 20 percent by email and 42 percent via Facebook; most later denied clicking; 78 percent had claimed awareness of risk. Leo recounts nearly falling for a Find-My-iPhone text.
- USB network adapters: a malicious USB device posing as a NIC can serve DHCP with proxy auto-config settings (WPAD) and, with the Responder tool, capture credentials from a locked machine because auto-install needs no login.
- Listener answers: spaces versus tabs data from GitHub, Carmack's complaint about project bloat, SQRL's self-installing single EXE design, MailStore Home for email archiving, why iOS is called more secure (control and patch speed, not audits), and why an HTTP-delivered form posting to HTTPS is unsafe because the form can be modified.
- COM and EXE named for compile-time versus execution-time binding; Little Flocker file-access control tool for macOS noted.
- Rowhammer background: repeated activations of one DRAM row flip bits in adjacent rows (about 1 in 1,700 bits flippable on susceptible memory); faster refresh (64 ms to 32 ms) and DDR4 row-access counters mitigate it.
- Flip Feng Shui: attacker VM finds its own flippable bit, copies a page known to exist in a victim VM (such as one holding an SSH authorised public key), waits for kernel same-page merging to share the page, then hammers to flip a bit in the victim's key, making the modulus easy to factor and letting the attacker log in as that user. Steve calls it the most sublime hack he has covered.
- SHA-1 appliance puzzle solutions: serve an expired SHA-1 certificate and keep the device clock within validity (including by spoofing its NTP server via DNS), or put the domain behind CloudFlare, which serves SHA-1 certificates to clients that cannot handle SHA-256.
