# Summary

The show begins year 20 with a RAID-as-water-coolers Picture of the Week. Steve first reports the result of his revocation experiment: after he blocked his servers from fetching DigiCert OCSP responses, the stapled "revoked" status lapsed, and every browser except Firefox (which queries OCSP responders by default) again displayed revoked.grc.com. He explains OCSP Must Staple and why the industry will not mandate it: it would make CA OCSP outages or DDoS attacks take customer sites offline, which is the reasoning behind the CA/Browser Forum moving to CRLs. He then covers August Patch Tuesday (about 90 fixes, six exploited zero-days), the discovery of 53 lost 1980s "Famous Computer Cafe" radio shows, IsBootSecure? release 3 with a silent mode, GRC's email system, and listener feedback. The main story is the National Public Data (NPD) breach, using Troy Hunt's analysis: roughly 2.7 to 2.9 billion rows, probably well under a billion people, containing names, addresses, relatives and Social Security Numbers, sold by a actor called USDoD and later leaked publicly. Steve urges everyone to freeze credit at the three bureaus and notes that people who had used data-removal services were absent from the data.

# Key points

- OCSP experiment: with stapling suppressed (DigiCert's OCSP host blackholed locally), Chrome, Safari and Edge accept a certificate revoked 21 days earlier; Firefox refuses it because "Query OCSP responder servers" is enabled by default. Chrome only does special handling for EV certificates.
- OCSP Must Staple (a certificate flag) would force stapling and give fast revocation with no privacy leak, but any OCSP outage longer than the staple lifetime would take sites offline; the CA/Browser Forum ballot cites responder reliability (Mozilla measured about 7% failed OCSP queries).
- Short certificate lifetimes (Let's Encrypt 90 days, others 397 days) are an alternative to revocation; a listener asked why not seven-day certificates, and Steve notes renewal-outage risk and issuance load.
- August 2024 Patch Tuesday: at least 90 vulnerabilities and six zero-days exploited, including three local privilege escalations, an Edge Internet Explorer mode remote code execution, a Mark of the Web bypass, and a Microsoft Project macro-related remote code execution.
- Internet Archive posted 53 recovered episodes (1984-85) of the "Famous Computer Cafe" radio show with interviews including Douglas Adams, Timothy Leary and Bill Gates.
- IsBootSecure? release 3 adds a silent mode returning exit codes 0-7 for enterprise inventory of Secure Boot and Platform Key status.
- GRC mailing: 8,443 announcement emails sent, five bounced; mail to securitynow@grc.com from unregistered senders now bounces.
- Listener questions: an OpenVPN on TCP 443 being cut off by Wi-Fi networks in Europe (Steve guesses idle-connection or traffic-shape filtering); after moving a domain to Hover, nameserver entries still pointed to the old registrar until changed; a Dell Platform Key valid until 2031 is not a problem because firmware does not enforce that date.
- NPD breach: aggregator Jerico Pictures (National Public Data) sold background-check data; USDoD offered 2.9 billion records for $3.5 million in April 2024; a user "Fenice" leaked data in August; class action Hofmann v. Jerico Pictures filed in Florida.
- Troy Hunt's analysis: two main files with 2.696 billion rows, only 31% of sampled rows had unique SSNs, so perhaps about 900 million distinct individuals; later partial dumps (a 27-part Telegram archive of 104 GB, 134 million email addresses) look like a mixture of other data, with questionable accuracy.
- People who had used data opt-out services were absent from the data, supporting that the source was a legal aggregator and showing opt-outs work.
- A public lookup site (npd.pentester.com, shortcut grc.sc/npd) shows matching records with only the last two SSN digits; Steve and Leo both found accurate entries.
- Advice: freeze credit with all three bureaus (free under US law; shortcut grc.sc/credit); the leaked data is permanent; consider paid removal services early in life; expect legislative pressure on data brokers.
- Leo adds that Krebs reported NPD's sister site RecordsCheck.net exposed an admin credential file, a possible entry point.
