# Summary

A quiet news week led into a Q&A. Steve warned of a risk with IoT purchases after Nest (Google) announced it would shut down the Revolv home hub it had acquired, despite buyers having paid for a lifetime subscription, tying it to the earlier loss of Zeo's cloud service. Bruce Schneier argued the FBI should disclose whatever vulnerability it used on the San Bernardino iPhone, and Steve and Leo noted the FBI was offering the unlock method to other agencies and had paid sums to a phone-forensics firm. WhatsApp had just enabled end-to-end encryption using the Signal protocol, and Steve postponed judgement on its authentication until reading the technical paper. He described a painful domain transfer away from Network Solutions, a mind-bending printed-magnet video, and feedback on his sleep formula and a SpinRite-and-fusion-drive question. In the Q&A: Shodan shows exposed SMB servers ahead of Badlock; a managed-service provider recovered from a Locky outbreak using hourly backups; how to argue for HTTPS to a library; the value of SQL Server transparent data encryption; why Norton flagged Never10 (a new certificate had no reputation); corporate TLS inspection is justified; the convenience-but-not-security of file.pizza; SNMP risks; why VLANs are weak for IoT security; and the limits of self-encrypting drives.

# Key points

- Revolv: a roughly 300 dollar hub sold with a lifetime subscription is to stop working on 15 May 2016 after acquisition by Nest; Steve urges caution about IoT systems that depend on a vendor's servers and wishes for standards and fallbacks. The Wi-Fi Alliance provisioning work is mentioned as a start.
- Schneier: the FBI's method is a vulnerability and should be disclosed so Apple can patch; one can infer vulnerable models from which lawsuits are dropped. Steve notes the FBI had paid a phone-forensics vendor, and said the claim that data was merely extracted rather than decrypted remained unproven.
- Burr-Feinstein bill still unseen; Steve expects vague language.
- WhatsApp announced end-to-end encryption with the Signal protocol; authentication of the other party is the key question; Steve plans a detailed look the next week.
- Domain transfer: leaving Network Solutions required a multi-day authorisation code delay and a retention offer; Steve asks for registrar recommendations; Leo mentions Hover and Google Domains, and a lock-in after registrant changes on GoDaddy.
- Polymagnet: magnets with printed north-south patterns can behave like latches or springs.
- Sleep formula feedback is mixed but mostly positive per Steve; he calls Vitamin D and magnesium foundational.
- SpinRite and fusion drives: SpinRite 6.1 is expected to control or disable the SSD tier using ATA extensions; Level 2 is the right mode meanwhile.
- Shodan shows many internet-exposed SMB services (for example media players), meaning Badlock aside, port 445 exposure is already a problem.
- Locky: an MSP restored files from hourly incremental backups after a user's infection spread to network drives over a weekend; versioned backups are the answer.
- An unencrypted registration form is a bad sign even if the POST is secure; Steve suggests the Apple-FBI attention makes it a good time to raise security.
- SQL Server TDE protects drives when servers are powered down or stolen, but key is in memory while online, so it adds little against live attacks.
- Norton's reputation check removed Never10 because the freshly issued SHA-256 signing certificate had no reputation; Steve notes the trend toward whitelisting.
- Let's Encrypt means more traffic corporate inspection devices cannot see; Steve now accepts company TLS-interception appliances with employee awareness.
- file.pizza uses WebRTC for browser-to-browser transfers; it is convenient but not authenticated, so encrypt files first (ZIP encryption, AxCrypt); Leo uses transfer.sh.
- SNMP: default community strings can expose or let attackers change devices; enable only on the LAN side and change passwords.
- VLANs are a management feature with a history of VLAN hopping, and useless for wireless IoT where frames are in the air.
- Self-encrypting drives and ATA passwords protect against theft but manufacturers have reportedly opened drives for law enforcement; Steve still prefers TrueCrypt, notes VeraCrypt's long unlock times, and mentions Intel Management Engine concerns.
- Never10 had reached about 107,000 downloads.

# Other segments

- Banter about the 555 timer chip, a cartoon of how the FBI cracked the phone, Safari's standards support, Leo's Linux distribution experiments (NixOS, Antergos), and feedback on Steve's PC build.
