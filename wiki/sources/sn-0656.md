# Summary

Steve criticizes the US response to election-security concerns: Homeland Security designated election systems as critical infrastructure and information-sharing centers (ISACs) exist, yet closed proprietary voting machines remain, and he argues for open, standards-based development like TLS. A discussion of security versus convenience follows, prompted by reports that the NSA tracked bitcoin addresses in its traffic collection years ago and by Russia giving Telegram a deadline to hand over keys. Symantec's 2018 report shows cryptojacking up 8,500%, spear-phishing behind 71% of targeted attacks, rising software-update supply-chain attacks, and Android devices mostly out of date. Netflix opened its bug bounty to all registered hackers via Bugcrowd (up to $15,000), and Dropbox published a researcher-friendly disclosure policy (no DMCA or CFAA action for good-faith research, but reasonable time to fix). Other items: the Stack Overflow developer survey, the CLOUD Act slipped into the omnibus spending bill, Firefox planning a built-in abusive-ad blocker, homograph (Unicode lookalike) domain defenses, Facebook Messenger on Android collecting call and SMS logs, a pre-announced critical Drupal fix for the next day, and DNS over HTTPS (Cloudflare and Mozilla's trusted recursive resolver pilot). The closing topic is the just-ratified TLS 1.3: academic review, dropped weak algorithms, modern curves (ChaCha20-Poly1305, x25519, Ed25519), fewer handshake round trips, resumption, downgrade protection, and mandatory forward secrecy over objections from finance.

# Key points

- Voting machines: Steve wants open, vetted election standards; ISAC bureaucracy does not substitute.
- NSA programs (OAKSTAR, MONEYROCKET) scanned traffic for bitcoin addresses; Steve's lesson is that relying on technology you do not control gives opportunistic, not absolute, security.
- Symantec: 71% of targeted attacks start with spear-phishing; only about 20% of Android devices run the latest major version; software supply-chain attacks nearly doubled (CCleaner, NotPetya via an accounting update); IoT cryptojacking is doubtful because devices are underpowered.
- Bug bounties: Bugcrowd-run public Netflix program covering web and mobile apps; Dropbox's policy aims to set an industry standard and asks for reasonable time before disclosure.
- Stack Overflow survey: JavaScript top language, Python rising, Rust most loved, VB6 most dreaded.
- CLOUD Act (Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data) lets US authorities compel data stored abroad and enables foreign-government access agreements; EFF calls it a backdoor around the Fourth Amendment.
- Homograph attacks: Edge shows Punycode, Chrome shows Unicode in the address bar, Firefox's network.IDN_show_punycode can be set to true; Phish.AI's IDN Protect extension warns for Chrome.
- Facebook Messenger on Android quietly logged calls and SMS metadata under older permission models.
- Drupal 7 and 8 pre-announced a highly critical update for 28 March with exploits expected within hours.
- DNS over HTTPS (DoH): Google offers it; Mozilla and Cloudflare pilot a trusted recursive resolver in Firefox 60 (settings under about:config "trr"); Steve expects DoH to win.
- Disk Cleanup with "Clean up system files" can reclaim ~15 GB on Windows.
- TLS 1.3 ratified: up to ten interoperable implementations, TRON academic workshops, MD5 and legacy suites removed, faster handshakes, resumption features, protection against negotiation downgrade, perfect forward secrecy mandatory; protocol timeline: SSL 2.0 1995, SSL 3.0 1996, TLS 1.0 1999, 1.1 2006, 1.2 2008, 1.3 2018.
