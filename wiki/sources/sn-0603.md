# Summary

The March 2017 Patch Tuesday finally delivered fixes for the flaws that were skipped in February, including Flash, the graphics component, an SMB client flaw, the PDF library, Hyper-V and Edge/IE. Steve then explains Google's Invisible reCAPTCHA, which works silently by leveraging the visitor's Google reputation. Other stories are Check Point's discovery of malware preinstalled on 36 Android devices, a surge of "Find My iPhone" phishing aimed at stolen phones, a US Naval Academy study showing WiFi MAC address randomization is largely ineffective, and a severe, hard-to-patch remote code execution flaw in Apache Struts 2 that took a Canadian tax site offline. He also covers TalkTalk blocking TeamViewer, a critique of the Confide messenger, and Tim Berners-Lee's web anniversary essay. The feature segment is Steve's reading of WikiLeaks' Vault 7 release of CIA hacking documents: nothing new technically, since it matches topics the show has covered, but it shows even elite agencies cannot secure their own assets, which is an argument against encryption backdoors.

# Key points

- March Patch Tuesday fixed seven critical bundles plus eleven important ones, covering many issues first seen exploited in the wild; Steve prefers waiting for Microsoft over third-party micro-patches. Microsoft's switch to cumulative updates is understandable from a developer's view.
- A Windows 10 File Explorer ad for OneDrive and Office 365 appeared, which Steve and Leo dislike.
- Invisible reCAPTCHA is rendered off-screen and approves most visitors without interaction, because Google's cookies identify them; it falls back to a visible challenge when unsure. Steve notes the privacy implication of Google being present on all sites that use it.
- Check Point found Loki and SLocker-style malware preinstalled in the ROM of 36 Android devices at two client companies; some could only be removed by reflashing. Leo suspects older phones bought from third-party resellers. Steve suggests scanning any secondhand phone with a mobile antimalware tool before use.
- Find My iPhone phishing: Brian Krebs reported organized groups texting owners of lost iPhones with fake Apple login pages to unlock and resell the devices.
- MAC randomization study (US Naval Academy): many Android phones do not randomize at all, others leak the real MAC in some frames, an attack defeated randomization on about 96 percent of Android phones, and a flaw in low-level control frames allowed tracking of every device including iPhones.
- Apache Struts 2 flaw in the Jakarta multipart parser let attackers inject OGNL expressions through the Content-Type header. Because Struts is compiled into each application, every app must be rebuilt rather than a library updated. Cisco saw heavy exploitation, with payloads such as IRC bouncers and DoS bots, and Statistics Canada took its tax-filing site offline.
- ISP TalkTalk blocked TeamViewer network-wide to counter tech-support scammers (its own customer data had earlier leaked via a SQL mistake); customers had little recourse beyond a VPN.
- Confide: Quarkslab's reverse engineering showed the server could read messages through a man-in-the-middle position; Matthew Green dismissed the design, while Steve recommends Signal or Threema with fingerprint verification and notes that end-to-end encryption is only as safe as both endpoints.
- Tim Berners-Lee's essay on data control, misinformation and political ads left Steve and Leo unconvinced about practical solutions, which they feared would mean heavy government intervention; Steve wants a usability layer that hides URLs and domains behind brand names.
- Listener Q&A: storing an unsalted SHA-1 hash of a Social Security number is not secure because nine digits can be brute-forced; IoT TVs should sit on an isolated network segment.
- Errata: the shorted mic-plug trick does not work on laptops where jack functions are software assigned.
- Vault 7: WikiLeaks published 8,761 documents on CIA tooling for phones, routers, PCs and smart TVs. Apple said many iOS issues were already patched; Notepad++ released a fix against a DLL swap (scilexer.dll) described in the documents. Steve's point: these are targeted tools, unlike NSA mass collection; if elite agencies cannot protect their own tools, nobody should be trusted with an encryption golden key. Leo adds that the Vulnerabilities Equities Process for disclosing flaws appears not to have been followed.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week about ads in Windows 10 File Explorer.
- Game of Life digression: after last week's Life clock, listeners found a version of Conway's Life running inside itself.
- Potatoes grown in simulated Mars conditions by the International Potato Center.
- Listener feedback on Syncthing as a BTSync/Dropbox replacement; a ZFS user's checksum errors traced by SpinRite to bad drive cables, with Steve explaining why CRC cabling errors matter since CRCs can miss errors.
- Light banter about Pi Day, the "Gibson" and "SQRL" names appearing by coincidence in the Vault 7 files, and the TWiT schedule shifting with daylight saving.
