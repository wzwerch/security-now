# Summary

Steve reports that GRC now offers ECDHE perfect forward secrecy, which earns the top rating in the Calomel Firefox add-on, made possible because every major browser now defends itself against BEAST; the change broke SSL for his old Eudora mail client and iOS devices until the cipher list is fixed. Google announced it is fully on 2048-bit certificates and PFS, and Yahoo promised data-centre encryption and optional HTTPS by early 2014. CryptoLocker figures: a Massachusetts police department paid the ransom, and Bitdefender sinkholing of the malware's generated domains saw about 12,000 distinct victims' IPs in under a week, implying millions in ransom; Microsoft data shows roughly 79 percent of infections in the US, suggesting targeted email delivery in zipped fake PDFs. Bitcoin peaked near 900 dollars after Congressional hearings. Further topics: the exposed and poorly secured baseband "second operating system" in every phone, the HTTP/2 working group's debate over mandatory encryption and "relaxed TLS", a Freedom of Information case about a US Internet kill switch, an MIT study showing GCC optimisation silently removes security checks, and the Coin and Loop wallet gadgets. The main explainer covers RADIUS, a 22-year-old centralised authentication protocol, and why a 12-character password limit for a VPN account is acceptable.

# Key points

- Perfect forward secrecy: ephemeral key agreement separate from certificate authentication means later theft of a server key cannot decrypt recorded traffic. GRC moved CBC suites above RC4 now that browsers mitigate BEAST.
- Google completed migration to 2048-bit keys and PFS cipher suites and is encrypting inter-data-centre links after the NSA tap revelations; Yahoo followed with a promise for 2014.
- CryptoLocker: payment of two bitcoin by a police department; Bitdefender's sinkhole count of about 12,016 contacting IPs in under a week; Microsoft (calling it Crilock) shows about 79 percent US infections; samples arrive in zip files hiding an executable named like a PDF.
- Domain-generation algorithms make the malware trackable but not easily blockable because the date-seeded names span many top-level domains.
- Bitcoin price spiked after positive US Congress hearings on virtual currency; Steve doubts long-term governmental tolerance.
- Baseband processors: an old ARM core runs a 1980s-90s cellular stack with little exploit mitigation and implicit trust in the cell tower, and often masters the application processor. Cheap software-defined radios will make fake base-station attacks easier.
- HTTP/2 debate: the working group is considering making encryption mandatory; "opportunistic encryption" or relaxed TLS would encrypt without authenticating the server, protecting against passive but not active interception. HSTS and browser preloading (as for GRC in Chrome) close the first-visit gap.
- A court case under information-freedom law revealed a Department of Homeland Security procedure (SOP 303) for shutting down Internet carriers in an emergency; Steve and Leo see it as plausible for isolating the US but troubling.
- MIT's STACK research showed GCC's optimiser discards null-pointer and overflow checks relying on undefined behaviour, affecting thousands of Debian packages.
- Coin: a programmable-looking card that Steve deduces is a static card acting as a proxy for stored cards via the vendor's cloud; Leo is sceptical of its marketing and viability. Loop: an inductive coil that emulates a magnetic stripe swipe, working with about 90 percent of terminals.
- SpinRite with SMART: running it puts a drive under load so SMART bars reveal problems; a listener returned a new drive that showed them.
- RADIUS (Remote Authentication Dial-In User Service, 1991) centralises authentication, authorisation and accounting over UDP; access points, VPN endpoints and "WPA2 Enterprise" Wi-Fi forward credentials, protected by a shared secret, to one server backed by SQL, LDAP or a file. FreeRADIUS is the common open implementation.
- Why the 12-character limit is tolerable: brute force must be online through the RADIUS round trip, a 66-symbol alphabet gives about 72.5 bits for a random 12-character password, and the credential mainly protects the provider's network rather than user data.
