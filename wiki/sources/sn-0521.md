# Summary

The episode begins year eleven of the show and is a dense news catch-up. Steve explains why the Stagefright patch for one of the six critical bugs was itself flawed: the check mixed 64-bit and 32-bit unsigned values so oversized chunk sizes slipped through, producing an undersized allocation and a buffer overrun; a security firm publicized this, creating a seventh vulnerability entry that the updated Zimperium tester flags. He uses it to argue that security is difficult and that C's integer behavior is a trap. Other items: an Android Google Admin sandbox bypass disclosed after Google missed its own 90-day deadline, an Ars Technica analysis of Windows 10 network traffic with privacy controls off, malvertising campaigns on Yahoo!, eBay and the Huffington Post chaining scripts to the Angler exploit kit and ransomware, Reuters' report that Kaspersky seeded false positives to harm rivals (which it denies), a Lenovo BIOS technique that drops its own executables into Windows at boot, FireEye findings on poorly protected fingerprint sensors, the New York Times account of AT&T's NSA cooperation, and the Wuala shutdown. The closing segment reviews three commentaries on web advertising, with Steve and Leo disagreeing about ethics and economics.

# Key points

- **Stagefright regression:** the patch for the MP4 tx3g integer overflow compared a 64-bit chunk size against a 32-bit limit in a way that never fired for large values; another firm disclosed it; a seventh CVE appeared and the Zimperium tester added a seventh check, with some users reporting suspicious MMS messages; Lookout's tester was not updated.
- **Why it is hard:** small type-size mistakes in C and patch review blind spots; Leo mentions functional programming as a counter-trend.
- **Google Admin app (MWR):** a sandbox bypass lets a malicious app read the app's files; disclosed after months with no fix and after Google missed its own 90-day policy; Steve suggests removing the app until patched.
- **Windows 10 traffic (Ars Technica):** with Cortana, telemetry and cloud features disabled, the OS still fetches a Bing file containing an apparently persistent machine identifier, MSN tile data over plain HTTP, and talks to a OneDrive-related host; Leo suggests activation checks; Steve keeps arguing the OS has become data-gathering, while Leo says disconnecting is the real control.
- **Malvertising:** Malwarebytes tracked ad chains starting at Yahoo! and later AOL's advertising network, via cloud-hosted and forum-hosted scripts, ending in the Angler kit delivering CryptoWall and ad-fraud malware; the Huffington Post saw four such incidents in eight months.
- **Kaspersky allegations:** Reuters cites two ex-employees saying the company tricked rivals' AV into flagging benign files; Kaspersky denies and describes a harmless-file experiment submitted to VirusTotal that was copied by many vendors.
- **Lenovo BIOS injection:** on many laptops the firmware replaces a Windows boot-check executable and spawns its own services, surviving reformatting; similar in design to Absolute's CompuTrace anti-theft agent; Microsoft's Windows Platform Binary Table documents an official mechanism for firmware-provided executables; Lenovo offered a fix but did not push it. Steve calls the behavior rootkit-like.
- **Android fingerprint security (FireEye):** a vendor stored raw fingerprint images in a world-readable file; many vendors failed to place the fingerprint sensor inside ARM TrustZone; all patched after notification.
- **AT&T and the NSA:** New York Times and Snowden documents describe a particularly cooperative relationship with surveillance gear in at least 17 US hubs.
- **Wuala:** encrypted cloud storage service closing (read-only then shutdown in November); Steve's model is client-side encryption on top of a large provider.
- **Browsing in a VM:** Steve reports mixed feedback; VM isolation is good but clumsy for links and downloads.
- **Advertising debate:** Gruber and iMore's Rene Ritchie on page weight and third-party scripts, Marco Arment's ethics essay, the earlier pop-up blocker era; Leo argues for supporting sites, Steve for user defenses and first-party ads.
