# Summary

Steve first notes that an online prankster used a scan for exposed printer ports to make about 150,000 printers print a message, and explains that most were exposed because Universal Plug and Play on home routers lets devices open ports without permission, repeating the long-standing advice to turn UPnP off. He covers the Vizio settlement with the FTC and New Jersey over screen-content tracking, Windows SMB client crash code released after Microsoft missed a 90-day deadline, and research showing Android pattern locks can be recovered from video of the finger. A pacemaker's data was used to challenge a homeowner's story in an arson case. A mobile app scanning firm found 76 popular iOS apps that fail to validate TLS certificates, leaving them open to interception, and conflicting court rulings on foreign-stored email warrants add confusion. Further items: SQL Slammer traffic resurging, Cellebrite's leaked material turning out to be recycled jailbreak code, the worrying direction of the W3C Encrypted Media Extensions debate, and listener questions on salts and IRC botnets. The closing story explains how Russian slot machines with weak pseudorandom generators were beaten by predicting their state.

# Key points

- Printer stunt: a script targeted devices exposing the Internet Printing Protocol, LPD or raw port 9100; nothing was truly compromised beyond printing a message, but it shows how many printers are public. Steve blames UPnP and suggests disabling it, rebooting and testing from outside with a port scanner, mapping any needed gaming ports statically.
- Vizio: televisions captured samples of screen pixels every second to identify what viewers watched and sent them to servers without clear consent; the settlement included payment and deletion of older data. Leo notes viewing data has long been sold to networks.
- SMB client flaw: a researcher published a proof-of-concept that crashes Windows clients connecting to a malicious server after Microsoft did not patch within 90 days; US-CERT warned it might allow code execution with kernel privileges. Mitigation for enterprises is blocking outbound SMB ports.
- Pattern lock: a computer-vision study recovered over 95 percent of Android lock patterns within five attempts from video of the finger alone, and complex patterns proved easier to break than simple ones.
- Pacemaker data: Ohio police used a cardiologist's reading of the suspect's pacemaker logs to dispute his account of escaping a house fire.
- iOS TLS validation: a scanning service found 76 popular iOS apps (over 18 million downloads) that accept invalid certificates, 24 at medium and 19 at high risk with credentials exposed; names withheld pending disclosure. The OS cannot fix it because apps need freedom for pinning and private certificate authorities. Interim advice: avoid sensitive actions on public WiFi. Steve notes developers pasting unreviewed code is a cause.
- Email warrants: a Philadelphia magistrate ordered Google to hand over foreign-stored email, contrary to the appeals court ruling in the Microsoft case; Google will appeal. Steve's takeaway is trust no one, meaning encrypt yourself before sending.
- SQL Slammer: Check Point saw its traffic spike again late 2016, probably because forgotten or revived old SQL Server instances kept scanning.
- Cellebrite leak: contents appeared to rely heavily on jailbreak community tools repackaged for forensics, as an iOS forensics expert noted.
- W3C EME: Chrome made its DRM implementation impossible to disable, and the W3C declined to protect security researchers from DRM-related legal threats, working only on narrow protections for those following reporting best practices.
- Q&A: salts derived from user input offer little protection versus a random per-user salt; botnets use IRC because it is simple, widely implemented and lets the operator stay hidden while commands propagate.
- Slot machines: after Russia banned gambling, used machines were reverse-engineered and found to use predictable pseudorandom number generators; attackers watched outcomes to infer internal state and a phone vibrated a split second before the profitable button press, earning large sums at US casinos. Steve contrasts this with using proper entropy for SQRL identities.
