# Summary

The first show of 2014 (now on Tuesdays) catches up on holiday news after Steve's visit to Leo's New Year's Eve marathon. The most disturbing item is a Reuters report that the NSA paid RSA 10 million dollars, about a third of its annual revenue that year, to make the Dual_EC_DRBG random number generator the default in the BSAFE library; Steve shows his own 1992 BSAFE copy and the 2009 release note that made it the default, and notes several speakers are withdrawing from the RSA conference. He then previews next week's deep dive into the NSA's ANT catalogue of implants and hardware exploits published by Der Spiegel, using the iPhone implant DropoutJeep as an example, and stresses that these are targeted tools unlike the corruption of a shared random number generator. Further items include webcam LED indicators that can be bypassed by reflashing the controller, an unexplained service listening on TCP port 32764 in many home routers (some exposed on the Internet), three CryptoLocker successors, Snapchat's Find Friends API abuse leaking 4.6 million usernames and numbers, McAfee's rebranding as Intel Security, UK porn filters blocking legitimate sites, Mozilla's asm.js speed progress, and a few listener and personal asides.

# Key points

- RSA/NSA: per Reuters, an NSA contract worth 10 million dollars made Dual_EC_DRBG the BSAFE default before NIST approved it, and the NSA then cited its existing use to push approval; the deal was handled by business staff, not technologists; Schneier had called the generator's weakness a back door. Steve contrasts the three other NIST generators (hash, HMAC and cipher based).
- ANT catalogue (Der Spiegel): a menu of implants and hardware for routers, firewalls, phones and BIOS firmware with prices from a few dollars to tens of thousands; Schneier is writing a post per item. Steve sees persistent firmware modification as a recurring theme and notes no evidence of vendor collusion.
- DropoutJeep (2008 era): an iPhone implant to pull files, texts, contacts, location, microphone and camera data via SMS or GPRS, initially installed by close access; Steve points to the baseband processor as an attack surface and cautions that the slides are dated.
- Webcam indicator: a laptop camera's LED was wired to the sensor's standby line, but the controller firmware is loaded by software, so researchers replaced it to disable standby and keep the LED dark. Advice: cover the lens physically, and laptops should have mechanical shutters. A malware victim in Steve's circle saw a ransom screen showing a photo taken by her own camera.
- Port 32764: discovered by Eloi Vanderbeken on a Linksys gateway and found on Netgear, Cisco and other routers; connecting returns a short identifier string; five models were confirmed exposed on the WAN. Steve says to check with ShieldsUP custom port probe and firewall the port; purpose unknown.
- CryptoLocker successors: "CryptoLocker 2" (a different author, C#, bitcoin only, 3DES, key files per document, spreads via fake software activators), a planned kit called PrisonLocker/PowerLocker by an amateur assembly programmer, and a flawed Delphi "Locker" using AES-CTR that can be decrypted without payment.
- Snapchat: researchers calling themselves Gibson Security (unrelated to Steve) published the reverse-engineered API after Snapchat ignored months of warnings; the custom request token was only obfuscation, and without rate limiting a third party enumerated 4.6 million usernames mapped to phone numbers at roughly 5,000 lookups per minute.
- McAfee becomes Intel Security.
- UK default porn filters have blocked health and charity sites and even the website of the MP who championed them.
- Mozilla's asm.js, a strict compilable subset of JavaScript, now runs within about 1.5 times native speed, which Steve prefers to Google's Native Client.
