# Summary

Steve notes that a new ETH Zurich paper on server-side attacks against password managers (Dashlane, LastPass, Bitwarden) just appeared and will get a full treatment next week, with "no hair on fire". He then explains that the AI.com Super Bowl failure was likely CPU-bound dynamic page generation rather than bandwidth, citing Linux Mint's forum slowdowns from AI crawlers. An editorial sees Microsoft retreating from its Secure Future push after Charlie Bell was replaced as security chief, and Windows 11 26H1 turns out to be a Snapdragon X2-only release. Chrome 145 ships Device Bound Session Credentials, which bind session cookies to hardware so stolen cookies cannot be replayed. News items: more countries restricting youth social media, Russia's Roskomnadzor removing YouTube, WhatsApp, Facebook and Instagram from its DNS, Discord's age assurance rollout, active exploitation of the WinRAR CVE-2025-8088 flaw (fixed in 7.13), leaked screenshots of Paragon's Graphite spyware panel, 30 fake AI-assistant Chrome extensions (260,000 users), 287 extensions leaking browsing history (37.4 million users), and the AgreeTo Outlook add-in hijack that stole about 4,000 credentials. Steve shares cautious thoughts on AI "vibe" coding and unit testing, and a listener tip on Cloudflare's free WARP VPN. The main segment recounts obtaining a three-year code signing certificate from IdenTrust after DigiCert's pricing changes, including the CA/Browser Forum requirement for an attestation letter from a lawyer or CPA, and explains why such vetting exists even though signatures only prove integrity and identity, not safety.

# Key points

- Password manager research (ETH Zurich plus Italian collaborators): server-compromise scenarios against Dashlane, LastPass and Bitwarden, e.g. protocol downgrade to old crypto combined with weak passwords; vendors responded; open source (Bitwarden) made the analysis easier. Full discussion deferred to the next episode.
- Dynamic CMS-driven sites cost CPU and database load per page; Steve argues bandwidth is no longer the main limit, and suggests static generation, OPcache and Redis caching; Linux Mint upgraded its forum server 10x CPU after bot and AI crawler load.
- Microsoft: Charlie Bell stepped back from security leadership, replaced by Hayete Gallot; Seriously Risky Business argues Microsoft only prioritized security under government pressure (after the CSRB report on the Chinese email breach) and is now focused on selling security products. Steve stresses reduced security effort only shows up later as breaches.
- Windows 11 26H1 is a scoped release only for new Qualcomm Snapdragon X2 ARM machines; everyone else stays on 25H2 until 26H2.
- Chrome 145 supports Device Bound Session Credentials (DBSC): session cookies are tied to a TPM or secure enclave key so a stolen cookie is useless elsewhere; requires server-side support; previously described in episode 1021.
- Age restrictions: Kazakhstan, Moldova and Romania consider youth social media bans, with newer laws dropping parental overrides. Discord clarified it is not demanding scans from everyone; it uses age prediction and only asks for a face scan or ID (processed on device or deleted) for adult-content areas. Steve warns that third-party verifiers have failed to delete IDs.
- Roskomnadzor removed YouTube, WhatsApp, Facebook and Instagram from Russia's national DNS after labelling Meta extremist and also blocked Tor, Windscribe, APK Mirror and the BBC.
- WinRAR CVE-2025-8088 (path traversal via alternate data streams, fixed in 7.13 on July 30, 2025) is exploited by Russian, Chinese and criminal actors per Google; Stairwell found over 80% of monitored environments still ran vulnerable versions. Steve found 7.12 on one of his own PCs.
- Paragon Graphite: leaked demo photos show it extracting messages from WhatsApp, Signal, Telegram, Line, Snapchat, TikTok and others, illustrating that spyware reads data after decryption on the device.
- LayerX "AiFrame": 30 Chrome extensions posing as Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini/Grok assistants (260,000 users) loaded remote server-controlled iframes, allowing silent behaviour change after store review; some were featured by the Chrome Web Store.
- QContinuum research: 287 Chrome extensions (37.4 million installs, about 1% of Chrome users) leak full browsing history to data brokers such as Similarweb; the Chrome store hosts roughly 240,000 extensions.
- AgreeTo / Koi Security: an abandoned Outlook add-in whose manifest pointed at a Vercel URL; an attacker claimed the dangling subdomain and served a phishing kit inside Outlook, stealing over 4,000 Microsoft credentials and other data. Steve criticizes Microsoft's URL-based add-in design and the lack of protection against domain takeover.
- AI coding thoughts: unit-tested modular generation may help, but non-coders cannot diagnose subtle faults, and AI "fixes" may mask root causes (Copilot parser example); Steve will keep writing his own code.
- Certificate lifetimes: from February 24, 2026 DigiCert TLS certs max 199 days and validation reuse periods shorten; Steve plans to move GRC to Let's Encrypt (domain validation) after his prepaid DigiCert certificates, while code signing certificate lifetimes also shrink.
- Code signing: DigiCert's bring-your-own-HSM plan is effectively $600/year; Steve bought a three-year IdenTrust certificate (about $538) for his own HSM. The CA/Browser Forum Code Signing Baseline Requirements (v3.8.0) require verification of the organization and, in this case, an attestation letter from a licensed attorney or CPA that the CA verifies by phone; individual validation needs face-to-face checks. Steve intends to dual-sign with the DigiCert certificate until it expires in August.
- Signatures prove only unmodified code and signer identity, not benign behaviour; because platforms (except Linux) refuse unsigned code, criminals try to obtain fraudulent certificates, which is why vetting is tightened. Leo notes ACME cannot help because it proves domain control, not identity.
