# Summary

Steve contrasts "Internet Background Radiation", a term he coined in 1999 for stray packets, with "Internet Foreground Radiation", the deliberate scanning of new websites. The news portion begins with mobile-security firm iVerify's claim that a use-after-free in iMessage's nickname feature (patched in iOS 18.3) was used in zero-click attacks on high-value targets, which Apple strongly disputes. Two npm supply-chain stories follow (84 malicious packages removed, and a remote-access trojan pushed into about 16 GlueStack UI packages). Nextgov reported that the NSA and CISA assessed Comcast and Digital Realty as likely Salt Typhoon victims while outside counsel at some carriers reportedly told incident responders not to look for the intrusion. Matthew Green confirms that X's XChat stores users' private keys on its servers, so it is not true end-to-end encryption; a report suggests Russia may be tracing Telegram users through network metadata rather than breaking encryption. Microsoft will finally block .library-ms and .search-ms attachments in new Outlook, Bitsight finds more than 40,000 open cameras, and listener topics include running SpinRite on encrypted drives and an LLM summary of Steve's changing views on Microsoft security. The main segment draws on HUMAN Security research showing web scanner bots dominate early traffic to a new site, locate it via new-domain and certificate-transparency feeds, and mostly hunt for .env and Git files.

# Key points

- iVerify "NICKNAME" report: anomalous crashes on iPhones of people tied to political campaigns, media, AI companies and governments in the US and EU (about 0.0001% of crash logs across 50,000 phones); a use-after-free in the imagent process triggered by rapid nickname updates, patched in iOS 18.3; circumstantial signs of exploitation include an Apple threat notification to an EU official and bulk attachment deletion. Victims had connections to Chinese-targeted topics or Salt Typhoon, but attribution is not established. Apple's Ivan Krstic says it was a conventional bug with no evidence of attack. iVerify recommends Lockdown Mode for high-risk users; Steve notes locked-down iPhones hamper independent forensics.
- npm: 84 malicious packages removed in one week (two intended to wipe production systems); the GlueStack compromise (a maintainer account hijacked, RAT added to about 16 packages with nearly a million weekly downloads) was attributed to the same actor as the earlier rand-user-agent attack.
- Salt Typhoon: Nextgov, citing three sources, reports NSA judged Comcast likely affected and CISA listed Digital Realty (300+ data centers, clients including AWS, Google, Microsoft) as potentially compromised; Comcast says it found no evidence on its enterprise network. Reported that incident-response staff at two major carriers were told by outside counsel not to look for it. Hundreds of organizations were notified; FBI calls it the most egregious nation-state breach in US history; initial access used old flaws (e.g. 2018 vulnerabilities) and stolen credentials; lawful-intercept systems were reached; CISA staffing cuts and the dissolved Cyber Safety Review Board worry commentators. Marc Rogers argues for patching and maintaining existing infrastructure rather than rip-and-replace.
- XChat: Matthew Garrett and Matthew Green note there is no forward secrecy (each message encrypted under a long-term key), and users' private keys are held on X servers via a three-server Juicebox secret-sharing scheme without HSMs, so X could access messages; the code is C/C++ rather than Rust. Steve contrasts this with Apple Advanced Data Protection and Signal; Facebook Messenger and Instagram also keep keys server-side (with HSMs).
- Telegram: reporting suggests Russian authorities may be exploiting Telegram's network infrastructure and metadata (who contacts whom) rather than breaking message encryption; Steve again advises against Telegram for privacy.
- Outlook (Web and new Outlook) will block .library-ms and .search-ms files from early July 2025; .library-ms was used in a CVE-2025-24054 NTLM-hash phishing campaign and .search-ms in attacks since 2022. Steve argues email attachments should be default-deny allow-listed, not block-listed.
- Bitsight: more than 40,000 cameras are streaming openly without credentials (about 14,000 in the US, concentrated in California and Texas); advice is to check external reachability, change default passwords, disable remote access, update firmware, and restrict access through firewalls or VPNs.
- SpinRite works the same on VeraCrypt, TrueCrypt or BitLocker drives because it sees opaque blocks; its Real Time Monitor just shows noise.
- A listener built a retrieval-augmented LLM over the show's transcripts; asked how Steve's view of Microsoft security changed, it described a drift from cautious optimism (around 2015) to growing skepticism, with a 2025 view acknowledging legacy complexity.
- Companies are being sued over AI scraping; Steve argues respecting robots.txt and paywalls should be required, while Leo says Cloudflare reports many scrapers ignore it.
- HUMAN Security honeypot study: scanner bots made up about 70% of bot traffic on a new site's first days; they find new sites through newly-registered-domain feeds, certificate transparency logs (e.g. CertStream) and search listings; with over 90% of sites sharing IP addresses, SNI means scanning IPs alone is no longer enough. About 33.6% of probes sought .env files, 33.5% Git repository files, 23.4% PHP/WordPress files; campaigns named include Mozi.a, a Mirai variant called Jaws and the "Romanian Distillery" scanner. Steve's advice: never expose anything before it is hardened, use IP allow-listing, and trap requests for files your site does not have.
