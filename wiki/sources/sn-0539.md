# Summary

The last episode of 2015 starts with Leo's question about a news story in which a hacker with a small hardware device compromised patrons of an open cafe Wi-Fi; Steve confirms the technique is plausible (promiscuous capture, forced disconnects and spoofing of known network names to become a man in the middle) and recommends a VPN or a travel router. A web-tracking audit shows hundreds of cookies on some of the top 1,000 sites. The headline story is Juniper's out-of-cycle ScreenOS patch for unauthorized code: researchers found a hard-coded SSH/Telnet password and, more strikingly, a changed constant in a Dual_EC_DRBG random number generator plus a bug that exposed its raw output, enabling passive VPN decryption by whoever knew the change. Other items: Oracle's FTC settlement about Java update practices, a Grub2 bug in which pressing backspace 28 times bypasses the boot password, WhatsApp being briefly blocked in Brazil, a misreported remark by Hillary Clinton, and Microsoft's increasingly forceful Windows 10 upgrade dialog. Eight listener questions cover VPNs against ISP interception, an overseen library Wi-Fi network's logging, detecting surveillance, password length disclosure, key escrow versus backdoors, a phishing copy of a Microsoft notice, IPv6 and NAT, and checking an IMAP TLS certificate with OpenSSL.

# Key points

- Open Wi-Fi: promiscuous capture is like a hub; attackers can force clients to disassociate, then pose as remembered networks to intercept. Defenses: tether to a phone, use a VPN on devices, or a travel router establishing a VPN tunnel; email and other unencrypted protocols are the usual weak point. Leo bought a hardware VPN router for a cruise.
- Web Privacy Census: a crawler of the top 1,000 sites found some sites setting hundreds of cookies, showing the cost of perverse incentives for ad and tracking scripts.
- Juniper ScreenOS: Juniper disclosed unauthorized code in its source repository and two issues. One was a malicious hard-coded password for SSH and Telnet. The second changed the Q constant in the Dual_EC_DRBG generator (in 2012) and a loop bug meant an intermediate generator was not reseeded, so DRBG output was exposed. Anyone who chose the constants could recover state and decrypt VPN sessions. Matthew Green, HD Moore and others reverse-engineered the patch. Attribution unknown; Steve uses it as a real example of why backdoors cannot stay private.
- Dual_EC_DRBG history: an NIST-approved generator whose constants of unknown origin permit prediction; RSA's BSAFE reportedly made it default after NSA payment.
- Oracle settled with the FTC: Java updates removed only the most recent previous version, leaving older vulnerable versions; Oracle must notify users and offer uninstall. Steve notes the browser plugin was the risk and he has no Java in his browser.
- Grub2 versions from 2009 to 2.02 had an integer flaw; pressing backspace 28 times at the password prompt drops to a rescue shell. Local-only; distros issued patches.
- Brazil: a lower court ordered WhatsApp blocked for 48 hours over refusal to produce messages in a criminal case; an appeals court lifted it after about 12 hours and suggested larger fines. WhatsApp is installed on over 90 percent of Android devices in Brazil.
- Misreporting: Ars headline claimed Clinton wanted a Manhattan-like project to break encryption; Steve says she asked tech and law enforcement to cooperate.
- Microsoft's Get Windows 10 dialog now offered only "Upgrade Now" or upgrade later.
- DVR/camera tip: reboot a TiVo-style device then pull the plug, since cutting power mid-recording damages disk surfaces.
- Q&A highlights: a VPN would pass through ISP certificate interception, though ISPs could block VPNs in a hypothetical future. A library Wi-Fi administrator reports extensive unavoidable client logging and DMCA notices. There is no reliable way to detect advanced surveillance malware. Revealing password length in conversation is low risk. Steve's phone-by-phone escrow proposal differs from a single master backdoor but still requires an enormous protection obligation. IPv6 will remove NAT but not stateful firewalls. A listener can inspect their IMAP server certificate with OpenSSL's s_client to detect corporate interception.
