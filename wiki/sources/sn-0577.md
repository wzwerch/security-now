# Summary

A Q&A episode. Steve opens with a note from a Flip Feng Shui author praising the previous week's coverage, then comments on Apple's iPhone announcement (his phone is "good enough", as Windows 7 is for many). Google patched an EXIF/JPEG parsing flaw and malicious apps were found with millions of downloads, underscoring Android's patching problem. A magazine write-up of the Blue Hydra tool shows how classic Bluetooth and Bluetooth LE devices advertise identifying data. A Romanian datacenter went down for ten hours after a test of its gas fire suppression, because the extreme sound pressure pushed disk heads off track mid-write. Steve corrects himself that Doom was mostly written in C, notes the GPU-accelerated BlazingDB, and reads a SpinRite-on-SSD testimonial. Ten listener items follow: whether encrypted drives need wiping, how to tell the Ubiquiti EdgeRouter X from a managed switch, whether to use a commercial VPN versus a home OpenVPN endpoint, how a public key relates to a private key, how to stay reasonably secure without a smartphone, SQRL's protocol versioning, Windows 7 update bootstrapping, whether ECC memory stops Rowhammer, lithium-ion charging advice, and why Go code uses tabs.

# Key points

- Android: Nexus patches for a metadata (EXIF) image-parsing flaw; apps with up to roughly 2.5 million installs carried malicious code; OEM patch lag remains the problem.
- Bluetooth and BTLE privacy: devices keep advertising UUIDs and other stable identifiers even with randomised MACs; signal strength gives distance; WiFi has similar leaks; convenience beats privacy.
- ING datacenter incident: loud gas discharge exceeded 130 dB and caused write-time head misalignment, with lasting damage; Steve recalls similar vibration fixes and that SpinRite's ancestor was written to realign a failing drive.
- Samsung Galaxy Note 7 battery fires: a reminder of the energy stored in lithium cells.
- Doom was mostly C with a small amount of assembly; id Software published the source on GitHub.
- Encrypted drive wipe: if whole-disk encryption was applied before use, destroying the key header makes data unrecoverable; sectors spared before encryption could leak small amounts; optionally overwrite with zeros.
- EdgeRouter X versus managed switch: listings do not make the routing capability obvious; check the manufacturer manual; Steve still recommends the EdgeRouter X.
- VPN advice: centralised commercial VPN exit points concentrate traffic and attention; Steve prefers a home OpenVPN endpoint (pfSense, EdgeRouter CLI or Raspberry Pi) with dynamic DNS (Google Domains offers it); Leo notes VPNs still help with geo-restrictions and public WiFi; a VPN does not stop a rogue access point from enticing the device to join.
- Public key and private key: the private prime factor is hidden inside the modulus and recoverable only if factoring becomes easy, which is why a single flipped bit helps an attacker.
- Security for people without smartphones: desktop authenticator apps and password managers help; choose banks with decent privacy practice; caller ID spoofing and scam calls are widespread.
- SQRL carries a compound version field so clients and servers can negotiate future cryptography.
- Windows 7 clean-install updating is difficult; a Microsoft Answers walkthrough is on GRC's Link Farm.
- Rowhammer countermeasures: standard ECC corrects single flips but multi-bit flips occur; targeted row refresh in LPDDR4 and some DDR4 helps; Memtest86 6.2 adds a Rowhammer test; faster refresh is a stopgap.
- Lithium-ion: avoid deep discharge and avoid sitting at full charge for long; keeping a docked laptop near half charge extends life; Apple devices manage charging well.
- Go enforces tabs through gofmt, explaining the tabs-versus-spaces chart.
