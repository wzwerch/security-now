# Summary

The show begins with a days-long ransom DDoS against the VoIP provider VoIP.ms, which began on 16 September and knocked out telephony for its customers. The attackers used the name REvil, but Steve and others doubt that, noting the amateurish Pastebin note and the jump from 1 to 100 bitcoin. Steve's point is that web-focused request-per-second defences do not protect non-web protocols such as SIP, so VoIP providers are a new extortion target; similar attacks hit UK providers earlier in the month. Patch Tuesday fixed 86 flaws including the MSHTML zero-day (which was being used to drop a Cobalt Strike Beacon) but a PrintNightmare fix broke network printing for many administrators. Google announced an expanded auto-reset of unused app permissions for Android 6 and later, Chrome fixed its ninth and tenth exploited zero-days of 2021 within days, and a Have I Been Pwned alert about Epik turned out to be scraped WHOIS addresses. Leo also relays a Washington Post report that the FBI held the Kaseya REvil decryption key for about three weeks. The main segment introduces Cobalt Strike, a commercial adversary-simulation framework whose Beacon implant, cracked copies and malleable command-and-control have made it a favourite of both state actors and low-skill criminals.

# Key points

- VoIP.ms DDoS (from 16 Sept 2021): DNS servers and the web site were hit first, then the IP addresses when customers were told to use hosts-file entries; the company moved site and DNS to Cloudflare, but calling stayed disrupted for days while SMS, MMS and recordings recovered. A ransom demand reportedly rose from 1 to 100 bitcoin.
- Similar attacks hit at least three UK VoIP providers from 31 August (100 to 450 Gbps, aimed at SIP infrastructure). Steve doubts the REvil attribution and says there is no large-scale DDoS protection service for VoIP protocols; customers with month-to-month service can leave quickly.
- September Patch Tuesday: 86 vulnerabilities, two exploited zero-days; the MSHTML zero-day CVE-2021-40444 was fixed and had been used to download a malicious DLL that installs a Cobalt Strike Beacon.
- The fix for the last PrintNightmare flaw (CVE-2021-36958) broke network printing for many sites; uninstalling the update restores printing but re-exposes the flaw, which Vice Society, Magniber and Conti were using. The issue had been reported to Microsoft in December 2020.
- Android permission auto-reset (from Android 11) will be extended from December 2021 to Android 6 and later through Google Play services, but only enabled by default for apps targeting API 30; Steve would have preferred a one-time prompt for older apps.
- Washington Post report relayed by Leo: the FBI obtained the REvil/Kaseya decryption key via access to the gang's servers and withheld it for nearly three weeks to protect a planned takedown; Steve compares it to protecting intelligence sources.
- Chrome 93.0.4577.82 fixed 11 flaws including two exploited zero-days: CVE-2021-30632 (V8 out-of-bounds write) and CVE-2021-30633 (IndexedDB use-after-free), reported 8 September; Steve praises the five-day turnaround versus Microsoft's months.
- Have I Been Pwned domain notification flagged two scraped WHOIS-contact addresses of GRC in the Epik registrar breach (over 15 million addresses; leak published by Anonymous-linked actors). GRC itself was not breached.
- Cobalt Strike: created by Raphael Mudge in 2012, owned by HelpSystems since 2020, about $3,500 per seat. Its Beacon is an asynchronous implant with configurable check-in sleep and jitter, malleable C2 profiles that mimic other traffic, and many post-exploitation and injection features.
- Proofpoint data: two-thirds of Cobalt Strike campaigns in 2016-2018 came from known APT or large crime groups, but only 15% since 2019 are attributable, after a cracked version 4.0 leaked in March 2020. It was used in the SolarWinds intrusion by Russia's SVR and gives obfuscation because many actors share one tool.
- Listener tip relayed by Leo: Cobalt Strike C2 hosts often use hard-coded IPs, so blocking connections not preceded by a DNS lookup could help, though Steve calls that tricky to implement.

# Other segments

- Picture of the Week: an immaculately laced cable bundle, with Steve noting that overly neat bundling makes changes brittle.
- Sci-fi reminders: Apple's Foundation series, the Dune remake and the Invasion miniseries.
- SpinRite 6.1: all new code now tested, benchmarks updated to the new I/O abstraction, next test release imminent, then data recovery and logging.
- Fun video: Adrian's Digital Basement running an old SpinRite to optimise sector interleave on an MFM drive emulator (grc.sc/837).
