# Summary

The post-Black Hat and DEF CON episode. Steve covers Kryptowire's finding of 47 firmware and preinstalled-app vulnerabilities in 25 Android phone models from many vendors (Google not among them), and argues that people who might be targeted are safest with a platform parent that patches quickly. Check Point's "man-in-the-disk" research shows Android apps staging updates or data in shared external storage, where another app can alter them; Google fixed its apps but Xiaomi's browser stayed unfixed. An AP investigation showed Google still records location through web and app activity even when location history is off. Brazilian D-Link modems were again hijacked, this time to point DNS at fake bank sites. Christopher Domas revealed a hidden coprocessor mode ("Rosenbridge") in VIA C3 CPUs, Check Point showed WhatsApp group messages can be spoofed by a group member (WhatsApp calls it a known tradeoff), and Patrick Wardle disclosed a macOS High Sierra synthetic-click zero day. Smaller items: Windows 10 Enterprise "InPrivate Desktop" sandbox, Criptext Signal-protocol email, Facebook's open-source Fizz TLS 1.3 library, and Let's Encrypt reaching all major root stores. The headline is Check Point's FaxSploit, which uses a malicious fax JPEG to take over an HP printer and then the network behind it.

# Key points

- Kryptowire (funded by DHS): 47 flaws including command execution as system, reading and sending texts, factory reset; affected Alcatel, Asus, LG, Nokia, Sony, ZTE and smaller brands. Steve and Leo weigh monoculture risk against buying from the platform maker (Pixel, iPhone).
- Man-in-the-disk: apps using world-accessible external storage without validation or signing can be hijacked; any app with storage permission can swap a staged update; Google Translate, Voice Typing and Text-to-Speech were fixed, Xiaomi browser was not.
- AP: Google keeps timestamped location markers from Maps and searches unless Web and App Activity is also disabled.
- D-Link DSL modem DNS hijacking aimed at two Brazilian banks; mitigations include HSTS on the bank side and setting DNS manually on important machines.
- Rosenbridge: a RISC side core in VIA C3 processors, activated by a special instruction, runs code at ring 0 and was enabled by default on some systems.
- WhatsApp: a participant in a group chat can alter quoted text or sender identity in the client-side protocol; Leo links this to disinformation in India.
- macOS: two consecutive synthetic mouse-down events were treated as a real click, letting malware approve security prompts; Mojave blocks synthetic events.
- Windows 10 Enterprise InPrivate Desktop: a disposable VM-like sandbox for untrusted executables, requires virtualization and 4 GB RAM.
- Fizz (Facebook): TLS 1.3 C++ library now securing over half of Facebook's traffic; Let's Encrypt root now in all major root programs, with IdenTrust cross-signing kept for old devices.
- FaxSploit: researchers reverse engineered an HP Officejet (ARM, ThreadX, no ASLR) via JTAG, found buffer overflows in the fax JPEG handling, and chained to EternalBlue and DoublePulsar to spread into the LAN; HP rated it critical (9.8) and shipped firmware before disclosure. Advice: update firmware, unplug the phone line if unused, segment the network. Not yet seen in the wild.
