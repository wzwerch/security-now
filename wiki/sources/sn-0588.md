# Summary

This Q&A episode starts with news. The San Francisco Muni fare system was hit by ransomware whose operators demanded roughly 73,000 dollars in bitcoin, leading to free rides while some two thousand systems were affected. Chrome announced that version 56, due around the end of January 2017, will stop trusting SHA-1 certificates. Steve covers "ImageGate" (Check Point found executables disguised as images spreading Locky through social media), the Speake(a)r research showing that software-configurable audio chips let headphones act as microphones, and an unpatched NTP server crash bug, with Leo adding a plea for funding the under-resourced NTP project. Steve walks through a WordPress update-authentication flaw found by Wordfence: the signature scheme let the caller pick the hash algorithm, and the weak Adler32 checksum shrank the guessing space to a practical size. Listener questions deal with randomised PIN pads, DNS providers such as OpenDNS, disk imaging software, a router that forces a strong password, and a student who saw telnet login attempts within seconds of exposing a router.

# Key points

- SFMTA ransomware: fare gates were opened as the system went down; reports cited about 2,000 infected machines and the attackers said the infection was untargeted. Agency said service was unaffected and gave few details. Steve and Leo assume restoration from backups.
- Chrome 56 removes SHA-1 certificate support; site owners should obtain SHA-256 replacements. Steve reluctantly backs Google's forcing function because change only happens under pressure.
- Deseat.me: scans a Gmail archive via OAuth to list services you signed up to; Steve dislikes granting a third party that access, and Leo notes it also lists spam.
- UK Investigatory Powers Act: a parliamentary petition against it had passed 100,000 signatures; the government response defended the act as increasing transparency.
- ImageGate: Check Point reported malicious executables uploaded to social sites as image files, used to spread Locky; Facebook and LinkedIn were notified and fixed it before full disclosure.
- Speake(a)r: because audio codec pins on common chips are software-defined, malware can turn headphone or speaker outputs into microphones even on machines without a mic.
- NTP: versions up to 4.2.8p9 could be crashed by one malformed UDP packet; exposed servers should be patched. The maintainer is effectively a single volunteer and the project is underfunded.
- NHTSA proposed voluntary "driver mode" guidelines to limit phone distraction.
- EFF recommendations for services (allow pseudonymous use, limit behavioural analysis, delete logs, encrypt in transit and end to end) are endorsed by Steve, though he finds the political framing unnecessary.
- WordPress/GitHub update flaw: GitHub notifies api.wordpress.org of new code with a hash of the spec plus a shared secret; since the caller named the hash, Adler32 or CRC32 could be requested, reducing an online brute force to roughly hundreds of thousands of tries. Wordfence reported it responsibly and WordPress fixed it; Steve notes it could have let someone push malicious updates to about a quarter of websites.
- Supercapacitor phone-charging headlines are premature, since storage physics and device voltage needs have not been solved.
- SpinRite graphics: a periodic pattern of unrecoverable sectors indicates physical surface damage such as a head-crash scratch.
- Q&A: randomising PIN pad layouts also thwarts the WiFi CSI keystroke attack; OpenDNS offers anycast resolvers, filtering, optional DNSCrypt and keeps serving stale records; Image for Windows is Steve's imaging tool of choice; a TP-Link router requiring a strong password at first boot is praised.
- Internet background radiation: a student who exposed a school MikroTik router via DMZ saw telnet login attempts from changing real IP addresses almost immediately.
