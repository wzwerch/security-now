# Summary

The main story is VPNFilter, identified by Cisco Talos on more than 500,000 consumer and small-business routers and some QNAP NAS devices (Linksys, MikroTik, Netgear, TP-Link and others). It has a persistent stage one that survives reboots, a RAM-resident stage two with a flash-wiping capability, and stage three plugins including sniffing for industrial control traffic and Tor communications. The FBI attributed it to the Russian GRU-linked APT28 (Fancy Bear), seized the ToKnowAll domain used for command and control, and asked owners to reboot; Steve explains that a reboot helps the FBI see the infected IPs but that a factory reset and firmware reflash are the real cleanup. Before that: Oracle will phase out Java serialization, Ghostery leaked 500-address batches of its users in a GDPR notice, the BackSwap banking trojan drives the browser through simulated keystrokes, an Echo "recording" incident is explained as a chain of fuzzy matches, Firefox 63 adds granular tracking protection, and a Z-Wave downgrade attack is called overhyped.

# Key points

- Java serialization: Oracle's chief Java architect called it a "horrible mistake"; it is the source of a third to a half of Java's historical security problems and will be replaced by a pluggable framework (JSON or XML for specific needs).
- Ghostery's privacy-policy email put hundreds of subscribers in the visible To field; the company apologized on Twitter.
- BackSwap (ESET): a banking trojan hidden in trojanized utilities that avoids browser injection by hooking Windows accessibility, detecting banking URLs, then typing JavaScript into the URL bar or developer console as if the user did; defeats AV and browser protections because pasted "javascript:" URLs are blocked but typed ones are not. Steve suggests a dedicated Debian or Chromebook machine for banking.
- Echo "fuzzy match": a family's conversation was sent to a contact after the device misheard a wake word, then a "send message" request, a contact name and a confirmation. Amazon called it extremely unlikely; Leo thinks it should ask for explicit confirmation. Steve frames it as a convenience-versus-security trade-off, not hacking.
- Firefox 63 plans: granular blocking for analytics, advertising, fingerprinting, cryptomining and social trackers with per-site exceptions, and TOTP support for Mozilla accounts.
- Columbia researchers hid data in tiny distortions of font glyph shapes; Steve is unmoved by steganography in general.
- An npm registry problem for users behind a proxy added a :443 port to hostnames and returned HTTP 418 "I'm a teapot", the April-fools coffee-pot RFC status code.
- Z-Shave (Pen Test Partners): Z-Wave S0 pairing uses an all-zero key and S2 devices can be forced to downgrade to S0 by active interference during pairing; Steve calls the 100-million-device headlines hype because the attacker must be nearby and actively jamming at pairing time; S2 uses Diffie-Hellman and is strong.
- Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 briefly hijacked via a BGP route announced by a Chinese ISP (Leo's aside).
- VPNFilter details: affected models include Linksys E1200/E2500/WRVS4400N, MikroTik 1016/1036/1072, several Netgear (DGN2200, R6400, R7000, R8000, WNR1000, WNR2000), TP-Link R600VPN and QNAP NAS; stage one fetches a pointer from photo EXIF GPS data on a photo site (fallback domain ToKnowAll), then downloads stage two; stage two can brick routers by wiping flash; no zero-days used according to Cisco; targeting was believed to be Ukraine ahead of the Champions League final; the FBI got VeriSign to hand over the domain. Advice: reboot, factory reset, update/reflash firmware, disable WAN management and UPnP, use strong non-default credentials.
- Hide and Seek earlier in May and VPNFilter are the two known persistent router botnets.
