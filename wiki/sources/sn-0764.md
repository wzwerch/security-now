# Summary

Steve revisits the Apple/Google contact tracing project, now renamed exposure notification, and walks through eight changes (random daily keys instead of derived keys, encrypted Bluetooth metadata, five-minute exposure intervals capped at 30 minutes, transmit power in beacons, configurable thresholds and AES instead of HMAC), while criticizing inaccurate press claims such as IP-based identification and broadcasting of infection status. He judges the ZecOps disclosure of two iOS Mail crash bugs overhyped, since no exploitation was demonstrated and Apple found no evidence of use. Other items: the choice of a VPN provider in light of the 14 Eyes surveillance pact, a typosquatting campaign with more than 725 malicious RubyGems that swapped cryptocurrency addresses, Vitamin D and COVID-19 research, a VirtualBox extension licensing warning, and SpinRite test tools. The main topic is RPKI: how signed Route Origin Authorizations from the five regional registries can complement BGP, and Cloudflare's "Is BGP Safe Yet?" test site.

# Key points

- Exposure notification updates: terminology change; daily keys now random; Bluetooth metadata encrypted; exposure reported in 5-minute steps up to 30 minutes; transmit power included to improve distance estimates; developers can set signal strength and duration thresholds; API reports days since exposure; AES replaces HMAC. Steve finds some stated reasons weak but the design sound, and wishes the vendors shipped their own apps since third-party apps could embed identity.
- Wired and Brookings claims about re-identifying positive users via phone IPs or Bluetooth cameras are, in Steve's view, wrong (cell IPs are ephemeral; no infection status is broadcast).
- ZecOps iOS Mail: two memory bugs (out-of-bounds write, heap overflow) in MIME handling, disclosed before Apple's patch with claims of in-the-wild use; Apple said it found no evidence and that the bugs alone cannot bypass protections; Steve notes repeated "41" bytes are just Base64 of zeroes. The fix was in iOS betas ahead of a release.
- 14 Eyes: the intelligence-sharing alliance grew from the UK/US pact to Five Eyes and then 14 members; VPN users should prefer audited no-log providers based outside member countries (TechRadar names ExpressVPN in the British Virgin Islands and NordVPN in Panama). A VPN is client-to-server encrypted, unlike end-to-end services such as Sync.com.
- RubyGems typosquatting: Reversing Labs found more than 725 malicious gems with names one character off from real ones (for example hyphen versus underscore) that ran a Windows clipboard-hijacking cryptocurrency stealer with registry persistence.
- Vitamin D: Trinity College Dublin's TILDA study suggests it may affect COVID-19 severity (immune and anti-inflammatory role); Steve repeats his advice.
- VirtualBox: a listener warns the closed-source Extension Pack is free only for personal use and Oracle audits can lead to large settlements.
- SpinRite: a boot-media preparation app, a RAM enumeration utility and plans for a custom himem replacement; first AHCI test tool (SpinTest) soon.
- RPKI: BGP lets any network announce any prefix, so hijacks and leaks (TTNet 2004, Pakistan/YouTube 2008, 2018 Amazon DNS hijack that stole cryptocurrency) occur. RFC 6480 defines a certificate hierarchy rooted in the five regional registries (AFRINIC, APNIC, ARIN, LACNIC, RIPE) that issue signed Route Origin Authorizations tying prefixes to autonomous system numbers, enabling filtering without changing BGP. Large networks adopting it protect much of the internet; Cloudflare's isbgpsafeyet.com site tests whether your ISP filters invalid routes (Steve's ISP failed). Job Snijders of NTT presents a free RPKI 101 webinar.
