# Summary

The first episode of 2017 begins with an Arkansas murder case in which police sought data from the suspect's Amazon Echo and smart water meter, raising questions about the reliability and privacy of home IoT data. Steve then warns about the PHPMailer library, used by roughly nine million sites and applications such as WordPress, Drupal and Joomla: the first fix for a remote-code-execution flaw was incomplete, details became public, and a second patch was still pending. Other items: Microsoft's marketing chief admitting the Windows 10 upgrade pop-up went too far, a six-year-old using her mother's thumb for shopping, Apple postponing mandatory App Transport Security, and Steve's deflation of headlines about Russian malware found on a Vermont utility's laptop. He explains why very long random passwords are safe even against professional cracking rigs, reports an unpatched remote flaw in Netgear WNR2000 routers whose vendor never answered, notes that TLS 1.3 is finalised, and recommends the EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense site. The main topic is how Google's checkbox "I'm not a robot" test decides: it relies on the user's cookies, IP history, device and mouse behaviour rather than a puzzle.

# Key points

- Echo and smart meter in a murder case: Amazon declined to hand over Echo audio absent a valid binding demand but gave purchase records; a water meter showed heavy usage during the night, which prosecutors read as washing evidence. Steve notes the Echo only streams after the wake word, clips are stored until deleted, and IoT data could be spoofed, complicating evidence.
- PHPMailer: a Polish researcher found a remote code execution flaw; the vendor's fix escaped one function but interacted badly with another escaping step, so versions through 5.2.19 stayed vulnerable and public disclosure followed. Steve says the real fix needs a later release and calls the patch a blacklisting approach rather than whitelisting.
- Microsoft's chief marketing officer told a Windows podcast that the red-X dismissal behaviour in the Windows 10 upgrade prompt went too far and cost two painful weeks. The Never10 tool counted over two million downloads.
- A six-year-old used her sleeping mother's fingerprint to buy about 250 dollars of toys, a lesson about convenience versus security.
- Apple extended its deadline for App Transport Security (HTTPS-only app networking), giving no new date; Steve cites it as proof that forcing change is hard.
- Vermont utility: malware linked by US agencies to a Russian campaign was found on a single laptop not connected to grid operations; officials' rhetoric was overheated. Steve hopes the attention turns into funding for a grid that has been known to be vulnerable for a decade. Next week's topic is the technical analysis of the Russian PHP malware sample.
- Password length: Jeremi Gosney, a leading password cracker, remarked that randomly generated 20-character-plus passwords from managers are fine; Steve says 16 truly random characters over a 95-symbol alphabet resist even an eight-GPU rig, especially if the site applies a slow hash such as PBKDF2.
- Netgear WNR2000 v5: a researcher found unauthenticated actions and a stack buffer overflow reachable if remote administration is enabled; scans found about 10,000 exposed routers and Netgear did not respond to disclosure, so he published. Steve is torn about disclosure when a vendor ignores reports and expects legislation eventually.
- TLS 1.3 design finalised: fewer round trips (zero for repeat connections), removal of legacy features and algorithms, and formal model checking before standardisation; supported in Firefox, Chrome and Opera.
- EFF Surveillance Self-Defense (ssd.eff.org) offers overviews, tutorials and briefings on threat modelling, passwords, encryption tools, phishing, borders and protests.
- Google's no-captcha reCAPTCHA: uses the Google cookies tied to the request, IP and browsing history, mouse movement patterns and the ability to render canvas content; incognito mode lacks the cookies so a harder challenge appears. It cannot be solved by cheap human labour farms the way image puzzles can.
- SpinRite can detect cabling errors by tracking the drive's interface CRC error counter in SMART data, revealing flaky cables.
