# Summary

Steve begins with Apple's proposal in the CA/Browser Forum to shrink the maximum TLS certificate lifetime from 398 days to 200, 100 and finally 45 days (September 2025 to April 2027), which he opposes because it forces automation on devices such as his own mail server and removes owners' choice, while revocation never worked anyway. The SEC fined Unisys, Avaya, Check Point and Mimecast a few million dollars in total for downplaying SolarWinds-related compromises, which Steve finds too small to deter. Google Messages gains five protections including on-device Sensitive Content Warnings, which leads Steve to argue that local AI filtering could end the encryption backdoor dispute. He highlights the Session messenger, which combines the Signal protocol with Tor-style onion routing and moved from Australia to Switzerland after police questioned an employee. He adds more reaction to the EU liability directive, a North Korean worker's vulnerable code at the Cosmos blockchain, and listener feedback. The main topic is a APNIC chief scientist's analysis of why IPv6 adoption is slow: client NAT and server name indication let IPv4 scale, DNS names rather than addresses now steer services, and US adoption has been flat since 2019.

# Key points

- Apple's 45-day certificate lifetime proposal (398 to 200 days in September 2025, 100 in September 2026, 45 in April 2027) assumes ACME automation; Steve objects that email servers and appliances are hard to automate, that Let's Encrypt-style domain validation would become the norm and erode CA business, and that owners should choose their own lifetimes. Revocation lists via Bloom filters shrink if certificates are short-lived, which is the motive.
- SEC settlements over misleading SolarWinds Orion breach disclosures: Unisys $4 million, Avaya $1 million, Check Point $995,000, Mimecast $990,000, no admission of wrongdoing; examples include Unisys's 33 GB exfiltration and Avaya's knowledge of compromised cloud systems by APT29. Steve doubts the amounts will teach any lesson.
- Google Messages: scam detection for package and job messages, warnings on dangerous links, controls for unknown international senders, Sensitive Content Warnings (blur nudity, opt-in for adults, default for under 18, processed on device), and key verification for contacts.
- Steve's thesis: on-device AI classification (as Apple did in iOS 15) can address child-safety and possibly other content concerns without breaking end-to-end encryption, though it risks slippery-slope expansion.
- Session (GetSession.org): open-source messenger using the Signal protocol fork, no phone number or email, key-pair identity, onion routing over thousands of incentivized nodes, minimal metadata ("Send messages, not metadata"); the foundation moved to Switzerland after Australian federal police visited an employee; the product dropped perfect forward secrecy and deniability, with a listener noting trade-offs.
- Further coverage of the EU software liability directive and the US Cyberspace Solarium Commission's unadopted liability recommendation; industry warns liability could harm innovation.
- Cosmos blockchain creator admitted it hired a North Korean IT worker whose code contained a major vulnerability; an employee failed to escalate an FBI notice; code audit under way. Steve expects live video verification to become standard.
- SpinRite listener: reinstalling Windows rewrites the SSD and gives the same speedup, so SpinRite is only needed to refresh data in place.
- Listener tip: have ChatGPT review and suggest improvements to code after writing it yourself.
- IPv6 (Geoff Huston, APNIC Labs): IPv6 specified in 1998, IPv4 pool exhausted over a decade ago, only about one third of users can reach IPv6-only services, and a linear extrapolation reaches 100% around 2045; only 47% of the top 1,000 sites are IPv6-reachable; US adoption has been flat since 2019.
- Why: IPv6 offered no better speed, features or security, only bigger addresses; CIDR, regional registries and NAT (clients) plus TLS Server Name Indication (servers share one IP among many names) relieved pressure; about 20 billion devices share 3.03 billion routed IPv4 addresses (average seven devices per address); DNS names have become the routing-like service identifier and TLS authenticates them. Steve predicts IPv4 never dies and carrier-grade NAT will fill gaps.
