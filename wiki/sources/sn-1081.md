# Summary

Steve begins by confirming that last week's warnings came true: attackers immediately exploited the UniFi OS flaws (adding a "John Sim" super admin account and triggering backups) and CISA gave federal agencies one day to fix the Drupal PostgreSQL SQL injection. He restates his case for automatic updates as a fuse-like safeguard. Dutch authorities dismantled the Asocks residential-proxy botnet of 17 million-plus compromised consumer devices by seizing 200 command servers, though Steve expects it to return via algorithmic DNS rendezvous. Microsoft Defender for Endpoint is gaining automatic device isolation (in preview). The ShinyHunters group, after a vishing attack on a Microsoft Entra account, leaked Charter Communications data affecting 4.9 million email addresses. Chrome moved device-bound session cookies to general availability. Anthropic said Mythos-class models will reach all customers in coming weeks. Daniel Stenberg reported curl already at a record 11 CVEs mid-release cycle from AI-driven reports, none using Mythos, and IBM with Red Hat announced the $5 billion Project Lightwell to help fix open source with AI. A long listener-feedback stretch covers the AI-overload complaint, falling demand for bug bounties and Pwn2Own, AI certification driven by insurers, practical Claude migration anecdotes, privacy of AI memory, and a wish for local home AI boxes. The closing feature is Kabir Acharya's essay "The CTF scene is dead", explaining how frontier models made open Capture the Flag competitions meaningless as skill measures.

# Key points

- UniFi OS exploitation began within days of Ubiquiti's patch: Reddit users found an unfamiliar super admin named "John Sim" and unexplained backups; about 100,000 devices are exposed (half in the US). Steve counters "bricking" fears: routers can revert to factory firmware, so auto-update risk is a nuisance outage while delay invites compromise. He wishes vendors would auto-update customers before disclosing.
- Drupal CVE-2026-9082 (PostgreSQL SQL injection found by Google/Mandiant) was added to CISA's KEV catalog with a BOD 22-01 one-day deadline; Imperva saw 15,000+ attempts against ~6,000 sites in 65 countries; Shadowserver tracked ~670 unpatched instances.
- Steve hopes the "all software has bugs" cliche can be refuted; software became too complex to understand, but LLMs may be able to understand it; Palo Alto Networks reportedly spent about $10 million in Mythos tokens. Microsoft's blog hinted at suing Nightmare-Eclipse; Steve says the bugs are Microsoft's own, and the researcher has teased a July 14 surprise.
- Asocks botnet: Dutch police and the NCSC took down 200 servers managing at least 17 million devices (computers, routers, phones, cameras) used as residential proxies; advice is change default passwords, use WPA2/WPA3 and update. Steve explains bots can rejoin via algorithmically generated DNS names (like the DNS Benchmark's random-looking queries).
- Microsoft Defender for Endpoint "automatic device isolation" / attack disruption: compromised devices lose network access except to the Defender service; time-limited and admin-releasable.
- Charter Communications: ShinyHunters claimed 42 million Salesforce records via a vishing-compromised Entra account in April; Have I Been Pwned counted 4.9 million unique accounts (names, emails, phones, addresses; 85,000 employee directory records with job titles). Charter denies CPNI exposure. GRC was listed only because its domain appears in spoofed addresses. Steve argues extortion gangs rarely resell data after payment because their leverage depends on keeping promises, though the FBI advises not paying.
- Device-bound session cookies (DBSC): Chrome's general availability ties session cookies to the device TPM or secure enclave so stolen cookies cannot be replayed; needs server-side support, so adoption will be slow.
- Anthropic (Opus 4.8 announcement, 28 May): plans a new higher-intelligence model class; "Mythos-class" models to be released broadly in coming weeks once cyber safeguards are ready.
- curl: 11 confirmed CVEs mid-cycle with new reports arriving daily, from AI tools generally, not Mythos. Steve notes vulnerabilities are finite and will be exhausted.
- IBM and Red Hat Project Lightwell: $5 billion and 20,000+ engineers using AI to fix open source vulnerabilities, starting with the Maven/Java ecosystem, then PyPI, npm and Go. Anthropic's Glasswing offers $100M credits and $4M funding for open source security.
- Listener ideas: bug bounties and Pwn2Own will fade as AI discovery becomes in-house; third-party AI vulnerability certification may be required by insurers, banks and card networks (like UL listing); Steve agrees and also agrees about the AI-cost curve collapsing (storage analogy).
- Practical uses: Claude translating a Fortinet configuration into a UniFi gateway plan and writing a script to migrate an EdgeRouter configuration; a lawyer-written trust PDF explained by ChatGPT; Leo's local agents "Quicksilver" and "Rosie" with Tailscale access.
- Privacy debate: cloud AI memory digests accumulate deeply personal data and are non-portable (lock-in); Venice.ai stores context in the local browser; Leo claims frontier providers keep little, Steve disagrees. Steve imagines a home "AI box" appliance with hybrid cloud fallback and "Terameters" of model size.
- CTFs: Kabir Acharya's post says GPT-4 made medium challenges one-shot, Claude Opus 4.5 and agent orchestration solved most medium and some hard ones, and GPT-5.5 can solve many insane-level pwn challenges, making open CTFs pay-to-win; CTFtime rankings, Plaid CTF and recruiting by CTF performance are collapsing; advice is to use learning platforms (picoGym, HackTheBox) and community meetups. Steve concludes Pwn2Own, bug bounties and Zerodium-style exploit brokers will die as software becomes secure.
