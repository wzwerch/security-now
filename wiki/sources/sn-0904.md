# Summary

The whole episode is devoted to LastPass after its 22 December 2022 follow-up disclosure. LastPass confirmed that an attacker, using information and credentials derived from the August 2022 source-code theft (a second employee was targeted), obtained cloud storage access and decryption keys and copied backups containing basic customer account metadata and backups of customer vaults, which hold some unencrypted fields (such as website URLs) alongside encrypted usernames, passwords, notes and form data. Steve criticises inflammatory commentary, such as password-cracking expert Jeremi Gosney's claim that nearly everything in a vault is unencrypted, but accepts several substantive findings by Gosney and by Wladimir Palant. He explains how password-based key derivation works and the history of LastPass's PBKDF2 iteration counts (1 in 2008, 500 in 2012, 5,000 in 2013, 100,100 in 2018), and learns from listeners that many accounts were never migrated from 5,000 iterations and that older eight-character master passwords were never forced to be strengthened; there are also claims that older entries remained in ECB mode rather than CBC. His conclusion is that LastPass cared too little about hardening legacy accounts, so he left for Bitwarden (open source, useful free tier, audited), importing a CSV export without trouble, while noting 1Password and Dashlane as reasonable alternatives. He advises: check your iteration count, use a long high-entropy master password, enable second factors, change important site passwords not protected by a second factor, and watch for targeted phishing based on the exposed URL and email metadata.

# Key points

- LastPass disclosure (22 Dec 2022): stolen backups include company names, end-user names, billing addresses, emails, phone numbers, IP addresses and customer vault backups; whether all customers are affected was not stated, but Steve reads it as everyone.
- Vault format: URLs and some metadata are only obfuscated (base64 and hex), not encrypted; the sensitive fields are encrypted client side. Steve asked listeners to write local tools to decode the unencrypted portion.
- Stolen vaults are protected only by each user's master password and key derivation work at the time of the backup; changing the master password now does not protect already stolen data.
- PBKDF2 iteration history at LastPass: 1 (2008), 500 (June 2012), 5,000 (Feb 2013), 100,100 (Feb 2018); reports and many listener confirmations show some accounts remained at 5,000.
- The 2018 master-password complexity rules were not enforced on existing accounts, so some users may still have only eight characters.
- Allegation that old entries remained in ECB mode (leaks duplicate passwords) rather than CBC; Steve found none in his own vault but treats it as unverified.
- Gosney also criticised memory handling (decrypted vault and key kept in memory) and 128 bits of entropy behind the AES-256 key; Steve calls some of this a necessary convenience trade-off but concedes LastPass design has aged badly.
- Steve's estimate: a 50-bit password with 100,100 iterations would cost around 1.5 million dollars and 200 years of single-GPU time; an average 40-bit password roughly a thousand times less; more iterations scale cracking cost linearly.
- Second-factor authentication does not strengthen the master-password-derived encryption key, so it does not help against offline cracking of a stolen vault.
- Alternatives praised by experts: 1Password (secret key makes stolen vaults uncrackable without it), Bitwarden (open source, free tier across devices, self-hosting option, annual third-party audits, bug bounty, default 100,001 client iterations plus 100,000 server side; Steve suggests about 350,000), and Dashlane (Argon2).
- Remediation advice: confirm iteration count is at least 100,100, use a long master password (Password Haystacks idea), keep time-based authenticators, rotate passwords on important accounts lacking strong second factors, expect phishing using leaked URLs.
- The LastPass vendor was meanwhile spun off from LogMeIn into an independent company owned by private equity.
