# Summary

This episode replaces the planned Q&A with the Logjam research. Before that, Steve reviews the draft subscriber agreement for Let's Encrypt, a router-USB (NetUSB) kernel driver flaw affecting many consumer routers, a joint letter from technology companies and cryptographers to the White House opposing encryption backdoors and the FBI director's dismissive reply, and the continuing debate over the airplane-hacking claim. Follow-ups on keyless entry clarify that thieves amplify the car's signal near the car, with the owner's fob possibly inside the house, and that signal-blocking pouches or even a closed metal tin may help. The Logjam explanation covers how a man in the middle can trick a server into using export-grade 512-bit Diffie-Hellman parameters even though the client never offered them, because the server's signed parameters do not bind the cipher suite. Because many servers reuse the same prime (Apache and mod_ssl defaults), expensive precomputation against that single prime makes breaking individual sessions feasible in minutes, and researchers suggested that the same method at 1024 bits could be within a nation-state's means, consistent with leaked NSA capabilities against VPNs. Fixes are for clients to reject small primes, for servers to remove export cipher suites, and for each server to generate its own prime.

# Key points

- Let's Encrypt draft subscriber agreement: six pages, readable; includes warranties of domain control, a clause about seized domains, and a prohibition on safety-critical use. The system targets domain-validated certificates through the ACME protocol and also supports automated revocation. TWiT's new site launches fully on HTTPS with a wildcard DigiCert certificate.
- NetUSB: a KCodes USB-over-IP kernel driver present in many router firmwares (D-Link, Netgear, TP-Link, ZyXEL, TRENDnet and others) has a buffer overflow reachable on the local network and, on some routers, from the Internet on TCP port 20005; the mere presence of a USB port can imply the driver. Steve suggests checking exposure with GRC's port probe, updating firmware, or replacing old routers; alternative firmware like Tomato or DD-WRT is likely unaffected.
- A letter signed by roughly 150 companies and experts (including cryptography pioneers) told the President that lawful-access backdoors cannot be built without weakening security; FBI Director Comey said the signers were not fair-minded and asked why it is too hard. Steve and a Techdirt commentary point out it is impossible rather than hard.
- The Patriot Act's bulk collection provision was about to expire, with Congress short on time; the government had begun winding down the program.
- Chris Roberts: Steve now sees credible people on both sides about whether in-flight entertainment is isolated from avionics; he reminds listeners that car entertainment systems have been attack paths.
- ScriptBlock is a Chrome extension comparable to NoScript; Steve notes about 80 percent of third-party content on news sites is tracking or analytics scripts.
- Keyless entry follow-up: relay amplifiers work at the car, so the fob can sit inside the home; test any pouch by walking up to the car; some cars disable passive entry when a second fob is locked in the glove box or have a menu setting to turn it off; rolling codes do not help against range extension.
- Leo reports removing the fob battery made his car impractical to start.
- Logjam background: 1990s export rules weakened ciphers and Diffie-Hellman to 512-bit primes. Clients do not offer export suites but about 8.4 percent of the top million HTTPS sites still accepted them. A man in the middle edits the client's cipher list and the server's response, and the server's signed key-exchange message does not name the cipher suite, so the client accepts the small prime.
- The final Finished-message check does not rescue the client because the attacker, who has solved the weak discrete logarithm quickly, can compute valid authentication values.
- Precomputation: the number field sieve for discrete logs has stages depending only on the prime. Since about 92 percent of servers supporting export Diffie-Hellman used one of two common primes (the Apache default accounts for most), one precomputation lets attackers break connections to many servers in minutes.
- Researchers estimated that academic teams could break 768-bit primes and that a state could break 1024-bit primes; 66 percent of VPN servers and 26 percent of SSH servers surveyed used a common 1024-bit prime, and they note that leaked NSA material on VPN decryption is consistent with this.
- This applies to classic finite-field Diffie-Hellman, not elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman; the paper notes fresh RSA keys can be harder than precomputed discrete logs, and urges implementers and cryptographers to communicate better.
- Fixes: browsers refuse small primes (the weakdh.org site tests a browser; Firefox and IE were fixed first, Chrome and Safari showed warnings); servers should remove export suites (Steve's published cipher order did this) and generate their own Diffie-Hellman prime. TLS 1.3 is expected to bind the cipher suite into the signed parameters.
