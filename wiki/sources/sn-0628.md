# Summary

The episode opens with a remembrance of author and early PC columnist Jerry Pournelle, who died the previous Friday at 84. Steve then covers an experiment in which an AI named SNAP_R sent automated spear-phishing tweets and lured roughly the same fraction of users as a human journalist, just at a far higher rate; he stresses the conversion rates were statistically indistinguishable. He explains that the announced Chrome feature to warn about man-in-the-middle software is really about badly built interception middleboxes, not a defence against snooping. Google is accused of trying to patent public-domain ANS entropy-coding technology. A D-Link consumer router is described as having ten zero-days. Google's timeline for distrusting Symantec certificates is laid out, with a warning for IoT devices that pin a single Symantec root, and Comodo is caught issuing a certificate despite a CAA record. After a SpinRite explainer and a Tarsnap recommendation, the main segment dissects the Equifax breach: about 143 million US consumers exposed, detection only in late July after a mid-May intrusion, a five-week delay in disclosure, an Apache Struts connection, and credit-freeze PINs that were simply timestamps. Steve's advice is to freeze credit at the bureaus, keep frameworks patched, and monitor networks.

# Key points

- Jerry Pournelle (Byte magazine, Chaos Manor, "The Mote in God's Eye" with Larry Niven) died at age 84.
- AI phishing experiment (SNAP_R): 6.75 tweets/min against a human's 1.075; conversion 38% vs 34.4%, which Steve calls not significant; the worry is zero-effort scalability.
- Chrome 63 will warn when an interception middlebox produces too many errors; it will not stop legitimate corporate TLS interception, and Chrome pins Google certificates which complicates interception.
- Google's patent filing on ANS compression (Jarek Duda's public-domain work, used in Apple LZFSE, Zstandard, Draco) is contested; Google reportedly may rework it.
- D-Link 850L: ten zero-days including hard-coded keys and passwords, unencrypted mydlink cloud relay, cleartext stored credentials, DNS config changes without authentication, root-level remote code execution in the DHCP daemon; advice is to replace it (e.g. with an EdgeRouter X or pfSense).
- Symantec CA sunset: Chrome 66 distrusts Symantec certificates issued before 1 June 2016; DigiCert takes over issuance by 1 December 2017; Chrome 70 (Oct 2018) removes the old roots entirely. IoT devices that trust only a Symantec root may stop working.
- CAA records became mandatory for CAs to check on 8 Sept 2017; a German researcher showed Comodo issued a certificate anyway.
- A Volkswagen engineer received 40 months in prison and a $200,000 fine for the emissions defeat device.
- iOS 11 removes app access to the ARP table, further limiting MAC address leakage.
- Tarsnap (Colin Percival) recommended as a cloud backup replacement for CrashPlan on Unix-like systems; scrypt from the same author underlies SQRL's EnScrypt.
- Equifax: 143M US consumers (about 44% of the population) lost names, SSNs, birth dates, addresses, some driver's licence numbers; about 209,000 credit card numbers; also some UK and Canadian residents.
- Breach timeline: intrusion around mid-May, detected 29 July, announced about five weeks later; executives' stock sales were said to be unrelated.
- Apache Struts is implicated but the specific flaw was unknown at the time; a Struts flaw fixed on 4 Sept was a candidate; Apache's advice is to patch, use defence in depth and monitor.
- Equifax credit-freeze PINs were a timestamp (month-day-year-hour-minute), i.e. no entropy; Equifax promised random PINs.
- Class action seeking up to $70 billion; Steve recommends credit freezes (bit.ly/freezecredit from episode 495), noting cost varies by state and the bureau sites were overloaded.
- Leo adds that Equifax's breach-check site pushed TrustedID with an arbitration clause, and discusses LifeLock and the fourth bureau Innovis.

# Other segments

- SpinRite explainer: its history from non-destructive interleave optimisation to data recovery, and the 6.x plan to remove BIOS dependence as groundwork for SpinRite 7; free for existing 6.x owners with pre-release for listeners.
- SQRL is described as nearly finished.
